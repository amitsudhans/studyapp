Longitudinal Waves

Section 10.3: Slinky Analogy, Compressions & Rarefactions

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

How does sound actually move through a medium? In Section 10.3, Activity 10.5 uses a flexible slinky spring toy to demonstrate wave mechanics. Hold one end of the slinky steady on the floor and give the other end a sharp forward push and pull: you see a localized cluster of tight, compressed coils race forward, followed immediately by spread-out coils! Notice carefully: individual wire coils merely oscillate forward and backward about their fixed equilibrium positions—no single piece of wire travels from one end of the room to the other! Now examine the Piston-Tube Model of air: when an oscillating piston moves forward, it crowds air particles together, creating a high-density, high-pressure region called a Compression, labeled C. When the piston pulls backward, it creates a low-density, low-pressure region called a Rarefaction, labeled R! Because particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation, sound is classified as a Longitudinal Wave, in contrast to transverse waves like light where oscillations are perpendicular! Remember this distinction: sound in air is strictly longitudinal, carrying energy through alternating high-density compressions and low-density rarefactions that push and pull neighboring air molecules in rhythmic sequence along the exact line of wave travel.

A vital principle of wave physics is that sound transfers energy across space without transferring physical matter! In Activity 10.6, stretch a thin rubber balloon membrane tightly across the mouth of a tin container and sprinkle dry grains of uncooked rice or semolina on top. Bring a metal tray close to the membrane and strike it sharply with a spoon: immediately, the rice grains dance and jump wildly! What caused them to jump? In Bridging Science and Society, look at how modern audio transducers harness this energy transformation. A microphone uses a flexible, ultra-thin diaphragm: incoming sound pressure waves push the diaphragm to and fro, converting mechanical acoustic energy into electrical voltage signals. A loudspeaker reverses this exact sequence: fluctuating audio electric currents drive an electromagnet, vibrating a conical paper or plastic speaker cone back and forth to recreate acoustic pressure waves that travel through the air into our ears! This complete acoustic-electrical-acoustic loop underpins every telephone handset, television broadcast, and public address sound system in our modern world, transforming human communication across every continent and community in profound ways.

Wave Graphical Model

Section 10.5: Density-Distance Graphs, Crests & Troughs

Slide 7 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Because longitudinal sound waves consist of invisible density fluctuations, physicists represent them using Density-Distance graphs in Section 10.5! On this graph, the horizontal x-axis represents distance from the sound source, while the vertical y-axis represents the local density or pressure of the medium. The central dashed horizontal line represents the unperturbed average density of the medium at rest. As sound propagates, the curve oscillates above and below this baseline! The highest point on the curve—where density and pressure reach their maximum positive deviation above average—is called a Crest, representing a Compression! The lowest point on the curve—where density and pressure fall to their minimum below average—is called a Trough, representing a Rarefaction! Plotting sound waves this way provides an extraordinarily intuitive mathematical model: even though individual air molecules vibrate longitudinally along the line of travel, the graph clearly displays the wave's spatial periodicity, wavelength, and peak density amplitude at a single glance! By translating longitudinal air pulses into familiar sinusoidal crests and troughs, we can apply universal mathematical equations to calculate wavelength, frequency, and speed effortlessly, unlocking a precise mathematical understanding of every sound wave that travels through our physical world and natural environment with total confidence.

Wavelength, Frequency, Time

Section 10.6.1 & Example 10.1: λ, ν = 1 / T & Musical Sa Re Ga

Slide 8 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now let us master the core mathematical quantities defining every sound wave in Section 10.6.1! First is Wavelength, symbolized by the Greek letter lambda: the distance between two consecutive compressions or two consecutive rarefactions. Its SI unit is the meter! Second is Frequency, symbolized by the Greek letter nu: the number of complete oscillations per unit time passing a given point. Its SI unit is the hertz, abbreviated Hz, named after Heinrich Hertz, where one hertz equals one oscillation per second! Third is Time Period, capitalized T: the time required for one complete oscillation. Frequency and time period are exact mathematical reciprocals: frequency nu equals one divided by T, and T equals one divided by nu! In Activity 10.7, students use audio apps like Phyphox to analyze frequencies of Indian vocal notes—Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni—observing how frequency ascends up the musical scale! Look at Example 10.1: if a source completes ten oscillations in two seconds, frequency nu is ten divided by two, which equals five hertz, and time period T is two divided by ten, which equals zero point two seconds! Always remember that frequency tells us how fast a source oscillates, while time period gives the duration of each single cycle.

Amplitude & Intensity

Section 10.6.2: Peak Displacement, Energy & Inverse Square Law

Slide 9 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

What makes a whisper different from a roar? Section 10.6.2 explores Amplitude and Intensity! Amplitude is the maximum magnitude of displacement or density change of the medium particles from their average equilibrium position. When you strike a tabla gently, its skin vibrates with small amplitude, producing a quiet, gentle sound. Strike the tabla vigorously with your hand: the skin deflects deeply with large amplitude, transferring much greater kinetic energy to the air and producing a loud booming sound! The energy carried by a wave is directly proportional to the square of its amplitude! Now define Intensity: the amount of sound energy passing through a unit area perpendicular to the direction of propagation per unit time, measured in watts per square meter. Notice why sound fades as you walk farther from a speaker: as sound radiates outward in three-dimensional concentric spheres, the total acoustic energy spreads over a sphere surface area of four pi r squared! Consequently, intensity drops with the square of the distance, explaining why whispers are audible only nearby while megaphones focus energy along a beam! Remember: larger amplitude means greater energy, which our auditory perception interprets as louder sound across every auditory frequency we experience.

Speed of Sound

Section 10.6.3 & Examples 10.2–10.4: v = λν & Lightning Delay

Slide 10 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

How fast does sound travel, and what equation connects its fundamental wave characteristics? Section 10.6.3 gives the universal Wave Equation: Speed v equals Wavelength lambda multiplied by Frequency nu! Because frequency nu is one divided by time period T, speed also equals lambda divided by T! The speed of sound depends strictly on the nature, temperature, and density of the medium. Sound travels slowest in gases (approximately 344 meters per second in air at 22 degrees Celsius), faster in liquids (about 1530 meters per second in water), and fastest in rigid solids (around 5000 meters per second in steel)! Look at our worked examples: in Example 10.2, for the human hearing range, a 20 hertz sound has a massive wavelength of 17.2 meters, while a 20 kilohertz ultrasonic wave has a tiny wavelength of 1.72 centimeters! In Example 10.3, seeing lightning flash and hearing thunder 5 seconds later locates the lightning strike distance: multiply 340 meters per second by 5 seconds to get 1700 meters, or 1.7 kilometers away! Notice that light travels at three hundred million meters per second, so you see the flash instantly, while acoustic sound follows far behind at its characteristic medium speed.

How do human senses decode acoustic waves? Section 10.6.4 links physical wave properties to sensory perception! Pitch is our subjective perception of frequency: high frequency produces a shrill, sharp high-pitched tone (like a bird chirping or a whistle), while low frequency produces a deep, bass low-pitched tone (like a roaring lion or a bass drum). Normal human hearing spans an audible range from 20 hertz to 20,000 hertz! Sounds below 20 hertz are infrasonic, and sounds above 20 kilohertz are ultrasonic. Loudness is our subjective perception of amplitude, measured on a decibel scale. Timbre or quality is the unique harmonic overtone structure that lets you distinguish a violin from a flute playing the exact same note at the exact same loudness! Our ears capture these waves: the outer ear funnel directs sound into the ear canal, vibrating the eardrum, which moves three tiny bones—the hammer, anvil, and stirrup—amplifying vibrations into the fluid-filled cochlea! Also explore C. V. Raman's pioneering acoustic research on Indian percussion instruments, explaining how the black syaahi patch on tabla heads creates pure musical harmonics! This brilliant fusion of Indian musical heritage and classical wave mechanics proved that traditional instruments follow precise mathematical principles of acoustic harmony.

Reflection & Echoes

Sections 10.7 & 10.7.1: Law of Reflection, 0.1 s Persistence & 17 m Rule

Slide 12 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Just like light bouncing off a silvered mirror, sound waves reflect when striking a solid or liquid boundary! Section 10.7 establishes the Laws of Reflection of Sound: the incident acoustic wave, the reflected wave, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane, and the angle of incidence strictly equals the angle of reflection! When you shout toward a distant tall cliff, building, or hillside in an open valley, you hear your own voice repeated clearly a moment later. This reflected acoustic repetition is called an Echo! Why does an echo require a large distance? Because the human brain retains an auditory sensation for approximately zero point one seconds—known as the persistence of hearing! To hear a distinct, separate echo, the reflected sound must return at least zero point one seconds after the original shout! Taking the speed of sound in air as 340 meters per second, the total round-trip distance must be at least 34 meters—meaning the obstacle must be at least half that, or 17 meters away! In Example 10.5, if an echo returns after 0.5 seconds, the obstacle distance is 340 times 0.5 divided by 2, which equals 85 meters!

Reverberation Control

Section 10.7.2: Persistence in Halls, Drapery & Gol Gumbaz

Slide 13 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

What happens when sound reflects repeatedly inside an enclosed auditorium or concert hall? Section 10.7.2 examines Reverberation! In a large hall, sound emitted from a stage bounces successively off the ceiling, walls, and floor. Because the distances between walls are often less than 17 meters, multiple reflected sound waves arrive at intervals under zero point zero five seconds, blending together into a prolonged, confused drone! This persistence of sound resulting from multiple repeated reflections is called Reverberation. If reverberation is too long, spoken words blur together and become completely unintelligible! To manage reverberation, acoustic architects install sound-absorbing porous wall panels made of compressed fiberboard, hang heavy pleated fabric draperies, lay thick carpets, and use upholstered auditorium seats that absorb acoustic reflections. In architectural history, marvel at the Whispering Gallery inside the magnificent dome of Gol Gumbaz in Vijayapura, Karnataka, built in 1656: its curved acoustic masonry causes the faintest whisper to reflect and circle the dome clearly over ten times, heard sixty meters across! This historic marvel proves how structural geometry and hard masonry surfaces preserve acoustic energy through seamless multiple reflections, delighting visitors from across the country and the world who test its whispering archways daily.

Section 10.8: Earthquakes, Imaging & Medical Lithotripsy

Beyond the limits of human hearing lies a vast acoustic frontier explored in Section 10.8! First, look at Infrasound—sound waves with frequencies below 20 hertz. Rhinoceroses, elephants, and whales communicate across dozens of kilometers using deep infrasonic rumbles that travel effortlessly through the ground and ocean water! Infrasound sensors detect early shockwaves from earthquakes, violent volcanic eruptions, and approaching tsunamis hours before macroscopic devastation occurs! In medical lithotripsy, concentrated high-energy ultrasound pulses shatter hard kidney stones inside patients into fine powder without any surgical incisions! In heavy industry, ultrasound cleans delicate electronic components in spiral tanks and detects invisible internal cracks inside metal aircraft wings and railway tracks! From deep subterranean seismic monitoring to life-saving medical procedures, inaudible acoustic frequencies transform modern technology, providing indispensable diagnostic and industrial capabilities without destructive testing or radioactive hazards.

Echolocation & Sonar

Sections 10.8.1 & Example 10.6: Bats, Submarines & 2d = vt

Slide 15 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Nature and human engineering converge beautifully in Section 10.8.1: Echolocation and SONAR! At night, flying bats emit high-pitched ultrasonic squeaks. When these high-frequency pulses bounce off tiny flying moths or tree branches, the bat's specialized ears analyze the echo's delay, pitch shift, and direction—building an instant, three-dimensional acoustic map of their surroundings to seize prey in mid-air! Marine dolphins and toothed whales use the exact same technique underwater. Humans copied nature to create SONAR, which stands for Sound Navigation and Ranging! A ship's underwater transmitter sends out a beam of ultrasonic pulses through seawater: the pulse travels down, reflects off the seabed, a sunken shipwreck, or an enemy submarine, and returns to an underwater receiver. Because the pulse travels down distance d and back up distance d, the total travel path is two d! Thus two d equals speed v multiplied by return time t, so distance d equals v times t divided by two! Look at Example 10.6: if a sonar signal returns in 0.90 seconds in seawater where sound speed is 1530 meters per second, the underwater object distance is 1530 times 0.90 divided by 2, which equals 688.5 meters! Whether mapping the deepest ocean trenches or tracking marine life, sonar demonstrates the unmatched versatility of ultrasonic waves underwater.

Let us synthesize all of Chapter 10 in our comprehensive At a Glance roadmap!
