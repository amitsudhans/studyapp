Welcome, students, to Chapter 6, How Forces Affect Motion. In Chapter 4, we learned how to describe motion using position, velocity, acceleration, and graphs. Now we ask a deeper question: what actually causes motion to start, speed up, slow down, or turn? The answer is force, and studying how forces act brings us to Isaac Newton's three laws of motion. Whether a tiny raindrop falls under Earth's pull or a massive rocket blasts upward from a launchpad, every change in velocity traces back to forces acting on an object. Section 6.1.1: Spring Balance, Weight & Yoctonewton Precision

Slide 2 of 20

How do scientists measure the exact magnitude of a force? In the school laboratory, the classic instrument is a spring balance! Inside a spring balance sits a coiled steel spring attached to a pointer moving along a calibrated newton scale. When you pull the hook—or hang an object from it—the spring stretches in proportion to the applied pull. Hanging a mango or a metal block from the hook measures its weight, which is the downward gravitational pull of the Earth on that object. Notice the difference: mass stays the same everywhere, whereas weight is a force measured in newtons! Now look at Threads of Curiosity to see how sensitive force measurements can get. On your fingertips, human perception can barely feel a tiny feather-light tap around one millinewton, or ten to the minus three newtons. Yet modern physics laboratories using trapped ions and optical tweezers can detect astonishingly tiny forces down to yoctonewtons—around ten to the minus twenty-four newtons! From bridge-testing hydraulic presses to single-atom sensors, measuring force accurately lets engineers and scientists test materials, weigh payloads, and probe nature at every scale, from tiny atomic traps up to heavy suspension bridges carrying daily traffic.

Balanced & Unbalanced

Section 6.2 & Example 6.1: Net Force, Tug-of-War & Couples

Slide 3 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

In real life, an object almost never experiences just one lonely force. When you push a heavy wooden crate across the floor, friction pushes backward against your push; when a plastic ball floats on a pond, Earth's gravity pulls downward while water's buoyant force pushes upward! To predict motion, we must find the net force—the vector sum of all forces acting on the object. Imagine a tug-of-war: if both teams pull with equal and opposite forces, the rope stays still because the net force is zero. These are balanced forces, and they cause zero change in motion. If one team pulls harder, the forces become unbalanced, producing a non-zero net force in the direction of the larger pull! Look at Example 6.1 with two forces of ten newtons and six newtons on a block. If both push right, they add to sixteen newtons right. If ten newtons pushes right and six newtons pushes left, they subtract to four newtons right; reverse them and you get four newtons left! In Ready to Go Beyond, notice what happens when two equal opposite forces act at different points—like turning a bicycle handlebar or a water tap: they form a couple causing rotation.

Why does a rolling cricket ball eventually stop on grass even after you stop kicking it? Because of friction—a contact force that always opposes relative motion between touching surfaces! On a level floor, gravity pulls a box downward while the floor pushes straight upward with a perpendicular normal force, keeping vertical forces balanced. Long ago, people wrongly believed a continuous push was needed to keep anything moving. This brilliant realization overturned centuries of confusion and paved the way for Newton's laws.

Newton's First Law

Section 6.4, Examples 6.2–6.3: Inertia & Zero Net Force Graphs

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Building directly on Galileo's insight, Isaac Newton formulated his First Law of Motion: an object at rest remains at rest, and an object in motion continues moving with constant velocity—same speed and same straight-line direction—unless acted upon by a net external force! This natural tendency of every object to resist any change in its state of rest or uniform motion is called inertia. That is why passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes suddenly, and why shaking a tree branch drops ripe fruits. Let us check Example 6.2: suppose you push a heavy box across a floor with a forward force exactly equal and opposite to the backward friction force. Because the forward push and backward friction cancel out, the net force is zero, so the box glides forward at a steady constant velocity! Now look at Example 6.3, which draws motion graphs for zero net force. For a stationary object, the position-time graph is a flat horizontal line and the velocity-time graph sits along zero. For an object in uniform motion under zero net force, the position-time graph is a straight inclined line and the velocity-time graph is a flat horizontal line above zero!

What happens when forces on an object are NOT balanced? A non-zero net force produces acceleration! Let us see how scientists measure this relationship cleanly in Activities 6.3 and 6.4 using a low-friction lab cart connected by a string over a pulley to a small hanging mass cup. Using slow-motion video, students measure the exact time T taken for the cart to travel a fixed track distance s from rest, computing acceleration via s equals half a T squared. First, in Activity 6.3, keep the cart's mass unchanged and double the hanging mass in the cup, which doubles the pulling force! The travel time drops sharply, showing that acceleration increases in direct proportion to the net applied force: double the force, double the acceleration! Second, in Activity 6.4, keep the hanging pulling force constant, but double the total mass on the cart by adding extra blocks. Now the cart speeds up much more sluggishly, taking longer to cover distance s. Acceleration drops to half—proving acceleration is inversely proportional to mass! Larger mass means greater inertia, making it harder for the same force to change the object's velocity. Keep these two clear trends in mind as we write the law!

Force & Momentum

Section 6.5: F = ma, 1 Newton, Weight F = mg & Momentum p = mv

Slide 7 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Combining Activities 6.3 and 6.4 gives us Newton's Second Law of Motion in crisp mathematical form: acceleration a equals net force F divided by mass m, or F equals m times a! From this equation, we define the SI unit newton cleanly: one newton is the force that gives a one-kilogram mass an acceleration of one metre per second squared. We can also calculate an object's weight instantly: since gravity accelerates falling objects at g equals nine point eight metres per second squared, Earth's gravitational pull is F equals m times g. A ten-kilogram backpack weighs ninety-eight newtons! Now look at Ready to Go Beyond for an even deeper view using momentum. Momentum, denoted by p, equals mass times velocity: p equals m v. A heavy truck moving slowly and a light bullet moving fast can both carry huge momentum! Because a equals v minus u over t, multiplying by m shows that F equals m v minus m u divided by t—meaning net force equals the rate of change of momentum delta p divided by delta t! This momentum viewpoint explains collisions and safety engineering across cars, sports equipment, and protective helmets used in everyday life.

F = ma & Momentum (p = mv)

Impulse & Safety

Section 6.5 Real-World Applications: Cricket Catch, Airbags & Coconuts

Slide 8 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

The momentum form of Newton's Second Law—force equals change in momentum divided by time—explains clever tricks in sports, road safety, and everyday life! Watch a skilled cricket fielder catching a fast-moving ball: instead of holding their hands rigid, the fielder pulls their hands backward smoothly as the ball lands. Why? Stopping the ball requires the exact same change in momentum delta p, but pulling the hands back increases the stopping time delta t! Since delta t sits in the denominator, a longer impact time drastically reduces the stinging force on the palms! Automotive airbags use the exact same physics during a car crash: inflating in milliseconds, the soft cushion extends the driver's stopping time, lowering peak impact force on the chest and head to prevent fatal injuries. Now flip the idea around: what if you want a HUGE force to crack open a hard coconut shell? You strike it with a rapid, high-velocity blow against a hard rock so the stopping time delta t is tiny—producing a massive peak force that shatters the shell instantly! Always remember this golden rule: longer stopping time cuts force, while shorter stopping time boosts peak impact force dramatically during collisions.

F = Δp / Δt: Stopping Time & Impact Force

Newton's Third Law

Section 6.6, Activities 6.5 & 6.6: Action-Reaction Pairs

Slide 10 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Have you noticed that whenever you push against something, it pushes right back at you? That universal symmetry is Newton's Third Law of Motion: whenever one object exerts a force on a second object, the second object simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on the first object! Try Activity 6.5: sit on a smooth wheeled office chair and push forward against a heavy fixed table. Instantly, your chair rolls backward! Your hands pushed the table forward, and the table pushed you backward with equal force. Pull the table toward yourself, and the table pulls your chair forward! Next, verify the exact numbers in Activity 6.6: hook two spring balances together and pull in opposite directions. No matter how gently or strongly you pull, both spring balances display identical newton readings at the exact same instant! Here is a crucial exam tip: why do action and reaction forces NEVER cancel each other out? Because they always act on TWO DIFFERENT objects! Your foot pushes the ground, while the ground pushes your body—different objects, so both forces remain real and uncancelled. Keep that key distinction crystal clear whenever you draw action-reaction force pairs in physics problems!

Rockets & Propulsion

Section 6.6 & Activity 6.7: Walking, Canoeing, Balloons & Chandrayaan-3

Newton's Third Law powers everything from a morning walk to lunar landings! When you walk or run, your foot pushes backward against the ground, and static friction from the ground pushes your body forward. When a farmer climbs a tall coconut tree, their hands and feet push downward on the trunk, and the trunk pushes them upward. When rowing a canoe, the paddle pushes water backward, so the water propels the canoe forward! Watch Activity 6.7 for a thrilling miniature rocket: thread a drinking straw taped to an inflated balloon onto a taut horizontal string, then release the neck. As air rushes backward out of the balloon, the escaping gas pushes the balloon forward along the string! Real space rockets work identically even in the airless vacuum of outer space—they do NOT need air to push against! Hot exhaust gases shoot downward at huge speed, propelling the rocket upward. And during India's historic Chandrayaan-3 mission, the Vikram lander fired its retro-thrusters downward toward the Moon's surface, using upward reaction thrust to brake gently for a soft lunar landing! Even far beyond Earth's atmosphere, Newton's Third Law remains our most reliable engine for deep space travel.

Non-Contact Action-Reaction

Section 6.6, Examples 6.7 & 6.8: Gravity, Magnets & Gun Recoil

Slide 12 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Does Newton's Third Law apply only when objects touch? Not at all! Action-reaction pairs operate across empty space for non-contact forces too—including magnetic, electrostatic, and gravitational forces. Hold two bar magnets near each other: Magnet A pulls Magnet B with the exact same force that Magnet B pulls Magnet A! Now look at Example 6.7: when a ripe mango falls from a tree, Earth pulls the mango downward with a gravitational force F, and the mango simultaneously pulls Earth upward with the exact same force F! Why do we see the mango race downward while Earth seems completely motionless? Because acceleration equals F divided by mass! The mango's tiny mass gets a large acceleration of nine point eight metres per second squared, whereas Earth's colossal mass receives an unimaginably tiny acceleration! Example 6.8 shows the same mass contrast in a rifle recoil: a two-newton firing force pushes a zero-point-one-kilogram bullet forward at twenty metres per second squared, while kicking the five-kilogram gun backward at just zero point four metres per second squared! Equal action-reaction forces always produce much larger accelerations on lighter masses and much smaller accelerations on heavier masses in every real physical interaction.

Multi-Body Systems

Section 6.7: Connected Masses, Internal Tension & System Acceleration

Slide 13 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

What if two or more objects are linked together, such as two train coaches coupled together, or two boxes of masses m1 and m2 joined by a taut string and pulled forward by an external force F? Let us see a brilliant problem-solving shortcut from Section 6.7! On the front box m1, the external force F pulls forward while the connecting string pulls backward with tension T. Meanwhile, on the rear box m2, that exact same string pulls forward with equal and opposite tension T! Inside the combined two-box system, those two tension forces are internal action-reaction pairs—equal in magnitude and opposite in direction—so when we treat both boxes and the string as a single combined system, the internal tension T cancels out completely! Only the external pulling force F accelerates the total mass m1 plus m2. Therefore, the system's common acceleration is simply a equals F divided by m1 plus m2! Once you find a, you can immediately compute the string tension T if needed by multiplying rear mass m2 times acceleration a. Grouping connected objects simplifies complex multi-body problems in seconds! You can apply this exact same system trick to three or more linked blocks or train coaches.

Friction Engineering

The Journey Beyond: Maglev, Capstan Effect & Newton's Principia

Slide 14 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Engineers constantly balance friction—sometimes boosting it for safe tyres and brakes, and other times slashing it to save energy! In The Journey Beyond, explore how modern transport minimizes sliding friction. Ball bearings replace sliding friction with much smaller rolling friction inside bicycle hubs and electric motors, while thin oil lubricants separate metal engine parts. Even more futuristic, magnetic levitation—or Maglev—trains use powerful electromagnets to lift the entire train slightly above the track, eliminating wheel-rail contact friction so trains glide past four hundred kilometres per hour! On the other hand, sailors and mountaineers exploit the amazing capstan effect: wrapping a rope just a few turns around a smooth cylindrical post multiplies rope friction exponentially, allowing one person to hold back a massive ship! You can test this safely with a broomstick and string, or use two toy cars with repelling magnets to witness non-contact Third Law pairs. For deeper inspiration, read translated excerpts from Isaac Newton's 1687 masterpiece, Principia Mathematica, where these three universal laws were first united. Understanding when to boost friction for safety and when to eliminate it for energy efficiency lies at the very heart of modern mechanical, aerospace, and high-speed transport engineering.

Chapter Summary

At a Glance: Friction, Inertia & Newton's Three Laws

Slide 16 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Let us bring all of Chapter 6 together in one crisp At a Glance summary! First, force is a vector push or pull measured in newtons with a spring balance; balanced forces give zero net force and zero acceleration, while unbalanced forces change velocity. Second, friction opposes relative motion between surfaces, depends on surface smoothness, and led Galileo to discover that frictionless motion continues forever. Third, Newton's First Law defines inertia: objects resist changes to rest or uniform velocity unless acted on by a net external force. Fourth, Newton's Second Law gives F equals m a and F equals delta p over delta t, explaining weight F equals m g, cricket catches, airbags, and vehicle braking. Fifth, Newton's Third Law states that every action has an equal and opposite reaction acting simultaneously on two DIFFERENT bodies—governing walking, swimming, balloon rockets, Chandrayaan-3 retro-firing, magnets, and gun recoil. Finally, for connected multi-body systems, internal tensions cancel so acceleration equals external force divided by total mass. You are now ready for the four silent mastery quizzes!
