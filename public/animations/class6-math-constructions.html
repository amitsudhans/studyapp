Ruler & Compass

Precision Instruments vs Freehand Sketching

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Geometry requires precision instruments: a straightedge for lines, a compass for circles and length transfers, and a protractor for angles.

In art, freehand sketching allows expressive, approximate forms. In geometry, however, precision is paramount. We use two classical instruments to create exact geometric shapes:

Ruler (Straightedge) Used to draw perfectly straight line segments and to measure linear distances in centimeters and millimeters.
Compass Used to draw circles and arcs of fixed radii, and to transfer exact lengths from one segment to another without reading numeric markings.
Protractor Used to measure and construct exact angles, particularly the critical 90° right angles needed for squares and rectangles.

A geometric construction differs from a simple drawing because every line, corner, and arc is defined by logical geometric relationships rather than guesswork.

Circles & Radius

The Locus of Points Equidistant from a Central Point

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
A circle is the set of all points at a fixed distance (radius r) from a central point P. Diameter is twice the radius (d = 2r).

A curve is any continuous path drawn on paper without lifting the pencil. When points are plotted such that every single point is at the exact same distance from a fixed center point P, they form a circle:

• Center (P): The fixed interior point where the metal compass needle rests.
• Radius (r): The constant distance from the center to any point on the boundary curve (e.g., r = 4 cm).
• Diameter (d): A straight segment passing through the center connecting two boundary points (d = 2 × r = 8 cm).

Geometric Definition: Circle = { Point X : Distance(P, X) = r }

If the compass needle slips, the radius varies, and the resulting curve is no longer a circle.

Waves & Motifs

Constructing Semicircles, Waves & Eye Arcs

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Alternating semicircular arcs along a baseline create smooth waves. Intersecting dual arcs create symmetrical eye motifs.

By placing the compass needle at strategically spaced points along a straight baseline, we can construct elegant repeating curves and natural motifs:

Three Classical Arc Constructions:

• The Wavy Wave: Divide a baseline AB (e.g., 8 cm) into equal 2 cm intervals. Draw alternating semicircles: first above the line using center C₁, then below using center C₂, producing an undulating continuous wave.

• The Symmetrical Eye: Mark two points B and C. Draw an upward arc from B centered at C, and a downward arc from C centered at B. The two intersecting arcs enclose an almond or eye-shaped motif.

• Composite Figures: A round head circle joined to shoulder arcs by selecting exact tangent center positions.

Complex patterns in traditional Indian Rangoli and Islamic geometric art are built entirely from intersecting circular arcs centered on regular grids.

Rectangles & Squares

The Sacred Right-Angle Quadrilaterals

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
A rectangle has opposite sides equal and 4 right angles (90°). A square has all 4 sides equal and 4 right angles. Every square is a rectangle.

Squares and rectangles are four-sided closed figures (quadrilaterals) defined by strict side-length and interior-angle rules:

Rectangle Properties:
• R1: Opposite sides are equal in length (AB = CD and BC = DA).
• R2: All four interior angles are right angles (∠A = ∠B = ∠C = ∠D = 90°).
Square Properties:
• S1: All four sides are equal in length (AB = BC = CD = DA).
• S2: All four interior angles are right angles (90° each).

Mathematical Heirarchy: Every square is a special rectangle (since all sides equal implies opposite sides are equal), but not every rectangle is a square!

A quadrilateral with four equal sides is a rhombus; only when its angles are also 90° does it become a square.

Naming Vertices

The Universal Cyclical Perimeter Rule

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Vertices must be named consecutively along the outer perimeter (clockwise or counter-clockwise): ABCD is valid, ABDC is invalid.

In geometry, naming a polygon is not arbitrary. You cannot mix up vertex letters across diagonals:

The Golden Rule of Vertex Naming:

Letters must be listed in consecutive cyclical order as you walk around the perimeter (either clockwise or counterclockwise).

VALID NAMES ABCD • BCDA
CDAB • DABC
ADCB • DCBA
INVALID NAMES ABDC • ACBD
ACDB • BDCA
(Jumps across diagonal!)

If you name a square 'ABDC', you are implying that edge B connects directly to edge D, crossing through the center of the shape!

Rotated Shapes

Orientation Invariance on Dot Grids

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Rotation preserves side lengths and interior angles. A square rotated at 45° on a dot grid remains a true geometric square.

A common misconception is that a square tilted on its corner is no longer a square, but a "diamond". In mathematics, orientation does not change identity:

Properties are Invariant under Rotation:

• Rotating a square by 45° tilts its sides relative to the page margins, but every side remains exactly equal in length (s).
• Every corner remains an exact 90° right angle.
• Therefore, a rotated square remains 100% a mathematical square!

Dot Grid Test: Slanted sides with steps (e.g. 2 right, 1 up) must have perpendicular steps (1 right, 2 down) to form 90° corners!

A "diamond" is an informal everyday word. In geometry, if its angles are 90°, it is a rotated square; if not 90°, it is a rhombus.

Constructing Squares

Step-by-Step Compass & Protractor Protocol

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Construct a square by drawing baseline s, raising 90° perpendiculars at both endpoints, cutting arcs of length s, and joining the top.

To construct a square of side length s = 6 cm with absolute precision, follow this 4-step sequence:

Step 1 (Baseline): Draw straight horizontal segment AB = 6 cm using a ruler.
Step 2 (Perpendiculars): At vertex A and vertex B, construct rays perpendicular to AB (90° angles using a protractor or compass arcs).
Step 3 (Compass Arcs): Set compass opening to exactly 6 cm. Place needle at A and cut an arc on the ray to locate point D. Place needle at B and cut an arc to locate point C.
Step 4 (Closing Edge): Join point D to point C with a straight ruler segment.

Always draw a quick rough sketch first with dimensions labeled to guide your formal instrument construction!

Constructing Rectangles

Unequal Length & Breadth with Perpendicular Corners

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Constructing a rectangle l × b requires drawing base l, raising 90° rays, cutting arcs of length b, and connecting the top edge l.

To construct a rectangle of length l = 6 cm and breadth b = 4 cm, the procedure mirrors the square, but uses two distinct compass settings:

1. Baseline: Draw base segment AB = 6 cm.
2. Perpendicular Rays: Use a protractor to erect 90° perpendicular lines upward from point A and point B.
3. Breadth Arcs: Set compass radius to 4 cm. Place needle at A to cut ray at D; place needle at B to cut ray at C (AD = BC = 4 cm).
4. Closing Segment: Draw segment CD. Measure CD with ruler: it must measure exactly 6 cm!

Verification check: If CD does not equal 6 cm, or if ∠C and ∠D are not 90°, one of your perpendiculars was inaccurate.

Point-to-Point Distance

Dynamic Distance Variation Across Rectangle Edges

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Between opposite sides of a rectangle, the minimum distance is the perpendicular breadth (4 cm); the maximum distance is the diagonal.

Consider rectangle ABCD of dimensions 7 cm × 4 cm. Let point X move along top edge AD, and point Y move along bottom edge BC:

What happens to the straight-line distance XY?

• Minimum Distance: When X and Y are directly opposite each other (perpendicular line segment XY ⊥ AD). Here, XY = 4 cm (the breadth of the rectangle).
• Maximum Distance: When X is at one corner (A) and Y is at the furthest opposite corner (C). Here, XY is the diagonal AC (approx 8.06 cm).

As point Y glides away from the perpendicular alignment, the line segment XY slants, forming the hypotenuse of a right triangle, which is always longer than the perpendicular breadth!

Geometric Invariant: The perpendicular distance between two parallel lines is the shortest possible path connecting them.

Rectangles from Squares

Partitioning and Length Transfer with a Compass

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Rectangles with length = k × breadth divide into k equal squares. Compass stepping transfers breadth lengths accurately without ruler marks.

A rectangle whose length is an exact integer multiple of its breadth can be partitioned into identical adjoining squares:

Examples of Modular Rectangles:

• 2-Square Rectangle: Dimensions 8 cm × 4 cm divides into two 4 cm × 4 cm squares.
• 3-Square Rectangle: Dimensions 12 cm × 4 cm divides into three 4 cm × 4 cm squares.

Compass Transfer Technique: Instead of measuring with a ruler, open your compass to breadth AD (4 cm). Step the needle from A along AB to mark point E, and from E to mark point B. Then raise perpendicular EF to split the rectangle into perfect congruent squares!

Stepping a compass along a line transfers exact lengths without cumulative ruler-reading parallax errors.

Diagonals of Quadrilaterals

Connecting Opposite Corners & Equality in Rectangles

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
A rectangle's diagonals connect opposite vertices, are strictly equal in length (AC = BD), and bisect each other at their midpoint.

A diagonal is a straight line segment connecting two opposite (non-adjacent) vertices of a polygon. A quadrilateral has exactly two diagonals:

Sacred Properties of Rectangular Diagonals:

• In rectangle ABCD, diagonals are AC and BD.
• Equality Theorem: The two diagonals are always exactly equal in length (AC = BD).
• Mutual Bisection: The diagonals cut each other into two equal halves at their intersection point M (AM = MC = BM = MD).

Carpenter's Square Test: Builders verify that a window frame or rectangular foundation is truly square (has 90° corners) by measuring both diagonals. If the diagonals are equal, the corners are 90°!

In a general parallelogram, diagonals are unequal. Equality of diagonals is a defining hallmark of rectangles and squares.

Diagonal Angle Splits

Rectangles (Unequal Angles) vs Squares (Exact 45° Bisectors)

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
In a rectangle, diagonals split 90° into unequal angles (e.g. 60°/30°). In a square, diagonals bisect 90° into equal 45° angles.

A diagonal passes through a 90° right-angle corner, splitting it into two complementary acute angles (α + β = 90°). How do rectangles and squares compare?

General Rectangle (Length ≠ Breadth):
• The diagonal does NOT bisect the corner angle!
• It splits 90° into two unequal angles (e.g. 60° and 30°, or 55° and 35°). The longer side faces the larger angle.
Square (All Sides Equal):
• The diagonal is an exact angle bisector!
• Because both adjacent sides are equal, the diagonal splits 90° into two perfectly equal angles: 45° and 45°.

A diagonal also splits a square into two congruent isosceles right triangles (45°-45°-90°).

Constructing from Diagonals

Given One Side and Diagonal: Compass Arc Intersection

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Given side AB and diagonal AC, erect a 90° ray at B, and strike an arc of radius AC from A. The intersection point defines vertex C.

Suppose you are given one side AB = 5 cm and diagonal AC = 7 cm. How do you construct the rectangle without knowing the breadth BC?

1. Baseline: Draw segment AB = 5 cm using a ruler.
2. Perpendicular Ray: At vertex B, erect a perpendicular ray BX (90° to AB). Vertex C must lie somewhere on this ray.
3. Diagonal Arc Intersection: Set your compass opening to exactly 7 cm. Place the compass needle at vertex A. Swing an arc cutting ray BX. The intersection point is vertex C!
4. Complete Rectangle: Now measure breadth BC with compass. Cut arc from A, cut 5 cm arc from C, locating D.

This construction uses the right-triangle property (hypotenuse AC = 7 cm, base AB = 5 cm) to locate vertex C automatically!

Equidistant Points

Finding Points at Equal Distance via Dual Compass Arcs

Mentors: Acharya Baudhāyana & Euclid of Alexandria (Masters of Sulba Cord Geometry & Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions)
Dual intersecting compass arcs of equal radius centered at B and C locate an equidistant point A (AB = AC) without trial and error.

Suppose you have two fixed points B and C (distance 6 cm apart). You need to find a point A that is at an equal distance of 5 cm from both B and C:

The Dual Arc Intersection Method:

1. Set compass radius to 5 cm.
2. Place needle at point B; draw an arc above the segment.
3. Without changing the compass radius (keep at 5 cm), place needle at point C; draw an intersecting arc.
4. The point where the two arcs cross is point A!

AB = 5 cm and AC = 5 cm → Triangle ABC is an Isosceles Triangle!

If you also draw intersecting arcs below the segment, you locate a second equidistant point A'. Line AA' is the perpendicular bisector of BC!
