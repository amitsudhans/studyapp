Position & Reference

Section 4.1 & 4.1.1: Reference Point O & Sign Convention

Slide 2 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Let us begin with the simplest kind of movement: motion in a straight line, also called linear motion. Think of swimmers racing in straight lanes, an apple falling straight down, or a train gliding along a straight track. To describe where an object is at any moment, stating a number alone is never enough. Saying a bus stop is five hundred metres away leaves us wondering: in which direction? Therefore, describing position requires three ingredients: a fixed reference point called the origin O, a distance from O, and a direction. Relative to this chosen origin O, an object is in motion if its position changes over time, and at rest if its position stays unchanged over time. Along a straight line, there are only two possible directions from the origin O: forward and backward, or right and left. By standard sign convention, positions to the right of origin O are assigned a plus sign, while positions to the left of origin O are assigned a minus sign. For instance, plus forty metres means forty metres right of O, whereas minus twenty metres means twenty metres left of O. Notice that changing your chosen origin O changes the position numbers, even though the object stays right there.

Distance vs Displacement

Section 4.1.2 & Activity 4.1: Scalar Path vs Vector Net Change

Slide 3 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now let us carefully separate two terms that sound similar in daily speech but mean very different things in physics: distance travelled and displacement. Total distance travelled is the entire length of the actual path covered by a moving object. It is a scalar quantity, meaning it needs only a numerical value with units, and it never decreases as long as the object keeps moving. Displacement, by contrast, is the shortest straight-line change in position from the starting point to the final point. Because displacement depends on direction as well as size, it is a vector quantity. Both distance and displacement share the SI unit metre. Let us examine Activity 4.1: a ball is thrown vertically upward from origin O to height ten metres at point A, falls past point B at six metres, and returns to O at point C. At A, distance is ten metres and displacement is plus ten metres. At B on the way down, distance grows to fourteen metres, while displacement drops to plus six metres. At C, distance reaches twenty metres, but net displacement becomes zero! Thus, magnitude of displacement is always less than or equal to total distance.

Average Speed

Section 4.1.3 & Example 4.1: Uniform Motion & Ancient Indian Problems

Slide 4 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

How fast does an object cover its path? Average speed is defined as total distance travelled divided by the total time interval. If an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, no matter how small those intervals are, it is in uniform motion and its speed stays constant. If it covers unequal distances in equal time intervals, like a bus weaving through city traffic, it is in non-uniform motion. Speed-time reasoning has deep roots in India's scientific heritage. Ancient mathematical treatises such as Aryabhata's Aryabhatiya from the fifth century CE and Narayana Pandita's Ganitakaumudi from the fourteenth century CE posed clever motion problems using traditional distance units like the yojana. Look at Example 4.1: two postmen start two hundred and ten yojanas apart and walk directly toward each other. One covers nine yojanas per day, and the other covers five yojanas per day. Together, each day they close the gap by nine plus five, which equals fourteen yojanas per day. Dividing the total distance of two hundred and ten yojanas by their combined daily rate of fourteen yojanas per day gives exactly fifteen days until they meet. This elegant reasoning shows how breaking a motion problem into relative daily progress makes complex journeys simple to solve.

Example 4.1: Ancient Postmen (210 Yojanas)
Combined rate = 9 + 5 = 14 yojanas/day. Time to meet = 210 / 14 = 15 days (Aryabhatiya / Ganitakaumudi tradition).

Average Velocity

Section 4.1.3 & Example 4.2: Directional Rate & Instantaneous Speed

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

While average speed tracks total path length over time, physics also needs to track how quickly position changes in a specific direction. Average velocity is defined as net displacement divided by the time interval, measured in metres per second. Because displacement is a vector, average velocity also carries both magnitude and direction! Consider Example 4.2: a swimmer swims across a twenty-five-metre pool and immediately swims back to the starting wall in fifty seconds. What is the total distance travelled? Twenty-five metres out plus twenty-five metres back equals fifty metres, so the average speed is fifty divided by fifty, or one metre per second. However, the swimmer returns to the exact starting point, making net displacement zero metres! Consequently, the average velocity is zero divided by fifty, which is zero metres per second. Now, what if we want to know a car's speed at one exact instant rather than averaged over a whole trip? Look at the dashboard speedometer: it shows instantaneous speed. Taking the limiting value of average velocity over an infinitesimally small time interval gives instantaneous velocity, bridging averages to exact moments. Whenever an object moves along a straight line at constant velocity, instantaneous velocity equals average velocity.

Average Acceleration

Section 4.1.4, Activity 4.2 & Examples 4.3–4.4: Velocity Change

Slide 6 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

In everyday life, vehicles rarely stay at constant velocity; they speed up at green lights and slow down at stops. To measure how rapidly velocity changes, we define average acceleration as the change in velocity divided by the time interval, written as final velocity v minus initial velocity u, all divided by time t. Its SI unit is metre per second squared. When speed increases in the direction of motion, acceleration is positive and points with velocity. When an object slows down, acceleration points opposite to velocity and is negative. In Activity 4.2, comparing cars accelerating from zero to one hundred kilometres per hour shows that shorter times mean higher acceleration. Look at Example 4.3: a highway bus speeds up from ten to twenty metres per second in twenty seconds, giving a change of plus ten divided by twenty, or plus zero point five metres per second squared. When it brakes from fifteen metres per second to rest in five seconds, acceleration is zero minus fifteen over five, giving minus three metres per second squared. In Example 4.4, a freely falling object gains nine point eight metres per second every second, giving constant acceleration due to gravity g.

Position-Time Graphs

Section 4.2.2, Activity 4.4 & Examples 4.6–4.7: Slopes & Steepness

Slide 8 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now let us learn how to read position-time graphs like a physicist. First, examine the shape of the graph. A straight inclined line tells us the object is moving at constant velocity, because position changes steadily with time. A curved line tells us velocity is changing, meaning the object is accelerating. What about Example 4.6, where the position-time graph is a flat horizontal line? Since position stays fixed as time ticks forward, the object is completely at rest, with zero velocity! Next, in Activity 4.4, we extract a number from a straight line segment AB by computing its geometric slope. Drop vertical and horizontal lines from A and B to form a right triangle ABC. The slope equals vertical rise BC divided by horizontal run CA, which is s2 minus s1 divided by t2 minus t1. That ratio is literally displacement divided by time interval—meaning the slope of a position-time graph equals average velocity! In Example 4.7, we compare two objects A and B on the same axes: the steeper line has a larger slope and therefore moves faster than the gentler line. Even without doing long arithmetic, the steepness of a position-time line immediately reveals which runner is ahead.

Velocity-Time Graphs

Section 4.2.3: Acceleration from Slope & Displacement from Area

Slide 9 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Let us now turn to velocity-time graphs, which pack even more information into a single picture. Here, time remains on the horizontal X-axis, while velocity is plotted on the vertical Y-axis. First, how do we interpret the shape? A horizontal straight line means velocity does not change over time: the object moves with constant velocity and zero acceleration. An inclined straight line sloping upward indicates constant positive acceleration, while a straight line sloping downward indicates constant deceleration. Just as before, let us check the geometric slope between two points on a velocity-time graph: vertical rise v minus u divided by horizontal run t2 minus t1. That ratio is change in velocity over time, so the slope of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration! Even more remarkably, look at the region trapped under the velocity-time line and above the time axis. For constant velocity, the area of that rectangle is height v times width t, which equals displacement! This rule holds for inclined lines too: the total area enclosed under the velocity-time graph with the time axis always equals the object's net displacement. For an accelerating vehicle starting from u and reaching v, that enclosed shape is a trapezium or triangle whose area you can compute in seconds.

First Kinematic Equation

Section 4.3: Deriving v = u + at Under Constant Acceleration

Slide 10 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now that we understand velocity-time graphs, we can derive the famous kinematic equations for motion in a straight line with constant acceleration. Let an object start at time t equals zero with initial velocity u, and accelerate uniformly at rate a until time t, reaching final velocity v. On a velocity-time graph, this motion forms a straight line rising steadily from height u at time zero to height v at time t. Recall that the slope of a velocity-time line equals acceleration a. Looking at the triangle formed above height u, the vertical rise is v minus u, and the horizontal run is time t. Therefore, acceleration a equals v minus u divided by t. Multiply both sides by t to get a times t equals v minus u. Adding u to both sides gives the first equation of motion: v equals u plus a t. Let us appreciate its power: if you know how fast an object started, its steady acceleration, and how long it accelerated, you can predict its exact velocity at any future second without redrawing the graph every time. You can also rearrange it to find how long steady braking takes.

First Equation: v = u + at

Kinematic Equations

Section 4.3: Deriving s = ut + ½at² and v² = u² + 2as

Slide 11 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Let us derive the second and third kinematic equations from the exact same velocity-time trapezium! Remember that displacement s equals the total area enclosed under the velocity-time line from zero to t. Split this trapezium into two clean shapes: a bottom rectangle of height u and width t, plus a top right triangle of base t and height v minus u. The rectangle's area is u times t. The triangle's area is half times base t times height v minus u. From our first equation, v minus u equals a times t. Substituting a t in place of v minus u gives the triangle's area as half a t squared! Adding both areas yields the second equation of motion: s equals u t plus half a t squared. Now, what if a problem does not give you time t? We eliminate t! Write the trapezium area as average velocity, u plus v over two, multiplied by t. From the first equation, t equals v minus u over a. Multiplying them gives s equals v squared minus u squared over two a, which rearranges neatly into our third equation: v squared equals u squared plus two a s.

Area Split: s = ut + ½at²

Section 4.3, Example 4.8 & Safe Driving: Stopping Distance & V2V

Slide 12 of 20

Physics equations save lives on real roads! Let us examine Example 4.8 to see why speeding is so dangerous. Suppose a car brakes with a constant deceleration of four metres per second squared, so a equals minus four metres per second squared, until it stops at v equals zero. First, convert highway speeds into SI units. At fifty-four kilometres per hour, which equals fifteen metres per second, using v squared equals u squared plus two a s gives zero equals fifteen squared minus eight s, so stopping distance s is twenty-eight point one metres. Now double the car's speed to one hundred and eight kilometres per hour, or thirty metres per second. Because stopping distance depends on u squared, doubling speed multiplies stopping distance by four—jumping to one hundred and twelve point five metres! Add to that the distance travelled during the driver's reaction time before the brake pedal is even touched. Modern Bridging Science and Society innovations tackle this with Vehicle-to-Vehicle, or V2V, wireless communication, where cars broadcast braking alerts instantly to warn following drivers before a collision can occur. Keeping safe following gaps always saves precious lives on busy Indian highways every day.

Motion in Plane

Section 4.4 & 3D Extension: 2D Trajectories & 3D Space

So far, our objects moved strictly forward or backward along a single straight line, which is one-dimensional motion. However, most motion around us unfolds across a flat surface or through open space! When an object changes position along two independent directions simultaneously, we call it motion in a plane, or two-dimensional motion. Picture a car overtaking another vehicle on a wide highway: it moves forward along the lane while shifting sideways into the passing lane. Or think of a kicked soccer ball arcing gracefully through the air: it travels horizontally across the field while rising and falling vertically under gravity, tracing a curved projectile path. A Ferris wheel cabin or a ceiling fan blade also moves in a plane, tracing a circle. Can motion go even beyond two dimensions? Yes! In Ready to Go Beyond, consider three-dimensional motion in space. A car climbing a winding mountain road changes its forward, sideways, and upward coordinates all at once, while an aircraft banking through clouds navigates freely along all three spatial axes: length, width, and altitude. Recognizing whether motion is 1D, 2D, or 3D helps us pick the right coordinate axes and physics model right away today.

Uniform Circular Motion

Section 4.4.1 & Activity 4.5: Constant Speed, Changing Velocity

Slide 14 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

One of the most important two-dimensional motions in nature and technology is circular motion, from a spinning bicycle wheel to satellites orbiting Earth. In one complete revolution along a circle of radius R, the total distance travelled is the circumference, two pi R, while the net displacement is zero because the object returns to its starting point. If one full lap takes time T, the average speed is two pi R divided by T. Now, suppose an object moves along a circular path at a strictly constant speed. We call this uniform circular motion. Here is a classic physics surprise: is uniform circular motion accelerated, even though speed never changes? Yes! Remember that velocity has both speed and direction. At every point on the circle, velocity points along the tangent to the circle, so its direction changes continuously! Changing velocity means non-zero acceleration. Activity 4.5 proves this beautifully: spin a marble inside a circular adhesive tape ring, then lift the ring suddenly. The marble flies off in a straight line directly along the tangent at the point of release, confirming that its instantaneous velocity always points strictly tangentially around the circle at every single moment!

Chapter Summary

At a Glance: Complete Kinematics Toolkit & Synthesis

Let us bring the entire chapter together in one crystal-clear mental map. First, describing position requires an origin O, distance, and direction, with plus to the right and minus to the left. Second, distance is the scalar total path length, whereas displacement is the vector net change in position, never exceeding distance. Third, average speed equals total distance over time, while average velocity equals displacement over time—so a round trip has zero average velocity even when average speed is high. Fourth, average acceleration equals v minus u over t in metres per second squared, matching gravity g equals nine point eight metres per second squared in free fall. Fifth, on position-time graphs, slope gives average velocity and horizontal lines mean rest; on velocity-time graphs, slope gives acceleration and enclosed area gives displacement. Sixth, for constant acceleration, our three kinematic equations—v equals u plus a t, s equals u t plus half a t squared, and v squared equals u squared plus two a s—explain everything from free fall to vehicle braking distances. Finally, uniform circular motion has speed two pi R over T yet remains accelerated because tangential velocity direction changes continuously. Great job completing the lectures!
