§ 10.2 Directed Lines Position Vector OP in 3D Right-Handed Coordinate System

Directed Line Segments & The 3D Position Vector

Restricting straight line $l$ to segment $AB$ assigns both a positive distance and a sense of traversal. Taking origin $O(0,0,0)$ gives the position vector $\vec{OP} = \vec{r}$.

Mathematical Definition & Length Metric

1. Directed Line Segment:

Denoted $\vec{AB}$ or $\vec{a}$. Point $A$ is the initial point, point $B$ is the terminal point.

Magnitude: $|\vec{AB}| = |\vec{a}| = a \ge 0$. The notation $|\vec{a}| < 0$ has no meaning!

2. Position Vector $\vec{OP}$:

In a 3D coordinate system, any point $P(x, y, z)$ relative to $O(0,0,0)$ defines $\vec{OP} = \vec{r}$.

|\vec{OP}| = |\vec{r}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}
Interactive Point $P(x, y, z)$: P(4, 3, 5)
|\vec{r}| = \sqrt{4² + 3² + 5²} = 7.07 units

Isometric 3D Space & Projection

Position vector $\vec{OP}$ connects origin $(0,0,0)$ to point $P(x,y,z)$ with Euclidean length $\sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2}$.

§ 10.2 Direction Metrics Direction Angles (α, β, γ) & Direction Cosines (l, m, n)

Direction Cosines: Proof of $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$

Direction angles $\alpha, \beta, \gamma$ are the angles made by vector $\vec{r}$ with the positive $x, y, z$ axes. Their cosines $l, m, n$ uniquely identify 3D spatial orientation.

Right-Triangle Geometry (Fig 10.3)

Right triangles $\triangle OAP, \triangle OBP, \triangle OCP$ give:
l = \cos \alpha = \frac{x}{r}, \quad m = \cos \beta = \frac{y}{r}, \quad n = \cos \gamma = \frac{z}{r}
Coordinates of $P$: $(x, y, z) = (lr, mr, nr)$.
l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = \left(\frac{x}{r}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{y}{r}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{z}{r}\right)^2 = \frac{x^2+y^2+z^2}{r^2} = \frac{r^2}{r^2} = 1
Crucial Distinction (Page 341):
• Direction Cosines $l, m, n$ ALWAYS satisfy $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$.
• Direction Ratios $a, b, c$ (any numbers proportional to $l, m, n$) do NOT sum to 1 in general: $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 \ne 1$!

Slide 4 of 21 • Section 10.3 • Vector Taxonomy Part - 2
§ 10.3 Vector Taxonomy Zero, Unit, Coinitial, Collinear, Equal & Free Vectors

The Six Fundamental Types of Vectors

Vectors are categorized by their geometric traits. In this entire course, all vectors are free vectors: they can be translated parallel to themselves without altering magnitude or direction.

1. Zero Vector $\vec{0}$ |0| = 0

Initial and terminal points coincide ($\vec{AA}, \vec{BB}$). Has zero magnitude and indeterminate direction.

2. Unit Vector $\hat{a}$ |â| = 1

Magnitude is unity (1 unit). Formula: $\hat{a} = \frac{1}{|\vec{a}|}\vec{a}$. Preserves direction with standardized unit length.

3. Coinitial Vectors Same Origin

Two or more vectors sharing the exact same initial point (e.g. $\vec{OA}, \vec{OB}, \vec{OC}$).

4. Collinear Vectors Parallel Lines

Parallel to the same line, irrespective of their magnitudes and whether their arrows point in the same or opposite sense.

5. Equal Vectors |a|=|b| & Same Dir

Identical magnitude AND identical direction, regardless of where their initial points are located.

6. Negative Vector $-\vec{a}$ \vec{BA} = -\vec{AB}

Same magnitude as $\vec{a}$, but arrow is exactly reversed ($180^\circ$ opposite). $\vec{a} + (-\vec{a}) = \vec{0}$.

§ 10.4 Vector Addition The Triangle Law & Closed Polygon Zero Resultant

10.4 Addition of Vectors: The Triangle Law of Addition

When a particle moves from $A$ to $B$ and then from $B$ to $C$, the net displacement is $\vec{AC} = \vec{AB} + \vec{BC}$. Placing vectors head-to-tail yields their sum.

The Triangle Law & Cyclic Zero Sum

Triangle Law Formula:
\vec{AC} = \vec{AB} + \vec{BC}
Since $\vec{AC} = -\vec{CA}$, substituting gives:
\vec{AB} + \vec{BC} + \vec{CA} = \vec{AA} = \vec{0}
When the sides of any polygon are taken in order, initial and terminal points coincide, leading to a zero resultant!
Vector Difference $\vec{a} - \vec{b}$ (Fig 10.8(iii)):
Construct vector $\vec{BC'} = -\vec{b}$ with opposite direction.
Then $\vec{AC'} = \vec{AB} + \vec{BC'} = \vec{a} + (-\vec{b}) = \vec{a} - \vec{b}$.

Interactive Head-to-Tail Visualizer

The third side $AC$ taken in reverse sense completes the triangle and delivers the sum $\vec{a} + \vec{b}$.

Slide 7 of 21 • Section 10.4 • Parallelogram Law Part - 2
§ 10.4 Parallelogram Law River Boat Resultant & Proofs of Commutativity & Associativity

The Parallelogram Law & Fundamental Algebraic Properties

If two vectors represent adjacent sides of a parallelogram, their sum is the coinitial diagonal. This directly proves the commutative and associative laws of vector addition.

Vector Addition Algebra

• Commutative Law: $\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a}$

In parallelogram ABCD: from $\triangle ABC$, $\vec{AC} = \vec{a} + \vec{b}$; from $\triangle ADC$, $\vec{AC} = \vec{b} + \vec{a}$. Hence $\vec{a} + \vec{b} = \vec{b} + \vec{a}$!

• Associative Law: $(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + \vec{c} = \vec{a} + (\vec{b} + \vec{c})$

Let $\vec{PQ} = \vec{a}, \vec{QR} = \vec{b}, \vec{RS} = \vec{c}$. In quadrilateral PQRS, diagonal evaluation proves brackets can be omitted: $\vec{a} + \vec{b} + \vec{c}$.

• Additive Identity: $\vec{a} + \vec{0} = \vec{0} + \vec{a} = \vec{a}$

Physical Model: River Boat Resultant (Page 344)

Triangle law and parallelogram law are geometrically equivalent: $\vec{OA} + \vec{OB} = \vec{OC}$.

§ 10.5 Scalar Scaling Multiplication by Real Scalar λ & Unit Vector Construction

Multiplication of a Vector by a Scalar & Unit Vectors

Multiplying vector $\vec{a}$ by scalar $\lambda$ yields $\lambda\vec{a}$ with magnitude $|\lambda||\vec{a}|$. Direction is conserved for $\lambda > 0$ and reversed $180^\circ$ for $\lambda < 0$.

Scaling Axioms & Unit Vector Construction

• Magnitude Scaling:
|\lambda\vec{a}| = |\lambda| |\vec{a}|
Direction: Same as $\vec{a}$ if $\lambda > 0$; opposite to $\vec{a}$ if $\lambda < 0$.
• Unit Vector in Direction of $\vec{a}$:
If $\lambda = 1/|\vec{a}|$ for non-zero $\vec{a}$, then:
|\lambda\vec{a}| = \left|\frac{1}{|\vec{a}|}\right| |\vec{a}| = \frac{1}{|\vec{a}|} |\vec{a}| = 1
\implies \hat{a} = \frac{1}{|\vec{a}|}\vec{a}

Slide 9 of 21 • Section 10.5.1 • Vector Components Part - 2
§ 10.5.1 Vector Components Unit Basis (î, ĵ, k̂) & The Double Pythagoras Theorem Proof

Components of a Vector in Three-Dimensional Space

Unit vectors $\hat{i}, \hat{j}, \hat{k}$ along axes $OX, OY, OZ$ allow every vector to be uniquely expanded as $\vec{r} = x\hat{i} + y\hat{j} + z\hat{k}$.

Rigorous Double Pythagoras Proof (Page 348)

Step 1: Right Triangle $\triangle OQP_1$ in XY-plane:
|OP_1|^2 = |OQ|^2 + |QP_1|^2 = x^2 + y^2
Step 2: Right Triangle $\triangle OP_1P$ in 3D Space:
Perpendicular from $P$ to XY-plane is $P_1P$ parallel to z-axis ($|P_1P| = z$).
|OP|^2 = |OP_1|^2 + |P_1P|^2 = (x^2 + y^2) + z^2
\implies |\vec{r}| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}

Component-Form Algebra (Page 348)

For $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$:
\vec{a} + \vec{b} = (a_1+b_1)\hat{i} + (a_2+b_2)\hat{j} + (a_3+b_3)\hat{k}
\vec{a} - \vec{b} = (a_1-b_1)\hat{i} + (a_2-b_2)\hat{j} + (a_3-b_3)\hat{k}
\vec{a} = \vec{b} \iff a_1 = b_1, \; a_2 = b_2, \; a_3 = b_3
\lambda\vec{a} = (\lambda a_1)\hat{i} + (\lambda a_2)\hat{j} + (\lambda a_3)\hat{k}

§ 10.5.1 Collinearity Proportionality Criterion & Textbook Examples 4 to 9

Component Collinearity & Standard Textbook Examples

Two vectors are collinear if and only if their scalar components are strictly proportional: $\frac{b_1}{a_1} = \frac{b_2}{a_2} = \frac{b_3}{a_3} = \lambda$.

Proportionality & Distributive Laws

Collinear Condition (Page 349):
\vec{b} = \lambda\vec{a} \iff \frac{b_1}{a_1} = \frac{b_2}{a_2} = \frac{b_3}{a_3} = \lambda
Distributive Laws:
• $k(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) = k\vec{a} + k\vec{b}$
• $(k + m)\vec{a} = k\vec{a} + m\vec{a}$
• $k(m\vec{a}) = (km)\vec{a}$

Benchmark NCERT Examples

Example 6: Unit Vector in direction of $\vec{a} = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + \hat{k}$
|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{2^2 + 3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{14}
\hat{a} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{14}}\hat{i} + \frac{3}{\sqrt{14}}\hat{j} + \frac{1}{\sqrt{14}}\hat{k}
Example 7: Vector in direction of $\vec{a} = \hat{i} - 2\hat{j}$ with magnitude 7
|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{1 + 4} = \sqrt{5} \implies \hat{a} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{5}}\hat{i} - \frac{2}{\sqrt{5}}\hat{j}
7\hat{a} = \frac{7}{\sqrt{5}}\hat{i} - \frac{14}{\sqrt{5}}\hat{j}

§ 10.5.2 Two-Point Vector Vector Joining P₁(x₁, y₁, z₁) and P₂(x₂, y₂, z₂)

10.5.2 Vector Joining Two Points in Space

Applying the triangle law to $\triangle OP_1P_2$ reveals that the vector joining two points is the position vector of the terminal point minus the initial point.

Derivation from Triangle Law (Fig 10.15)

From triangle $\triangle OP_1P_2$:
\vec{OP_1} + \vec{P_1P_2} = \vec{OP_2}
\implies \vec{P_1P_2} = \vec{OP_2} - \vec{OP_1}
\vec{P_1P_2} = (x_2 - x_1)\hat{i} + (y_2 - y_1)\hat{j} + (z_2 - z_1)\hat{k}
Magnitude:
|\vec{P_1P_2}| = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}

Example 10: P(2, 3, 0) to Q(-1, -2, -4)

Initial Point: $P(2, 3, 0)$
Terminal Point: $Q(-1, -2, -4)$
\vec{PQ} = (-1 - 2)\hat{i} + (-2 - 3)\hat{j} + (-4 - 0)\hat{k}
\vec{PQ} = -3\hat{i} - 5\hat{j} - 4\hat{k}
Length: $|\vec{PQ}| = \sqrt{(-3)^2 + (-5)^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 25 + 16} = \sqrt{50} = 5\sqrt{2}$

Slide 12 of 21 • Section 10.5.3 • Section Formula Part - 2
§ 10.5.3 Section Formula Internal Division, External Division & Midpoint Vector

10.5.3 The Section Formula in Vector Algebra

When point $R$ divides the line segment joining $P(\vec{a})$ and $Q(\vec{b})$ in the ratio $m : n$, its position vector $\vec{r}$ is found by balancing vector ratios.

The Three Division Cases

Case I: Internal Division (Fig 10.16)
m \vec{RQ} = n \vec{PR} \implies m(\vec{b} - \vec{r}) = n(\vec{r} - \vec{a})
\vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} + n\vec{a}}{m + n}
Case II: External Division (Fig 10.17)
\vec{r} = \frac{m\vec{b} - n\vec{a}}{m - n}
Midpoint Formula (m = n):
\vec{r} = \frac{\vec{a} + \vec{b}}{2}

Example 11: Ratio 2 : 1 with $\vec{OP} = 3\vec{a} - 2\vec{b}$

Given $\vec{OP} = 3\vec{a} - 2\vec{b}$ and $\vec{OQ} = \vec{a} + \vec{b}$:
• Internal Division (2 : 1):
\vec{OR} = \frac{2(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) + 1(3\vec{a} - 2\vec{b})}{2 + 1} = \frac{5\vec{a}}{3}
• External Division (2 : 1):
\vec{OR} = \frac{2(\vec{a} + \vec{b}) - 1(3\vec{a} - 2\vec{b})}{2 - 1} = 4\vec{b} - \vec{a}

Vertices: $A(2,-1,1), B(1,-3,-5), C(3,-4,-4)$
\vec{AB} = -\hat{i} - 2\hat{j} - 6\hat{k} \implies |\vec{AB}|^2 = 1 + 4 + 36 = 41

Vector Equally Inclined to Axes (Q14)

Given vector $\vec{a} = \hat{i} + \hat{j} + \hat{k}$:
|\vec{a}| = \sqrt{1^2 + 1^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{3}

\cos\alpha = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \quad \cos\beta = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}, \quad \cos\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
\implies \alpha = \beta = \gamma = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right)
Hence the vector is equally inclined to $OX, OY,$ and $OZ$.

§ 10.6.1 Scalar Product Definition, Orthogonality Criterion & Orthonormal Basis

10.6.1 The Scalar (or Dot) Product of Two Vectors

The scalar product produces a real number: $\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\cos\theta$. Orthogonal vectors have zero dot product!

Key Observations & Orthonormal Rules

• Orthogonality Criterion:
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = 0 \iff \vec{a} \perp \vec{b} \quad (\text{for non-zero vectors})
• Orthonormal Basis:
\hat{i}\cdot\hat{i} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{k} = 1
\hat{i}\cdot\hat{j} = \hat{j}\cdot\hat{k} = \hat{k}\cdot\hat{i} = 0
• Component Formula:
\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3
Angle: $\cos\theta = \frac{a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3}{|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|}$

§ 10.6.2 Projections Projection on a Line & Cauchy-Schwarz / Triangle Inequalities

Projection of a Vector on a Line & Master Inequalities

The projection of $\vec{a}$ on line $l$ or vector $\vec{b}$ is its scalar shadow: $\vec{a}\cdot\hat{p} = \frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|}$.

Projection Formula & Sign Rules (Fig 10.20)

Projection Formula:
\text{Projection of } \vec{a} \text{ on } \vec{b} = \frac{\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}}{|\vec{b}|} = |\vec{a}|\cos\theta
• $0^\circ \le \theta < 90^\circ$: $\cos\theta > 0 \implies$ Positive projection in direction of line.
• $\theta = 90^\circ$: $\cos 90^\circ = 0 \implies$ Zero vector projection!
• $90^\circ < \theta \le 180^\circ$: $\cos\theta < 0 \implies$ Negative projection opposite to line.

Cauchy-Schwarz & Triangle Inequalities

• Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality (Example 19):
|\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| |\vec{b}|

Proof: $|\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b}| = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}||\cos\theta| \le |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|(1)$.

• Triangle Inequality (Example 20):
|\vec{a} + \vec{b}| \le |\vec{a}| + |\vec{b}|

Equality holds if and only if points $A, B, C$ are collinear ($\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ have same direction).

Slide 16 of 21 • Section 10.6.3 • Cross Product Part - 2
§ 10.6.3 Cross Product Right-Hand Rule, Anti-Commutativity & Geometric Area

10.6.3 Vector (or Cross) Product of Two Vectors

The cross product produces a vector perpendicular to both inputs: $\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta \hat{n}$. Its magnitude gives the area of a parallelogram!

Definition & 3x3 Determinant Form

• Definition: $\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = |\vec{a}||\vec{b}|\sin\theta \hat{n}$
• Parallel criterion: $\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = \vec{0} \iff \vec{a} \parallel \vec{b}$
• Anti-Commutative: $\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = -(\vec{b}\times\vec{a})$
• Cyclic Unit Basis:
$\hat{i}\times\hat{j} = \hat{k}, \quad \hat{j}\times\hat{k} = \hat{i}, \quad \hat{k}\times\hat{i} = \hat{j}$
\vec{a}\times\vec{b} = \begin{vmatrix} \hat{i} & \hat{j} & \hat{k} \\ a_1 & a_2 & a_3 \\ b_1 & b_2 & b_3 \end{vmatrix}

Geometric Area Formulas

• Area of Parallelogram:
\text{Area} = |\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|

Base $|\vec{b}|$ times altitude $|\vec{a}|\sin\theta = |\vec{a}\times\vec{b}|$.

• Area of Triangle ABC:
\text{Area} = \frac{1}{2} |\vec{AB} \times \vec{AC}|
• Angle Formula:
\sin\theta = \frac{|\vec{a} \times \vec{b}|}{|\vec{a}||\vec{b}|}
