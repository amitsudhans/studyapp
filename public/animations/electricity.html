Water Analogy & Continuous Circuit Path

Electricity is a controllable and convenient form of energy used in homes, schools, hospitals, and industries.

  • Flow of Charge: Just as water flows in rivers to form water currents, electric charges (free electrons in metals) flow through conductors to constitute an electric current.
  • Electric Circuit: A continuous and closed path of an electric current. If the circuit is broken anywhere (or the switch is turned off), the current stops flowing immediately.
  • Role of a Switch: A switch makes a conducting link between the power source (cell or battery) and the load (such as a torch bulb).

Rate of Flow of Charge & Flow Directions

  • Quantitative Definition: Electric current ($I$) is defined as the amount of net electric charge ($Q$) flowing across any cross-section of a conductor per unit time ($t$):
    $$I = \frac{Q}{t}$$
  • Charge Carriers: In metallic conductors, negatively charged electrons constitute the moving charges.
  • Conventional Direction: Electricity was discovered before electrons were known. Historically, current was considered the flow of positive charges. Conventionally, current direction is taken as opposite to the direction of electron flow (from positive terminal to negative terminal outside the cell).

The Coulomb & Electron Number Derivation

The SI unit of electric charge is the coulomb (C).

  • Each electron carries an elementary negative charge of magnitude $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.
  • Number of electrons ($n$) in $1\text{ C}$ of charge:
    $$Q = n e \implies n = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{1\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{18} \approx 6 \times 10^{18}\text{ electrons}$$
  • Thus, $1\text{ coulomb}$ is equivalent to the collective charge carried by nearly $6 \times 10^{18}$ electrons!

The Ampere & Ammeter Measurement in Series

  • Ampere (A): Named in honour of French physicist André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836).
    One Ampere is constituted by the flow of one coulomb of charge per second:
    $$1\text{ A} = 1\text{ C} / 1\text{ s} = 1\text{ C}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$$
  • Submultiples: Milliampere ($1\text{ mA} = 10^{-3}\text{ A}$) and Microampere ($1\text{ }\mu\text{A} = 10^{-6}\text{ A}$).
  • Ammeter: An instrument with very low resistance used to measure current. It is always connected in series in the circuit branch so that the entire current to be measured passes through it.
  • Example 11.1: Current $I = 0.5\text{ A}$ for $t = 10\text{ min} = 600\text{ s} \implies Q = It = 0.5 \times 600 = 300\text{ C}$.

The Hydraulic Analogy & Chemical Origin

Charges do not flow in a copper wire on their own, just as water in a horizontal tube does not flow without a height or pressure difference.

  • Electric Pressure Difference: In a circuit, electrons move only if there is a difference of electric pressure—called the potential difference ($V$)—along the conductor.
  • Chemical Action of a Cell: Chemical reactions inside a cell create a potential difference across its terminals even when no current is drawn. Connecting the cell to a circuit releases this chemical energy to establish a steady current.

Mathematical Definition, The Volt & Voltmeter

Electric potential difference ($V$) between two points is defined as the work done ($W$) to move a unit charge ($Q$) from one point to the other:

$$V = \frac{W}{Q} \iff W = V Q$$

  • The Volt (V): Named after Italian physicist Alessandro Volta (1745–1827).
    One Volt is the potential difference between two points when 1 joule of work is done to move a charge of 1 coulomb between them:
    $$1\text{ V} = \frac{1\text{ J}}{1\text{ C}} = 1\text{ J}\cdot\text{C}^{-1}$$
  • Voltmeter: An instrument with very high resistance used to measure potential difference. It is always connected in parallel across the two points under test.
  • Example 11.2: Moving $Q = 2\text{ C}$ across $V = 12\text{ V} \implies W = VQ = 12 \times 2 = 24\text{ J}$.

Ohm’s Law & The Linear V–I Characteristic

Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator
Teacher's Interactive Audio Commentary

"Potential difference is directly proportional to current—yielding a straight line through the origin whose slope reveals resistance."

Georg Simon Ohm formulated in 1827 that at constant temperature, the current in a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.

Activity 11.1 & Mathematical Formulation

In Activity 11.1, a nichrome wire $XY$ is connected to 1, 2, 3, and 4 cells in turn. In every trial, the ratio $V/I$ remains constant!

  • Ohm's Law Statement: The potential difference, $V$, across the ends of a given metallic wire in an electric circuit is directly proportional to the current $I$ flowing through it, provided its temperature remains constant:

$$V \propto I \implies \frac{V}{I} = \text{constant} = R \implies V = I R$$

  • Resistance ($R$): The property of a conductor to oppose or resist the flow of charges through it.
  • The Ohm ($\Omega$): SI unit of resistance. $1\ \Omega = \frac{1\text{ V}}{1\text{ A}}$. If $V = 1\text{ V}$ produces $I = 1\text{ A}$, the resistance is exactly $1\ \Omega$.

The V–I Graph & Inverse Current Relationship

  • Linear V–I Graph (Fig 11.3): A plot of $V$ against $I$ for a metallic conductor is a straight line passing through the origin $(0, 0)$. The slope $\Delta V / \Delta I$ gives the resistance $R$.
  • Inverse Dependence ($I = V/R$): Current is directly proportional to voltage and inversely proportional to resistance:

$$I = \frac{V}{R}$$

  • If resistance is doubled while voltage remains constant, the current is halved!
  • If resistance is halved, current doubles.

Concept Checkpoint 2

Activity 11.2 & The Spectrum of Conductivity

In Activity 11.2, inserting different materials into circuit gap $XY$ produces different ammeter readings:

  • Good Conductor: A component of a given size that offers very low resistance (e.g., silver, copper, aluminium).
  • Resistor: A conductor that possesses an appreciable resistance designed to limit current (e.g., nichrome wire, carbon resistors).
  • Poor Conductor: A component that offers higher resistance than ordinary metals.
  • Insulator: A substance that offers extremely high resistance, practically blocking current flow entirely (e.g., rubber, glass, dry paper).

Microscopic Cause of Electrical Resistance

  • Atomic Collisions: Electrons do not travel unimpeded through a metal. As they accelerate under the electric field, they repeatedly collide with positive metal ions/atoms in the lattice.
  • These collisions retard electron forward drift and convert kinetic energy into thermal vibrations (heat).
  • Rheostat Operation: A rheostat contains a coil of high-resistance wire (like nichrome) and a sliding contact. Sliding the contact changes the effective length of wire in the circuit, thus smoothly varying $R$ and controlling $I$.

Activity 11.3 Experimental Findings

By testing different wires in circuit Fig 11.5, we observe:

  • Doubling Length ($l \to 2l$): Current in the ammeter decreases to exactly one-half. Thus, resistance doubles!
  • Thicker Wire ($A \to 2A$): Current in the ammeter increases. A thicker wire offers lower resistance!
  • Different Material (Copper vs Nichrome): Current changes drastically even when length and cross-section are identical!

The Master Equation & Electrical Resistivity (ρ)

Combining experimental proportionalities:

$$R \propto l \quad \text{and} \quad R \propto \frac{1}{A} \implies R \propto \frac{l}{A} \implies R = \rho \frac{l}{A}$$

  • Resistivity ($\rho$, rho): A constant of proportionality called the electrical resistivity of the material.
  • SI Unit of Resistivity: $\rho = \frac{R A}{l} \implies \frac{\Omega \cdot \text{m}^2}{\text{m}} = \mathbf{\Omega \cdot \text{m}}$ (ohm-metre).
  • Characteristic Property: Resistivity does NOT depend on the length or shape of the wire; it depends strictly on the nature of the material and temperature!

Conductors vs Alloys (Table 11.2)

Why Heating Appliances Use Alloys & Insulators

Example 11.3 & 11.4 Solutions

  • Example 11.3 (Bulb vs Heater on 220 V):
    (a) Bulb filament ($R = 1200\ \Omega$): $I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{220\text{ V}}{1200\ \Omega} = 0.18\text{ A}$.
    (b) Heater coil ($R = 100\ \Omega$): $I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{220\text{ V}}{100\ \Omega} = 2.2\text{ A}$.
    Observation: Lower resistance appliance draws over 12 times more current!
  • Example 11.4 (Heater Voltage Increase):
    Initially: $V_1 = 60\text{ V}, I_1 = 4\text{ A} \implies R = \frac{60}{4} = 15\ \Omega$.
    When voltage increases to $120\text{ V}$: $I_2 = \frac{120\text{ V}}{15\ \Omega} = 8\text{ A}$.

Example 11.5 & 11.6 Solutions

  • Example 11.5 (Predicting Wire Material):
    $l = 1\text{ m}, R = 26\ \Omega, d = 0.3\text{ mm} = 3 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$.
    $A = \frac{\pi d^2}{4} = \frac{\pi (3 \times 10^{-4})^2}{4} = 7.068 \times 10^{-8}\text{ m}^2$.
    $\rho = \frac{RA}{l} = \frac{26 \times 7.068 \times 10^{-8}}{1} = 1.84 \times 10^{-6}\ \Omega\cdot\text{m}$.
    Conclusion: Table 11.2 identifies this material as manganese!
  • Example 11.6 (Wire Geometry Variation):
    Initial wire: $R_1 = \rho \frac{l}{A} = 4\ \Omega$.
    New wire with length $l/2$ and area $2A$:
    $R_2 = \rho \frac{l/2}{2A} = \frac{1}{4} \left(\rho \frac{l}{A}\right) = \frac{1}{4} R_1 = \frac{1}{4}(4) = \mathbf{1\ \Omega}$.

Activities 11.4 & 11.5 Experimental Proofs

Consider three resistors $R_1, R_2, R_3$ joined end-to-end (Fig 11.6):

  • Activity 11.4 (Uniform Current): Shifting the ammeter to any position between the resistors gives the exact same reading! In a series circuit, current $I$ is constant through every resistor.
  • Activity 11.5 (Voltage Addition): Measuring voltmeter readings across individual resistors gives $V_1, V_2, V_3$. Measuring across the entire chain gives $V$:
    $$V = V_1 + V_2 + V_3$$

Mathematical Derivation of Equivalent Resistance

Applying Ohm's law ($V = IR$) to each individual resistor:

$$V_1 = I R_1, \quad V_2 = I R_2, \quad V_3 = I R_3$$

For the equivalent single resistor $R_s$ with total voltage $V = I R_s$:

$$I R_s = I R_1 + I R_2 + I R_3 \implies R_s = R_1 + R_2 + R_3$$

  • Key Principle: When several resistors are joined in series, the equivalent resistance $R_s$ equals the sum of their individual resistances and is therefore greater than any individual resistance in the group!

Example 11.7: Lamp & Conductor in Series

An electric lamp ($R_1 = 20\ \Omega$) and conductor ($R_2 = 4\ \Omega$) connected to a $6\text{ V}$ battery (Fig 11.9):

  • (a) Total Resistance: $R_s = R_1 + R_2 = 20 + 4 = \mathbf{24\ \Omega}$.
  • (b) Circuit Current: $I = \frac{V}{R_s} = \frac{6\text{ V}}{24\ \Omega} = \mathbf{0.25\text{ A}}$.
  • (c) Potential Differences:
    • Across lamp: $V_1 = I R_1 = 0.25\text{ A} \times 20\ \Omega = \mathbf{5\text{ V}}$.
    • Across conductor: $V_2 = I R_2 = 0.25\text{ A} \times 4\ \Omega = \mathbf{1\text{ V}}$.
    Check: $V_1 + V_2 = 5\text{ V} + 1\text{ V} = 6\text{ V}$ (Matches battery voltage!).

Why Series Wiring Fails in Household Circuits

  • Single Fault Breakdown: In a series circuit, if any single appliance fuses or is switched off, the entire circuit is broken and none of the components work!
  • Fairy Lights Nightmare: In decorative festival lights, an electrician spends hours locating the single fused bulb because every bulb must be tested individually.
  • Differing Current Demands: An electric bulb needs $\approx 0.18\text{ A}$, while an electric heater requires $\approx 2.2\text{ A}$ (Example 11.3). In series, forcing the same current through both makes it impossible for either to operate properly!
  • Cumulative Resistance: Adding devices increases $R_s$, severely reducing circuit current and power.

Resistors in Parallel: Derivation & Current Branching

Activity 11.6 Experimental Observations

Three resistors $R_1, R_2, R_3$ joined across points $X$ and $Y$ (Fig 11.10 & 11.11):

  • Uniform Potential Difference: A voltmeter connected across any individual resistor reads the exact same voltage $V$ (equal to the battery potential).
  • Current Division: Inserting ammeters in each branch reveals currents $I_1, I_2, I_3$. The total main line current $I$ equals their sum:
    $$I = I_1 + I_2 + I_3$$

Derivation of Equivalent Parallel Resistance (Rp)

Applying Ohm's law ($I = V/R$) to each parallel branch:

$$I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1}, \quad I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2}, \quad I_3 = \frac{V}{R_3}$$

For the equivalent parallel resistor $R_p$, main line current $I = V / R_p$:

$$\frac{V}{R_p} = \frac{V}{R_1} + \frac{V}{R_2} + \frac{V}{R_3} \implies \mathbf{\frac{1}{R_p} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}}$$

  • Key Principle: The reciprocal of equivalent resistance equals the sum of the reciprocals of individual resistances.
  • Crucial Theorem: $R_p$ is always less than the smallest individual resistance in the combination!

  • 1. Independent Operation: Each gadget has its own switch; turning off or failure of one appliance does not disrupt any other.
  • 2. Full Mains Voltage (220 V): Every appliance receives the full rated line voltage across its terminals.
  • 3. Appropriate Current Allocation: Different appliances (low current bulbs vs high current heaters) automatically draw current suited to their individual resistances.
  • 4. Lower Total Resistance: Parallel equivalent resistance is small, reducing unwanted line power dissipation.

Heating Effect of Electric Current & Joule’s Law

Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator

Derivation of the Heating Formula

Consider current $I$ flowing through a resistor $R$ across potential difference $V$ for time $t$ (Fig 11.13):

  • Work done in moving charge $Q$ across potential difference $V$ is $W = V Q$.
  • Since $Q = I t$, energy supplied by source in time $t$ is:
    $$W = V I t$$
  • Power input to the circuit is $P = W/t = V I$.
  • In a purely resistive circuit, this energy is dissipated entirely as heat ($H = W$):

$$H = V I t = (I R) I t = \mathbf{I^2 R t}$$

Joule’s Law of Heating: Three Fundamental Rules

Joule's Law of Heating states that the heat produced in a resistor is:

  1. Directly proportional to the square of current for a given resistance: $H \propto I^2$.
  2. Directly proportional to the resistance for a given current: $H \propto R$.
  3. Directly proportional to the time for which the current flows: $H \propto t$.

Example 11.11: $100\text{ J}$ heat produced each second in a $4\ \Omega$ resistor $\implies I = \sqrt{H / (Rt)} = \sqrt{100 / (4 \times 1)} = 5\text{ A} \implies V = IR = 5 \times 4 = 20\text{ V}$.

Heating Appliances & Incandescent Bulbs

  • Heating Appliances: Electric iron, toaster, oven, electric kettle, and room heaters utilize high-resistivity alloy coils (nichrome) to produce localized heat.
  • Incandescent Bulbs:
    • Tungsten Filament: Has an extraordinary melting point of $3380^\circ\text{C}$, allowing it to reach incandescence without melting.
    • Inert Gas Filling: Bulbs are filled with chemically unreactive nitrogen and argon gases to prevent filament oxidation and prolong service life.
    • Most power consumed appears as heat; only a small fraction is radiated as visible light.

The Electric Fuse: Working Principle & Rating

  • Function: A vital safety device that protects circuits and appliances by stopping the flow of unduly high current (overloads or short-circuits).
  • Connection: Always connected in series with the live wire before the appliance.
  • Mechanism: Made of a lead-tin alloy wire of suitable low melting point encased in a porcelain cartridge. When current exceeds the rated limit, $I^2Rt$ heat melts the fuse wire, breaking the circuit!
  • Ratings: Domestic fuses are rated $1\text{ A}, 2\text{ A}, 3\text{ A}, 5\text{ A}, 10\text{ A}$, etc.
  • Worked Case: A $1\text{ kW}$ iron at $220\text{ V}$ draws $I = 1000/220 = 4.54\text{ A}$. A $5\text{ A}$ fuse must be used!

Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator
Teacher's Interactive Audio Commentary

Formulas for Electric Power (P)

Power is the rate of doing electrical work ($P = W/t$):

$$P = V I = I^2 R = \frac{V^2}{R}$$

  • The Watt (W): Power consumed when $1\text{ A}$ flows at a potential difference of $1\text{ V}$ ($1\text{ W} = 1\text{ V} \times 1\text{ A} = 1\text{ V}\cdot\text{A}$).
  • Kilowatt ($1\text{ kW} = 1000\text{ W}$): Practical unit for high-power appliances.
  • Example 11.12: Bulb on $220\text{ V}$ draws $0.50\text{ A} \implies P = VI = 220 \times 0.50 = 110\text{ W}$.

Commercial Energy Unit: The Kilowatt-Hour (kWh)

Electrical energy is power multiplied by time ($E = P \times t$):

  • Commercial Unit (The 'Unit'): Kilowatt-hour (kW h).
  • Joule Conversion:
    $$1\text{ kW h} = 1000\text{ W} \times 3600\text{ s} = 3,600,000\text{ J} = \mathbf{3.6 \times 10^6\text{ J}}$$
  • More to Know: Electrons are not consumed in a circuit! We pay electricity boards for the energy needed to push electrons through our appliances.
  • Example 11.13: A $400\text{ W}$ refrigerator runs $8\text{ h/day}$ for $30\text{ days}$ $\implies E = 400 \times 8 \times 30 = 96,000\text{ Wh} = 96\text{ kWh}$. At $\text{Rs } 3.00/\text{kWh}$, cost is $96 \times 3 = \mathbf{\text{Rs } 288.00}$.

Q4: Ratio of Heat in Series vs Parallel at Same V:
$H_s = \frac{V^2 t}{R_s} = \frac{V^2 t}{2R}$; $H_p = \frac{V^2 t}{R_p} = \frac{V^2 t}{R/2} = \frac{2V^2 t}{R}$.
$\frac{H_s}{H_p} = \frac{1/2}{2} = \mathbf{1:4}$ (Option c).
