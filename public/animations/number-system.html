Natural Numbers (ℕ): Counting numbers {1, 2, 3, 4, ...}. They are the core inner subset of all whole numbers, integers, rational numbers, and real numbers: ℕ ⊂ 𝕎 ⊂ ℤ ⊂ ℚ ⊂ ℝ.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Mathematics begins with numbers. As human civilization advanced, the need for new types of numbers grew systematically to solve increasingly complex practical and algebraic problems.

1. Natural Numbers ($\mathbb{N}$)

The counting numbers: $\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\}$. Used for enumeration and ordering. Closed under addition and multiplication, but not under subtraction.

2. Whole Numbers ($\mathbb{W}$)

Natural numbers including zero: $\mathbb{W} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. Zero represents the absence of quantity or the identity element for addition ($a + 0 = a$).

3. Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$)

Whole numbers along with their negative counterparts: $\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$. From the German word 'Zahlen' (to count). Allows subtraction without limitation.

4. Rational Numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$)

Numbers expressible as $p/q$ where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ and $q \neq 0$. From the word 'Quotient' ($\mathbb{Q}$). Allows division by any non-zero number.

The Nested Containment Principle:

$\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{W} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q} \subset \mathbb{R}$

Every natural number is a whole number; every whole number is an integer; every integer is a rational number ($z = z/1$); and every rational number is a real number.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Formal Mathematical Definition:

A real number $r$ is called a Rational Number if it can be expressed in the form:

$r = \\frac{p}{q}, \\quad \\text{where } p, q \\in \\mathbb{Z} \\text{ and } q \\neq 0$
  • Numerator ($p$): Any integer (positive, zero, or negative).
  • Denominator ($q$): Any non-zero integer ($q \neq 0$).
  • Is $0$ a rational number? Yes! $0 = \\frac{0}{1} = \\frac{0}{2} = \\frac{0}{-5}$. Here $p=0$ and $q=1 \neq 0$.
Standard / Simplest Form:

A rational number $p/q$ is in simplest form if $p$ and $q$ are co-prime, meaning their Greatest Common Divisor is 1:

$\\gcd(p, q) = 1$ and $q > 0$

Example: $12/16 = (3 \\times 4)/(4 \\times 4) = 3/4$, where $\\gcd(3, 4) = 1$.

Why $q \\neq 0$? (Division by Zero):

If $\\frac{p}{0} = k$, then $p = k \\times 0 = 0$. If $p \\neq 0$, no such number $k$ can exist. If $p = 0$, any number $k$ would satisfy the equation, making it indeterminate. Thus, division by zero is strictly undefined.

Equivalent Rational Numbers:

Multiplying or dividing both numerator and denominator by the same non-zero integer $m$ yields equal values: $\\frac{p}{q} = \\frac{p \\cdot m}{q \\cdot m}$. For example: $\\frac{1}{2} = \\frac{2}{4} = \\frac{10}{20} = \\frac{-5}{-10}$. All represent the same point on the number line.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

The number line (or Cartesian line) is a visual one-dimensional continuum where zero is the origin ($O$). To plot any rational number $\\frac{p}{q}$ ($q > 0$):

Algorithmic Procedure for Plotting $\\frac{p}{q}$:

  1. Identify the Denominator ($q$): Divide every unit interval (between 0 and 1, 1 and 2, -1 and 0, etc.) into exactly $q$ equal sub-intervals.
  2. Step Size: Each sub-interval has length $\\Delta x = \\frac{1}{q}$.
  3. Count Units ($p$): Starting from the origin $0$:
    • If $p > 0$, move $p$ steps to the right.
    • If $p < 0$, move $|p|$ steps to the left.
Proper Fractions ($|p| < q$):

Fractions like $\\frac{3}{5}$ or $-\\frac{2}{3}$ always lie strictly inside the interval $(-1, 1)$. For $\\frac{3}{5}$, divide the segment between $0$ and $1$ into $5$ equal parts and mark the $3$rd point from $0$.

Improper Fractions ($|p| \\geq q$):

Convert to mixed fractions first: $\\frac{7}{3} = 2 + \\frac{1}{3}$. This tells us immediately that $\\frac{7}{3}$ lies between $2$ and $3$, exactly one-third of the way past $2$.

Fundamental Algebraic Properties

Closure, Commutativity, Associativity, Distributivity, Identity & Inverses

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"The set of rational numbers forms a rich algebraic structure known as a mathematical Field. It satisfies closure, commutativity, associativity, and distributivity under addition and multiplication. Furthermore, zero acts as the additive identity, and one is the multiplicative identity. Every non-zero rational number has a unique multiplicative inverse or reciprocal."

Interactive Lab 1.4: Algebraic Field Axioms Demonstrator
Commutative Property: Order of operands does not change the result: a + b = b + a and a × b = b × a. Subtraction and division do not hold this property.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

For any rational numbers $a, b, c \\in \\mathbb{Q}$, the following field axioms hold true:

Property Under Addition ($+$) Under Multiplication ($\\times$)
Closure $a + b \\in \\mathbb{Q}$ (Always rational) $a \\times b \\in \\mathbb{Q}$ (Always rational)
Commutative $a + b = b + a$ $a \\times b = b \\times a$
Associative $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ $(a \\times b) \\times c = a \\times (b \\times c)$
Identity Element $a + 0 = a$ ($0$ is additive identity) $a \\times 1 = a$ ($1$ is mult. identity)
Inverse Element $a + (-a) = 0$ (Additive inverse is $-a$) $a \\times \\frac{1}{a} = 1$ ($a \\neq 0$, Reciprocal)
Distributive Law $a \\times (b + c) = (a \\times b) + (a \\times c)$
Non-Properties to Remember:

Subtraction is neither commutative nor associative: $a - b \\neq b - a$, and $(a - b) - c \\neq a - (b - c)$. Division is also neither commutative nor associative, and division by zero is impossible.

Density of Rational Numbers & Proof

The Density Theorem: Between Any Two Rationals Lie Infinitely Many More

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"A profound property of rational numbers is that they are dense on the number line. Unlike integers, where there are no integers between 1 and 2, between any two rational numbers a and b, no matter how close, there exist infinitely many other rational numbers. We prove this using the Midpoint Method: the average of a and b is always strictly between them."

Interactive Lab 1.5: Rational Density Zoom Magnifier
Density Proof Principle: The midpoint (a + b)/2 is strictly between a and b. We can repeat this bisection infinitely many times, proving that infinite rational numbers reside in any interval.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

The Density Theorem for Rational Numbers:

Between any two distinct rational numbers $a$ and $b$ (with $a < b$), there exists at least one rational number $c$ such that $a < c < b$. Consequently, there exist infinitely many rational numbers between $a$ and $b$.

Formal Mathematical Proof (The Mean / Midpoint Method):

Let $a, b \\in \\mathbb{Q}$ with $a < b$. Consider their arithmetic mean:

$m_1 = \\frac{a + b}{2}$

Step 1 (Rationality): Since $a, b \\in \\mathbb{Q}$, their sum $a+b$ is rational by closure under addition. Dividing by $2$ ($= 2/1 \\neq 0$) yields a rational number. Thus $m_1 \\in \\mathbb{Q}$.

Step 2 (Ordering): Since $a < b$:

  • Add $a$ to both sides: $a + a < a + b \\implies 2a < a + b \\implies a < \\frac{a + b}{2} = m_1$.
  • Add $b$ to both sides: $a + b < b + b \\implies a + b < 2b \\implies m_1 = \\frac{a + b}{2} < b$.

Combining both inequalities gives: $a < m_1 < b$.

Step 3 (Infinite Iteration): By repeating this procedure between $a$ and $m_1$, we find $m_2 = \\frac{a + m_1}{2}$. Continuing infinitely, we generate an unbounded infinite sequence of distinct rationals.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

To find $n$ rational numbers between two given rational numbers $a$ and $b$ ($a < b$), mathematicians use two standard computational algorithms:

Method 1: Equal Step-Size Algorithm ($d = \\frac{b - a}{n + 1}$)

This method spaces all $n$ numbers uniformly like markers on a ruler:

$d = \\frac{b - a}{n + 1}$

The $n$ required rational numbers are generated sequentially by:

$a + d, \\quad a + 2d, \\quad a + 3d, \\quad \\dots, \\quad a + nd$

Example: Find $5$ rationals between $1$ and $2$. Here $n = 5$, $d = \\frac{2 - 1}{5 + 1} = \\frac{1}{6}$. The numbers are $1+\\frac{1}{6}=\\frac{7}{6}, \\frac{8}{6}, \\frac{9}{6}, \\frac{10}{6}, \\frac{11}{6}$.

Method 2: Common Denominator Scaler (Multiply by $(n + 1)$)

  1. Ensure both numbers have a common denominator $D$.
  2. If the gap between numerators is smaller than $n$, multiply both numerator and denominator by $(n + 1)$ (or $10, 100$).
  3. Pick the consecutive numerators in between.

Example: Find $5$ rationals between $\\frac{3}{5}$ and $\\frac{4}{5}$. Multiply by $5 + 1 = 6$: $\\frac{3 \\times 6}{5 \\times 6} = \\frac{18}{30}$ and $\\frac{4 \\times 6}{5 \\times 6} = \\frac{24}{30}$. The numbers are $\\frac{19}{30}, \\frac{20}{30}, \\frac{21}{30}, \\frac{22}{30}, \\frac{23}{30}$.

"Every rational number has a decimal expansion that either terminates or repeats periodically. A decimal terminates when the remainder in long division becomes zero after a finite number of steps. There is a beautiful theorem in number theory: a rational number in simplest form terminates if and only if the prime factorisation of its denominator q contains only powers of 2, powers of 5, or both."

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

When we divide the numerator $p$ by the denominator $q$ using standard long division, two distinct cases can occur with the sequence of remainders:

Case 1: The Remainder Becomes Zero (Terminating Decimals)

After a finite number of division steps, the remainder becomes zero. The decimal expansion stops (terminates).

$\\frac{7}{8} = 0.875$
$\\frac{1}{2} = 0.5$
$\\frac{639}{250} = 2.556$

The Fundamental Denominator Factorisation Theorem:

Let $x = \\frac{p}{q}$ be a rational number in simplest form ($\\gcd(p, q) = 1$). Then $x$ has a terminating decimal expansion if and only if the prime factorisation of $q$ is of the form:

$q = 2^m \\times 5^n, \\quad \\text{where } m, n \\in \\mathbb{W} \\text{ (non-negative integers)}$

Why? Because our number system is base 10, and $10 = 2 \\times 5$. Any terminating fraction can be converted into an equivalent fraction with a power of 10 in the denominator: $\\frac{p}{2^m 5^n} = \\frac{p \\cdot 2^{n} 5^m}{10^{\\max(m,n)}}$.

Non-Terminating Recurring Decimals

Pigeonhole Principle in Division, Period Length, and Repetend Notation

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"When the remainder never becomes zero, what happens? Because the divisor is q, the only possible remainders are 1, 2, up to q minus 1. By the Pigeonhole Principle, after at most q minus 1 steps, a remainder MUST repeat. Once a remainder repeats, the entire quotient cycle repeats forever! This produces a non-terminating recurring decimal."

Interactive Lab 1.8: Long Division Remainder Loop Engine ($1/7$)
Pigeonhole Principle Demonstration: Dividing by 7 allows only 6 non-zero remainders {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}. Once a remainder repeats, the quotient repeats forever!

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Case 2: The Remainder Never Becomes Zero (Repeating Decimals)

If the remainder never reaches zero, a repeating pattern of remainders emerges. This yields a non-terminating recurring (repeating) decimal expansion.

$\\frac{1}{3} = 0.333\\dots = 0.\\bar{3}$
$\\frac{1}{7} = 0.\\overline{142857}$
$\\frac{7}{11} = 0.\\overline{63}$

Mathematical Terminology:

  • Period: The repeating block of digits. For $0.\\overline{142857}$, the period is $142857$.
  • Periodicity / Length of Period: The number of digits in the repeating block. For $1/7$, periodicity is $6$. For $1/3$, periodicity is $1$.
  • Repetend / Vinculum Bar: A horizontal line drawn over the repeating block: $0.\\bar{6}$ means $0.6666\\dots$.
The Pigeonhole Principle Constraint:

When dividing by an integer $q$, the only possible non-zero remainders are $\{1, 2, 3, \\dots, q - 1\}$. There are at most $q - 1$ distinct remainders. Therefore, the repeating block of digits in $\\frac{p}{q}$ can never have more than $(q - 1)$ digits! For example, $1/7$ has a period of at most $7 - 1 = 6$ digits.

Converting Recurring Decimals to p/q Form

Step-by-Step Algebraic Algorithm: The 10^k Scaling & Subtraction Technique

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"Since all recurring decimals represent rational numbers, we must be able to convert any repeating decimal back into p over q form. We use an elegant algebraic algorithm. We define x as the recurring decimal, multiply x by ten to the power of k where k is the number of repeating digits, and subtract the original equation. The infinite repeating tails cancel out perfectly!"

Interactive Lab 1.9: Recurring Decimal to $p/q$ Algebraic Solver
Step 1: Let x = 0.3333... (Equation 1)
Step 2: Multiply by 10¹ = 10 ➔ 10x = 3.3333... (Equation 2)
Step 3: Subtract Eq (1) from Eq (2) ➔ 10x - x = 3.333... - 0.333...
Result: 9x = 3 ➔ x = 3/9 = 1/3 (Rational!)
Algebraic Principle: Multiplying by 10^k perfectly shifts one cycle to the left of the decimal, allowing the infinite tails to cancel out!

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Every recurring decimal can be converted back to an exact rational fraction $\\frac{p}{q}$. Here is the step-by-step universal algorithm:

Universal Algebraic Conversion Algorithm:

  1. Step 1: Let $x$ equal the given decimal: $x = 0.\\overline{a_1 a_2 \\dots a_k} \\quad \\text{--- (Equation 1)}$.
  2. Step 2: Count the number of repeating digits ($k$).
  3. Step 3: Multiply Equation (1) by $10^k$ to shift one full period to the left of the decimal point:

    $10^k x = a_1 a_2 \\dots a_k . \\overline{a_1 a_2 \\dots a_k} \\quad \\text{--- (Equation 2)}$

  4. Step 4: Subtract Equation (1) from Equation (2). The infinite tails cancel:

    $(10^k - 1) x = a_1 a_2 \\dots a_k$

  5. Step 5: Solve for $x$ and reduce to simplest co-prime form: $x = \\frac{a_1 a_2 \\dots a_k}{10^k - 1}$.
Example A (Pure Recurring): $0.\\bar{3}$

Let $x = 0.3333\\dots$
$10x = 3.3333\\dots$
Subtract: $10x - x = 3 \\implies 9x = 3 \\implies x = 3/9 = \\mathbf{1/3}$.

Example B (Mixed Recurring): $0.2\\bar{7}$

Let $x = 0.2777\\dots$
$10x = 2.777\\dots$ and $100x = 27.777\\dots$
Subtract: $100x - 10x = 25 \\implies 90x = 25 \\implies x = 25/90 = \\mathbf{5/18}$.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Formal Definition of Irrational Numbers ($\mathbb{I}$ or $\mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$):

A real number $s$ is called Irrational if it cannot be written in the form $\\frac{p}{q}$, where $p$ and $q$ are integers and $q \\neq 0$.

Decimal Characterisation: Non-Terminating and Non-Recurring
Famous Examples:
  • $\\sqrt{2} = 1.41421356237\\dots$ (Never repeats, never ends)
  • $\\sqrt{3} = 1.73205080756\\dots$
  • $\\pi = 3.141592653589793\\dots$
  • $0.101001000100001\\dots$ (Constructed pattern)
The $\\pi$ vs $\\frac{22}{7}$ Distinction:

$\\pi$ is irrational because its exact circumference-to-diameter ratio cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers. The fraction $\\frac{22}{7} = 3.\\overline{142857}$ is a rational approximation used for practical arithmetic, but $\\pi \\neq \\frac{22}{7}$.

The Real Number Continuum ($\mathbb{R}$):

The set of all Rational numbers and Irrational numbers taken together forms the complete set of Real Numbers ($\mathbb{R} = \mathbb{Q} \cup \mathbb{I}$). Every real number corresponds to a unique point on the number line, and every point represents a unique real number (The Cantor-Dedekind Axiom).

Rigorous Proof of Irrationality of √2

Proof by Contradiction, Euclid's Divisibility Lemma, and Parity Argument

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"One of the crowned jewels of ancient mathematics is the proof that the square root of two is irrational. We use Proof by Contradiction. We assume the opposite: suppose root two is rational and can be written as a over b in simplest co-prime form. Through algebraic squaring, we discover that both a and b must be even numbers. But if both are even, they share a common factor of two! This contradicts our assumption that they are co-prime."

Interactive Lab 1.11: Proof Tree for Irrationality of $\sqrt{2}$
STEP 1 Assume $\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}$
gcd(a, b) = 1
STEP 2 $a^2 = 2b^2$
2 divides $a$ (even)
STEP 3 $b^2 = 2k^2$
2 divides $b$ (even)
CONCLUSION CONTRADICTION!
$\sqrt{2} \notin \mathbb{Q}$ (Irrational)
Logical Deduction: We assume √2 is rational and can be written in irreducible co-prime form gcd(a, b) = 1. Click 'Next Logical Step' to advance the proof.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Theorem: $\\sqrt{2}$ is an Irrational Number.

We prove this using Reductio ad Absurdum (Proof by Contradiction).

Assumption: Assume to the contrary that $\\sqrt{2}$ is rational. Then there exist integers $a$ and $b$ ($b \\neq 0$) such that:

$\\sqrt{2} = \\frac{a}{b}, \\quad \\text{where } a, b \\in \\mathbb{Z} \\text{ and } \\gcd(a, b) = 1 \\text{ (co-prime)}$

Step 1: Square both sides:

$2 = \\frac{a^2}{b^2} \\implies a^2 = 2b^2 \\quad \\text{--- (Equation 1)}$

Since $2$ divides $2b^2$, it follows that $2$ divides $a^2$. By Euclid's Lemma, if a prime divides $a^2$, it must divide $a$. Thus, $a$ is an even integer.

Step 2: Since $a$ is even, we can write $a = 2k$ for some integer $k$. Substitute $a = 2k$ into Equation (1):

$(2k)^2 = 2b^2 \\implies 4k^2 = 2b^2 \\implies b^2 = 2k^2 \\quad \\text{--- (Equation 2)}$

This means $2$ divides $b^2$, which implies $b$ is also an even integer.

Step 3 (The Contradiction):

Both $a$ and $b$ are even, which means they have at least $2$ as a common factor. This directly contradicts our foundational premise that $\\gcd(a, b) = 1$ (co-prime)!

Conclusion: Our initial assumption that $\\sqrt{2}$ is rational must be false. Therefore, $\\mathbf{\\sqrt{2}}$ is irrational. Q.E.D.

Rigorous Proof of Irrationality of √3

Modulo Arithmetic, Divisibility by 3, and Generalisation to Non-Square Primes

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"In the same manner, we can prove that root three is irrational. If root three were equal to a over b, then three times b squared equals a squared. This proves that three divides a. Substituting a equals three k shows that three also divides b. Once again, a and b share a common factor of three, violating co-primality. In fact, the square root of ANY positive integer that is not a perfect square is ALWAYS irrational!"

Interactive Lab 1.12: Proof Tree for Irrationality of $\sqrt{3}$
STEP 1 Assume $\sqrt{3} = \frac{a}{b}$
gcd(a, b) = 1
STEP 2 $a^2 = 3b^2$
3 divides $a$ ($a=3m$)
STEP 3 $b^2 = 3m^2$
3 divides $b$
CONCLUSION CONTRADICTION!
$\sqrt{3} \notin \mathbb{Q}$ (Irrational)
Generalised Non-Square Rule: Since 3 is not a perfect square, √3 is irrational. If a² = 3b², 3 divides a. Substituting a = 3m shows 3 divides b, violating co-primality.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Theorem: $\\sqrt{3}$ is an Irrational Number.

We extend the method of contradiction to prime $p = 3$.

Assumption: Suppose $\\sqrt{3}$ is rational: $\\sqrt{3} = \\frac{a}{b}$, where $a, b \\in \\mathbb{Z}$, $b \\neq 0$, and $\\gcd(a, b) = 1$.

Step 1: Squaring both sides yields:

$3 = \\frac{a^2}{b^2} \\implies a^2 = 3b^2 \\quad \\text{--- (Equation 1)}$

Thus $3$ divides $a^2$. Since $3$ is prime, $3$ divides $a$ ($a$ is a multiple of $3$).

Step 2: Substitute $a = 3m$ ($m \\in \\mathbb{Z}$) into Equation (1):

$(3m)^2 = 3b^2 \\implies 9m^2 = 3b^2 \\implies b^2 = 3m^2 \\quad \\text{--- (Equation 2)}$

Thus $3$ divides $b^2$, which implies $3$ divides $b$.

Step 3 (Contradiction & Conclusion):

Both $a$ and $b$ are multiples of $3$, contradicting that $\\gcd(a, b) = 1$. Thus, $\\mathbf{\\sqrt{3}}$ is irrational.

Generalised Principle for Square Roots:

For any positive integer $n$:

$\\sqrt{n} \\text{ is Rational } \\iff n \\text{ is a Perfect Square } (1, 4, 9, 16, 25, \\dots)

If $n$ is not a perfect square (such as $2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 11$), then $\\sqrt{n}$ is strictly irrational.

Plotting Irrational Numbers on the Number Line

Pythagorean Theorem Construction: Locating √2, √3, and √5 with Compass & Ruler

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"How can we pinpoint the exact position of an irrational number on a number line if its decimal never ends? We use geometry! By applying the Pythagorean Theorem, a right-angled triangle with base of 1 unit and perpendicular height of 1 unit has a hypotenuse of root 2. Using a compass with its needle at zero and pencil at the hypotenuse, we swing an arc down to the number line to plot the exact point root 2."

Interactive Lab 1.13: Compass Arc Construction for $\sqrt{2}$
Pythagorean Construction: Right triangle with base 1 and height 1 produces hypotenuse √2. Swinging compass centered at origin transfers this exact length onto the number line.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

The Pythagorean Theorem provides the bridge connecting irrational square roots to Euclidean geometry: in any right triangle with legs $a$ and $b$, the hypotenuse $c$ satisfies $c = \\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.

Step-by-Step Construction for $\\sqrt{2}$:

  1. Step 1: On the number line, mark origin $O(0)$ and point $A(1)$. The length $OA = 1$ unit.
  2. Step 2: At $A$, construct a perpendicular segment $AB = 1$ unit, perpendicular to the number line ($AB \\perp OA$).
  3. Step 3: Join $OB$. By Pythagoras Theorem in right $\\triangle OAB$:

    $OB = \\sqrt{OA^2 + AB^2} = \\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \\sqrt{2} \\approx 1.414$

  4. Step 4: Using a compass centered at $O(0)$ with radius $OB = \\sqrt{2}$, draw an arc intersecting the number line at point $P$. The point $P$ represents $\\mathbf{\\sqrt{2}}$.
Constructing $\\sqrt{3}$ from $\\sqrt{2}$:

At point $B$ on hypotenuse $OB$ (length $\\sqrt{2}$), erect a perpendicular of length $1$ unit to point $C$. The new hypotenuse is $OC = \\sqrt{(\\sqrt{2})^2 + 1^2} = \\sqrt{2 + 1} = \\sqrt{3} \\approx 1.732$.

Direct Construction for $\\sqrt{5}$:

Take base $OA = 2$ units and vertical height $AB = 1$ unit. Then hypotenuse $OB = \\sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \\sqrt{4 + 1} = \\sqrt{5} \\approx 2.236$.

The Square Root Spiral (Theodorus Spiral)

Algorithmic Iteration: Chaining Hypotenuse √n with Unit Perpendiculars

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"The Greek philosopher Theodorus of Cyrene created a breathtaking geometric construction known as the Square Root Spiral. Starting with an isosceles right triangle of legs 1 and 1, the hypotenuse is root 2. At its tip, we attach another perpendicular of 1 unit to produce root 3, then root 4, root 5, and so on. As you continue, it coils like a nautilus seashell, visualizing all square roots sequentially."

Interactive Lab 1.14: Theodorus Square Root Spiral Generator
Theodorus Spiral Construction: Currently showing roots up to √2. Each new right triangle takes the previous hypotenuse as base and adds a perpendicular of 1 unit!

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

The Theodorus Recursive Algorithm:

The Square Root Spiral is an algorithmic sequence of contiguous right-angled triangles sharing a common vertex at the origin $O$:

$H_{n+1} = \\sqrt{(H_n)^2 + 1^2} = \\sqrt{n + 1}$

Recursive Step-by-Step Construction:

  • Triangle 1: Base $1$, Height $1 \\implies \\text{Hypotenuse } H_1 = \\sqrt{1^2 + 1^2} = \\mathbf{\\sqrt{2}}$.
  • Triangle 2: Base $\\sqrt{2}$, Height $1 \\implies \\text{Hypotenuse } H_2 = \\sqrt{(\\sqrt{2})^2 + 1^2} = \\mathbf{\\sqrt{3}}$.
  • Triangle 3: Base $\\sqrt{3}$, Height $1 \\implies \\text{Hypotenuse } H_3 = \\sqrt{(\\sqrt{3})^2 + 1^2} = \\sqrt{4} = \\mathbf{2}$.
  • Triangle 4: Base $\\sqrt{4} = 2$, Height $1 \\implies \\text{Hypotenuse } H_4 = \\sqrt{2^2 + 1^2} = \\mathbf{\\sqrt{5}}$.
  • Triangle $n$: Base $\\sqrt{n}$, Height $1 \\implies \\text{Hypotenuse } H_n = \\mathbf{\\sqrt{n + 1}}$.
Historical Milestone:

Theodorus constructed this spiral up to $\\sqrt{17}$, where the 16th triangle completes almost a full $360^\\circ$ rotation without overlapping the starting segment. It vividly proves that every square root $\\sqrt{n}$ is a constructible Euclidean segment!

Interactive Lab 1.15: Conjugate Denominator Rationaliser
Original Fraction: 1 / (√7 - 2)
Multiply Numerator & Denominator by Conjugate (√7 + 2):
[1 × (√7 + 2)] / [(√7 - 2)(√7 + 2)]
Apply Difference of Squares: (√7)² - 2² = 7 - 4 = 3
Rationalised Result: (√7 + 2) / 3
Rationalisation Rule: Using conjugate (a ∓ √b) turns the irrational denominator into an integer using (x+y)(x-y) = x² - y².

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Rules of Mixed Arithmetic Operations:

  • Rational + Irrational = Irrational: $2 + \\sqrt{3}$ is irrational.
  • Non-zero Rational $\\times$ Irrational = Irrational: $5\\sqrt{2}$ is irrational.
  • Irrational $\\pm / \\times / \\div$ Irrational: Can be rational or irrational!

    $\\sqrt{2} \\times \\sqrt{2} = 2$ (Rational), but $\\sqrt{2} \\times \\sqrt{3} = \\sqrt{6}$ (Irrational).

Rationalising the Denominator:

Standard mathematical convention requires removing square roots from denominators because division by an irrational decimal is computationally inefficient.

Type 1 (Monomial): $\\frac{1}{\\sqrt{a}}$

Multiply numerator & denominator by $\\sqrt{a}$:

$\\frac{1}{\\sqrt{a}} \\times \\frac{\\sqrt{a}}{\\sqrt{a}} = \\frac{\\sqrt{a}}{a}$

Type 2 (Binomial Conjugate): $\\frac{1}{a + \\sqrt{b}}$

Multiply by the conjugate $(a - \\sqrt{b})$ using difference of squares $(x+y)(x-y) = x^2 - y^2$:

$\\frac{1}{a + \\sqrt{b}} \\times \\frac{a - \\sqrt{b}}{a - \\sqrt{b}} = \\frac{a - \\sqrt{b}}{a^2 - b}$

Laws of Exponents for Real Numbers

Index Laws, Fractional Exponents (a^(p/q) = q√a^p), and Algorithmic Thinking

Unit 1 • Number System (12 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Speech & Concepts)

"In our final content slide, we consolidate the Laws of Exponents for positive real bases. Whether exponents are integers, fractions, or negative numbers, the fundamental laws of powers remain invariant. We also establish computational thinking skills: recognizing that calculating cube roots and fractional exponents follows deterministic algebraic algorithms."

Interactive Lab 1.16: Laws of Exponents Algorithmic Engine
Law 1: Product with Same Base: a^m × a^n = a^(m + n)
Example: 2^(2/3) × 2^(1/3) = 2^(2/3 + 1/3) = 2^(3/3)
Simplified Evaluated Value: 2¹ = 2
Computational Thinking: Exponent rules allow logarithmic reduction of computational complexity from exponential products to simple addition of fractions.

Curriculum Concepts, Proofs & Computational Thinking

Let $a, b > 0$ be real bases and $p, q, m, n \\in \\mathbb{Q}$ be rational exponents. The universal laws of exponents are:

1. Product with Same Base:

$a^m \\cdot a^n = a^{m + n}$

Example: $2^{2/3} \\cdot 2^{1/3} = 2^{2/3 + 1/3} = 2^1 = 2$.

2. Quotient with Same Base:

$\\frac{a^m}{a^n} = a^{m - n}$

Example: $7^{1/5} / 7^{1/3} = 7^{1/5 - 1/3} = 7^{-2/15}$.

3. Power of a Power:

$(a^m)^n = a^{m \\cdot n}$

Example: $(3^{1/4})^4 = 3^{(1/4 \\times 4)} = 3^1 = 3$.

4. Different Base, Same Exponent:

$a^m \\cdot b^m = (ab)^m$

Example: $13^{1/5} \\cdot 17^{1/5} = (13 \\times 17)^{1/5} = 221^{1/5}$.

Definition of Fractional Exponents (Radicals):

$a^{m/n} = (\\sqrt[n]{a})^m = \\sqrt[n]{a^m}, \\quad a^{-m/n} = \\frac{1}{a^{m/n}}$

Example: Evaluate $64^{1/2} = \\sqrt{64} = 8$, and $32^{2/5} = (\\sqrt[5]{32})^2 = 2^2 = 4$.
