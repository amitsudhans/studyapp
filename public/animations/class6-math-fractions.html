Equal Shares

Quantifying Fair Portions from a Shared Whole

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Fractions represent equal shares of a whole. When 1 item is divided into n equal pieces, each piece is 1/n.

When a single whole item (like a round roti or a candy bar) is divided equally among multiple people, whole numbers (0, 1, 2, 3...) cannot describe the size of each share. A fraction quantifies equal portions of a whole.

1 roti divided equally between 2 children = 1/2 roti each ("one half").
1 roti divided equally among 4 children = 1/4 roti each ("one quarter").
General Rule: 1 whole divided into n equal parts produces fractional units of 1/n.

A fraction strictly requires that all parts are exactly equal in size. If a roti is split into two unequal pieces, neither piece is truly 1/2.

Comparing Unit Fractions

As Denominator Increases, Share Size Decreases

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
As the denominator of a unit fraction grows, the size of each slice shrinks: 1/2 is much bigger than 1/10.

A unit fraction has a numerator of 1 (such as 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, 1/5, ..., 1/100). The bottom number (the denominator) indicates how many equal parts the whole is partitioned into.

The Sharing Principle:

• Sharing 1 cake among 2 people gives huge slices: 1/2.

• Sharing 1 cake among 5 people gives smaller slices: 1/5.

• Sharing 1 cake among 9 people gives even tinier slices: 1/9.

1/2 > 1/3 > 1/4 > 1/5 > 1/6 > ... > 1/100

Ancient Knowledge: In the Rig Veda, 3/4 was called 'tri-pada', reflecting modern Indian terms like 'teen paav' (three quarters) or 'mukkaal'.

Parts of a Whole

Combining Unit Fractions to Form Non-Unit Quantities

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Combining multiple unit fractions forms general fractions. 3 pieces of 1/4 equal 3/4 of the whole.

When you gather multiple unit fractions together, you build general fractions. For example, if you take 3 pieces of a chikki bar that was cut into 4 equal quarters, you possess 3/4 of the chikki.

1/4 + 1/4 + 1/4 = 3/4 ("three quarters" or "three fourths")

• The whole contains 4 equal units (4/4 = 1 whole).
• The selected portion contains 3 of those units.
• The unshaded remaining part is 1/4, because 3/4 + 1/4 = 4/4 = 1.

A fraction represents two distinct numbers: the count of parts chosen (3) and the total equal parts making up the whole (4).

Different Shapes, Same Share

Equal Fractional Area in Diverse Geometric Cuts

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Equal shares can have different visual shapes. A square cut into 4 strips, 4 corner squares, or 4 triangles each has pieces of area 1/4.

Equal shares do not need to look identical in shape to represent the same fraction! As long as each piece covers the exact same area, it is a valid equal share.

Ways to cut a square into 4 equal quarters (1/4 each):

4 Vertical Columns
(Stripes)
2×2 Square Grid
(Four Corners)
2 Diagonals
(Four Triangles)

All three partitioning methods yield pieces with an area of exactly 1/4 of the original square, despite their differing shapes.

Conservation of Area: A triangular quarter and a rectangular quarter have different perimeters, but represent the exact same fraction (1/4) of the square's area.

Paper Strip Folding

Generating Halves, Quarters, Eighths & Thirds by Folding

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Folding paper strips repeatedly in half generates powers-of-two fractions: 1/2, 1/4, 1/8. Folding into thirds yields 1/3 and 1/6.

Paper folding provides a tactile and visual proof of how fractional units arise naturally:

• 1st Fold in Half: Divides the strip into 2 equal parts. Crease marks 1/2.
• 2nd Fold in Half: Doubling the creases creates 4 equal parts: 1/4, 2/4, 3/4, 4/4.
• 3rd Fold in Half: Doubling again creates 8 equal parts: 1/8, 2/8, 3/8, ..., 8/8.
• Folding into Thirds: Creating an S-shape or Z-fold produces 3 equal parts (1/3, 2/3), and folding each in half produces sixths (1/6).

Every fold in half multiplies the total number of parts by 2, halving the width of each fractional unit: 1/2 → 1/4 → 1/8 → 1/16.

Numerator & Denominator

Anatomy of a Fraction: Count vs Type

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Numerator counts the units taken; denominator names how many parts make up 1 whole. 5 times 1/2 is 5/2.

Any fraction can be understood as a repeated count of a unit fraction. For example, 5 times 1/2 equals:

1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 + 1/2 = 5/2 ("five halves")
Numerator The top number. It counts how many fractional units are being considered (from Latin numerator, "numberer/counter").
Denominator The bottom number. It names the size or type of the unit by telling how many parts make 1 whole (from Latin nomen, "namer").

In 5/2: The denominator 2 tells us the unit is 'halves'. The numerator 5 tells us we have 5 of those halves.

Fractions on Number Line

Mapping Fractional Distances Between Whole Integers

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
To plot a/b on the number line, partition each unit gap into b equal segments and step a times from 0.

Just like whole numbers 0, 1, 2, 3 occupy fixed points on a number line, fractions represent precise lengths and locations along that continuous line:

Protocol for plotting a fraction a/b:

1. Look at the denominator b. Divide every unit interval (0 to 1, 1 to 2) into b equal segments.

2. Each segment step has length 1/b.

3. Starting from 0, count a steps to the right. The a-th tick mark marks the location of a/b.

Example: To mark 3/5, divide the interval from 0 to 1 into 5 equal parts: 1/5, 2/5, 3/5, 4/5, 5/5 (= 1). 3/5 is at the third tick mark.

Density of Fractions

Infinitely Many Fractions Exist Between Any Two Numbers

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Fractions are infinitely dense. Between any two distinct fractions, there exist an infinite number of other fractions.

Unlike integers where there is no whole number between 2 and 3, fractions are infinitely dense. Between ANY two fractions on the number line, you can always find another fraction!

Zooming between 0 and 1:

• Halving gives: 1/2.

• Halving between 0 and 1/2 gives: 1/4.

• Halving between 0 and 1/4 gives: 1/8, 1/16, 1/32, 1/64, ...

No matter how close two fractions are, their midpoint (their average) is a new fraction strictly between them.

There are infinitely many fractions between 0 and 1, between 0 and 1/100, and even between 99/100 and 1.

Proper vs Improper

Fractions Less Than One vs Fractions Greater Than One

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Proper fractions have numerator < denominator and value < 1. Improper fractions have numerator > denominator and value > 1.

Depending on whether a fraction represents less than, equal to, or more than one whole, mathematicians categorize it into distinct families:

Proper Fraction: Numerator < Denominator (Value < 1).
Examples: 1/2, 3/4, 5/6, 99/100. It is genuinely a "part" of a single whole.
Fraction Equal to One: Numerator = Denominator (Value = 1).
Examples: 2/2 = 1, 4/4 = 1, 7/7 = 1.
Improper Fraction: Numerator > Denominator (Value > 1).
Examples: 5/2, 7/4, 9/5. It represents more than one complete whole.

Historically, the word 'fraction' meant broken piece (less than 1). When quantities exceeded 1, early mathematicians labeled them 'improper'.

Mixed Numbers

Combining Whole Integers with Proper Fractional Parts

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Mixed numbers combine a whole number and a fraction: 9/2 = 4 1/2. Conversely, 3 3/4 = (3×4+3)/4 = 15/4.

When you have more than 1 whole, an improper fraction can be rewritten as a mixed fraction (or mixed number), showing whole units and remaining parts:

Converting Improper to Mixed (e.g., 9/2):

• Divide 9 by 2: 9 ÷ 2 = 4 with a remainder of 1.
• Whole part = 4, fractional part = 1/2.
• Therefore, 9/2 = 4 1/2 ("four and a half").

Converting Mixed to Improper (e.g., 3 3/4):

• 3 wholes have 3 × 4 = 12 quarters.
• Add the 3 remaining quarters: 12 + 3 = 15 quarters.
• Formula: 3 3/4 = (3 × 4 + 3) / 4 = 15/4.

Mixed numbers are intuitive in everyday speech: "I ran 2 1/2 kilometers" or "I bought 1 3/4 kg of apples".

Equivalent Fractions

Scaling Numerator & Denominator by the Same Factor

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Equivalent fractions share the same value. Multiplying or dividing top and bottom by k preserves the value: (a×k)/(b×k) = a/b.

Two fractions are equivalent if they represent the exact same value or share.

Real-World Roti Sharing:

• 1 roti shared by 2 children = 1/2 roti each.
• 2 rotis shared by 4 children = 2/4 = 1/2 roti each.
• 3 rotis shared by 6 children = 3/6 = 1/2 roti each.

The Golden Rule of Equivalence:

Multiplying or dividing both the numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number k does not change the fraction's value:

(a × k) / (b × k) = a / b

Example: 2/3 = (2×4)/(3×4) = 8/12. Cutting each third into 4 sub-pieces yields 8 twelfths.

Simplest Form

Cancelling Common Factors to Reach Lowest Terms

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
A fraction is in simplest form when numerator and denominator have HCF = 1. Divide both by their HCF to reduce in one step.

A fraction is in its simplest form (or lowest terms) when its numerator and denominator share NO common factor other than 1 (meaning they are co-prime).

Two Methods to Simplify (e.g., 24/36):

• Step-by-Step Division: Divide top and bottom by common factors:
  24/36 ÷ 2 = 12/18 ÷ 2 = 6/9 ÷ 3 = 2/3.
• Direct HCF Division: Find HCF(24, 36) = 12.
  Divide directly by 12: (24÷12) / (36÷12) = 2/3.

Once a fraction is in simplest form, it cannot be reduced any further without introducing non-integer decimals.

Comparing Fractions

Common Denominators & Cross-Multiplication

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
To compare fractions, convert them to a common denominator using the LCM, or cross-multiply a×d vs b×c.

To compare two fractions with different denominators (like 3/4 and 5/6), you cannot simply look at the numbers. You must express them in the same fractional unit.

Method 1: Common Denominator (LCM):

• Denominators are 4 and 6. LCM(4, 6) = 12.
• Convert 3/4 = (3×3)/(4×3) = 9/12.
• Convert 5/6 = (5×2)/(6×2) = 10/12.
• Since 10/12 > 9/12, we conclude 5/6 > 3/4.

Method 2: Cross-Multiplication Shortcut:

Compare a/b with c/d by computing (a × d) vs (b × c):
3 × 6 = 18 vs 4 × 5 = 20. Since 18 < 20, 3/4 < 5/6!

This common-denominator technique also allows arranging long lists of fractions in ascending or descending order.

Adding Like Fractions

Combining Fractions with Identical Denominators

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
For like fractions, add or subtract numerators and keep the denominator: a/d + b/d = (a+b)/d. Never add denominators!

When fractions have the same denominator, they share the identical fractional unit. You simply add or subtract the numerators while keeping the denominator unchanged:

a/d + b/d = (a + b) / d    |    a/d − b/d = (a − b) / d

• Addition Example: 2/5 + 1/5 = (2 + 1) / 5 = 3/5.
• Subtraction Example: 6/7 − 4/7 = (6 − 4) / 7 = 2/7.

Common Trap: NEVER add the denominators! 1/4 + 1/4 is 2/4 (which is 1/2), NOT 2/8. Adding denominators shrinks the unit instead of combining counts!

Think of it like objects: 2 fifths + 1 fifth = 3 fifths, just as 2 apples + 1 apple = 3 apples.

Brahmagupta's Method

Unlike Denominators & Ancient Fraction History

Mentors: Acharya Brahmagupta & Abu Bakr al-Hassar (Pioneers of Fractional Arithmetic & The Fraction Bar)
Brahmagupta unified fraction addition: a/b + c/d = (ad + bc)/bd. Numerators over denominators originated in India; Al-Hassar added the bar.

In 628 CE, the legendary Indian mathematician Brahmagupta formulated the universal rule for adding and subtracting fractions with unlike denominators in his treatise Brahmasphutasiddhanta:

Brahmagupta's 3-Step Algorithm:

1. Multiply cross-wise to express both fractions with a common denominator (b × d):
   a/b = (a × d) / (b × d) and c/d = (c × b) / (b × d).

2. Add or subtract the numerators: (a×d ± b×c) / (b×d).

3. Reduce the result to its simplest form by dividing by the common factor.

Historical Note on Notation:

Writing the numerator directly over the denominator originated in India (Bakhshali Manuscript ~300 CE). The horizontal dividing bar was introduced in the 12th century by Moroccan mathematician Abu Bakr al-Hassar.
