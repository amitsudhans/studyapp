Introduction to Linear Programming & Optimisation

Every enterprise, engineer, and decision-maker faces a universal dilemma: how to achieve the best possible outcome with strictly limited resources. This general class of challenges forms Optimisation Problems, with Linear Programming Problems (LPP) standing as its most powerful foundation.

From Class XI Inequalities to Real-World Decisions

In Class XI, we learned how to graph linear inequalities in two variables and find their common half-plane solutions. In Class XII, we apply these systems of inequalities to real-world industrial, commercial, and economic planning.

What is an Optimisation Problem?

An optimisation problem seeks to maximise (e.g., profit, efficiency, output) or minimise (e.g., production cost, time, waste, material usage) a linear quantity subject to a given set of operational constraints.

Leonid Kantorovich (1912–1986)

Linear programming was first formulated in 1941 by Russian mathematician L. Kantorovich and American economist F. L. Hitchcock for transportation economics. Kantorovich would later receive the Nobel Prize in 1975.

The Furniture Dealer Problem: Strategies & Constraints

Let us walk through the classic NCERT furniture dealer problem. Understanding how physical limitations translate into algebraic inequalities is the heart of mathematical modelling.

Resource Specifications

• Available Investment Capital: Maximum ₹ 50,000.
• Storage Capacity: Maximum 60 items total.
• Purchase Costs: Table = ₹ 2,500; Chair = ₹ 500.
• Expected Profit: Table = ₹ 250; Chair = ₹ 75.

Strategy A: Buy Tables Only

He can buy $50000 / 2500 = 20$ tables. Storage is easily satisfied ($20 \le 60$).
Total Profit: $20 \times 250 = \mathbf{₹ 5,000}$.

Strategy B: Buy Chairs Only

With ₹ 50,000, he could afford $50000 / 500 = 100$ chairs, but his warehouse holds only 60 pieces! He buys 60 chairs.
Capital used: $60 \times 500 = ₹ 30,000$.
Total Profit: $60 \times 75 = \mathbf{₹ 4,500}$.

Strategy C: Buy 10 Tables and 50 Chairs

Items = $10 + 50 = 60$ (storage fully used).
Capital = $10(2500) + 50(500) = 25000 + 25000 = ₹ 50,000$ (capital fully used).
Total Profit: $10(250) + 50(75) = 2500 + 3750 = \mathbf{₹ 6,250}$! Substantially higher!

Mathematical Formulation: Decision Variables & Constraints

To solve any optimisation problem systematically, we translate the dealer's decisions and limitations into precise mathematical inequalities in terms of variables $x$ and $y$.

1. Decision Variables

Let $x =$ number of tables purchased.
Let $y =$ number of chairs purchased.
Since physical quantities cannot be negative, we have the non-negative constraints:
$$x \ge 0, \quad y \ge 0$$

2. Investment (Capital) Constraint

Cost of $x$ tables and $y$ chairs is $2500x + 500y$. Since investment is capped at ₹ 50,000:
$$2500x + 500y \le 50000$$
Dividing by 500:
$$\mathbf{5x + y \le 100}$$

3. Storage Space Constraint

Total items cannot exceed 60 pieces:
$$\mathbf{x + y \le 60}$$

Objective Function & Formal LPP Terminology

Every Linear Programming Problem consists of two core components: an Objective Function to be optimised, and a system of Linear Constraints that define allowable decisions.

Objective Function ($Z$)

A linear function $Z = ax + by$ (where $a, b$ are real constants) which is to be maximised or minimised is called the linear objective function. For the dealer:
$$Z = 250x + 75y$$

Decision Variables

The variables $x$ and $y$ in the objective function whose values are to be determined by the decision-maker are called decision variables.

Constraints & Non-Negative Restrictions

The linear inequalities or equations restricting the decision variables are called constraints. Inequalities $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ are the non-negative restrictions.

Why 'Linear' and 'Programming'?

• Linear: All relationships involving the variables are first-degree algebraic relations.
• Programming: Refers not to coding, but to the mathematical planning or scheduling of a course of action.

Graphical Method: Half-Planes & Feasible Region

By plotting each linear constraint as a line and identifying its qualifying half-plane, the mutual overlap defines the Feasible Region where every candidate solution lives.

Plotting Boundary Lines in Quadrant I

1. For $5x + y = 100$: Intercepts are $(20, 0)$ and $(0, 100)$. Testing $(0,0)$ gives $0 \le 100$ (True). The half-plane includes origin.
2. For $x + y = 60$: Intercepts are $(60, 0)$ and $(0, 60)$. Testing $(0,0)$ gives $0 \le 60$ (True). The half-plane includes origin.
3. Non-negative constraints $x \ge 0, y \ge 0$ restrict us strictly to the first quadrant.

Intersection Point B

Subtracting $x + y = 60$ from $5x + y = 100$:
$$4x = 40 \implies x = 10$$
$$y = 60 - 10 = 50$$
Boundary intersection is point $B(10, 50)$.

Feasible Region $OABC$ & Infeasible Region

• Feasible Region: The shaded polygon $OABC$ with vertices $O(0,0), A(20,0), B(10,50), C(0,60)$.
• Feasible Solution: Any point inside or on the boundary of $OABC$ (e.g., $(10, 50), (0,60), (20,0)$).
• Infeasible Solution: Any point outside $OABC$ (e.g., $(25, 40)$, where $5(25)+40=165 > 100$).

Section 12.2.2 • The Theory Slide 6 of 21

Fundamental Theorems: Convex Polygons & Corner Points

Since the feasible polygon $OABC$ contains infinitely many points, testing every single point is impossible. Two fundamental mathematical theorems unlock a remarkably simple solution method.

Theorem 1 (Corner Point Optimality)

Let $R$ be the feasible region (convex polygon) for an LPP and let $Z = ax + by$ be the objective function. When $Z$ has an optimal value (maximum or minimum), where variables $x, y$ are subject to linear constraints, this optimal value must occur at a corner point (vertex) of the feasible region.

Theorem 2 (Bounded Existence)

Let $R$ be the feasible region for an LPP and let $Z = ax + by$. If $R$ is bounded (can be enclosed completely within a circle), then the objective function $Z$ has both a maximum and a minimum value on $R$, and each occurs at a corner point of $R$.

Corner Point & Boundedness Definitions

• Corner Point: A point in the region which is the intersection of two boundary lines.
• Convex Region: A region where the line segment joining any two points in the region lies entirely inside it.
• Unbounded Region: A region extending indefinitely in at least one direction (cannot be enclosed in any finite circle).

The Corner Point Method: Algorithm & Furniture Solution

The Corner Point Method transforms complex multi-variable optimisation into a straightforward tabular computation of vertex coordinates.

Step-by-Step Algorithm for Bounded Regions

1. Graph all linear inequalities and identify the shaded feasible region $R$.
2. Determine the coordinates of all corner points (vertices) by inspection or solving pairwise linear systems.
3. Compute the objective value $Z = ax + by$ at each vertex.
4. For a bounded region, the largest value $M$ is the maximum and smallest $m$ is the minimum.

Section 12.2.2 • Open Half-Plane Test Slide 8 of 21

Corner Point Method for Unbounded Feasible Regions

When a feasible region extends indefinitely into infinity, the smallest or largest corner value is only a candidate. We must execute the rigorous Open Half-Plane Test.

The Challenge with Unbounded Regions

In an unbounded region, values of $Z = ax + by$ could theoretically grow infinitely large ($+\infty$) or infinitely small ($-\infty$). Hence, a maximum or minimum may fail to exist.

The Open Half-Plane Test Rules

Let $M$ be the largest value of $Z$ at the corner points and $m$ be the smallest.

• Maximum Rule: $M$ is the true maximum of $Z$ if and only if the open half-plane $$ax + by > M$$ has NO point in common with the feasible region. If it has common points, $Z$ has no maximum value.

• Minimum Rule: $m$ is the true minimum of $Z$ if and only if the open half-plane $$ax + by < m$$ has NO point in common with the feasible region. If it has common points, $Z$ has no minimum value.

How to Graph the Test Inequality

Draw the boundary line $ax + by = M$ (or $m$) as a dashed line (since the inequality is strict). Shade the qualifying open half-plane. Check if any shaded test region overlaps the feasible polygon.

Example 4 Deep Dive: Unbounded Region & 'No Minimum'

Example 4 is the definitive NCERT case study of an unbounded feasible region where the apparent corner minimum turns out to be an illusion.

1. Corner Point Tabulation

The feasible region is unbounded with four corner points:
• $(0, 5) \implies Z = -50(0) + 20(5) = 100$
• $(0, 3) \implies Z = -50(0) + 20(3) = 60$
• $(1, 0) \implies Z = -50(1) + 20(0) = -50$
• $(6, 0) \implies Z = -50(6) + 20(0) = \mathbf{-300}$ (Smallest corner value)

2. Applying the Open Half-Plane Test

Since the region is unbounded, $-300$ may or may not be the true minimum. We graph the strict inequality:
$$-50x + 20y < -300$$
Dividing by 10:
$$-5x + 2y < -30$$

3. The Fatal Overlap

We check if the open half-plane $-5x + 2y < -30$ has points in common with the feasible region.
Consider the point $(9, 2)$ in the feasible region: $-5(9) + 2(2) = -45 + 4 = -41 < -30$ (Satisfied!).
Or $(8, 5)$: $-5(8)+2(5) = -30$, so points just to the right satisfy it.
Conclusion: Because the open half-plane shares points with the feasible region, $Z$ has NO minimum value!

Example 5 Deep Dive: Inconsistent Constraints & Infeasible Region

What happens when constraints contradict each other so completely that no single coordinate can satisfy them simultaneously? Example 5 introduces the Empty Feasible Region.

1. Plotting the Two Constraints

• Constraint 1: $x + y \ge 8$. The line $x + y = 8$ passes through $(8, 0)$ and $(0, 8)$. The inequality shades upwards and to the right (away from origin).
• Constraint 2: $3x + 5y \le 15$. The line $3x + 5y = 15$ passes through $(5, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$. The inequality shades downwards and to the left (towards origin).

2. No Common Overlap ($\emptyset$)

Look at the two shaded half-planes: they face in opposite directions with a wide gap between them! There is no point in the Cartesian plane that satisfies both $x + y \ge 8$ and $3x + 5y \le 15$ with $x, y \ge 0$ simultaneously.

3. The Infeasible Conclusion

Because the intersection of the constraints is the empty set $\emptyset$, there is no feasible region. Consequently, the linear programming problem has no feasible solution.

• $Z(4,0) = -3(4) + 4(0) = \mathbf{-12}$ (Minimum)
• $Z(2,3) = -6 + 12 = 6$
• $Z(0,4) = 16$
Minimum $Z = -12$ at $(4, 0)$.

Q3: Maximise $Z = 5x + 3y$ subject to $3x + 5y \le 15, 5x + 2y \le 10, x, y \ge 0$

Intersection: $19x = 20 \implies x = 20/19, y = 45/19$. Vertices: $(0,0), (2,0), (20/19, 45/19), (0,3)$.
• $Z(0,0)=0$, $Z(2,0)=10$, $Z(0,3)=9$, $Z(20/19, 45/19) = \frac{100+135}{19} = \frac{235}{19} \approx 12.37$.
Maximum $Z = 235/19$ at $(20/19, 45/19)$.

Q5: Maximise $Z = 3x + 2y$ subject to $x + 2y \le 10, 3x + y \le 15, x, y \ge 0$

Intersection: $3(10-2y)+y=15 \implies 30-5y=15 \implies y=3, x=4$. Vertices: $(0,0), (5,0), (4,3), (0,5)$.
• $Z(0,0)=0$, $Z(5,0)=15$, $Z(0,5)=10$
• $Z(4,3) = 3(4) + 2(3) = 12 + 6 = \mathbf{18}$
Maximum $Z = 18$ at $(4, 3)$.

Q4: Minimise $Z = 3x + 5y$ such that $x + 3y \ge 3, x + y \ge 2, x, y \ge 0$

Unbounded region. Intersection of $x+3y=3$ and $x+y=2$: $2y=1 \implies y=1/2, x=3/2$.
Corner points: $(3, 0), (3/2, 1/2), (0, 2)$.
• $Z(3,0) = 9$
• $Z(3/2, 1/2) = 3(1.5) + 5(0.5) = 4.5 + 2.5 = \mathbf{7}$ (Smallest)
• $Z(0,2) = 10$
Test open half-plane $3x + 5y < 7$: Does it share points with feasible region? Testing $(0,0)$ gives $0 < 7$, line passes through $(7/3, 0) \approx 2.33$ and $(0, 1.4)$. It has no points in common with feasible region! Hence, Minimum $Z = 7$ at $(3/2, 1/2)$.

Q6: Minimise $Z = x + 2y$ subject to $2x + y \ge 3, x + 2y \ge 6, x, y \ge 0$

Corner points: $(6, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$. (Note: line $2x+y=3$ lies entirely below $x+2y \ge 6$ in the first quadrant except near origin).
• $Z(6, 0) = 6 + 0 = 6$
• $Z(0, 3) = 0 + 2(3) = 6$
Both corners yield $Z = 6$! Test open half-plane $x + 2y < 6$: has no points in common.
Conclusion: The minimum value of $Z$ is 6, and it occurs at all points on the line segment joining $(6, 0)$ and $(0, 3)$.

Q9: Maximise $Z = -x + 2y$ subject to $x \ge 3, x + y \ge 5, x + 2y \ge 6, y \ge 0$

Unbounded region. Corner points: $(6, 0), (4, 1), (3, 2)$.
• $Z(6,0) = -6$, $Z(4,1) = -4+2 = -2$, $Z(3,2) = -3+4 = \mathbf{1}$ (Largest corner value).
Test open half-plane $-x + 2y > 1$: As $y \to \infty$, points like $(3, 5)$ give $-3 + 10 = 7 > 1$, and $(3, 5)$ is inside feasible region! The open half-plane has common points with the region.
Conclusion: $Z$ has no maximum value!

Q7: Minimise & Maximise $Z = 5x + 10y$ subject to $x + 2y \le 120, x + y \ge 60, x - 2y \ge 0, x, y \ge 0$

Bounded region with four vertices:
• $(60, 0) \implies Z = 5(60) + 0 = \mathbf{300}$ (Minimum)
• $(120, 0) \implies Z = 5(120) + 0 = \mathbf{600}$ (Maximum)
• $(60, 30) \implies Z = 5(60) + 10(30) = 300 + 300 = \mathbf{600}$ (Maximum)
• $(40, 20) \implies Z = 5(40) + 10(20) = 200 + 200 = 400$
Results: Minimum value is 300 at $(60, 0)$. Maximum value is 600 at all points on the line segment joining $(120, 0)$ and $(60, 30)$!

Q8: Minimise & Maximise $Z = x + 2y$ subject to $x + 2y \ge 100, 2x - y \le 0, 2x + y \le 200, x, y \ge 0$

Bounded region with vertices $(0, 50), (20, 40), (50, 100), (0, 200)$.
• $Z(0, 50) = 100$, $Z(20, 40) = 20 + 80 = 100$
• $Z(50, 100) = 50 + 200 = 250$
• $Z(0, 200) = 0 + 400 = \mathbf{400}$ (Maximum)
Results: Minimum value is 100 at all points on the segment joining $(0, 50)$ and $(20, 40)$. Maximum value is 400 at $(0, 200)$.

Q10: Maximise $Z = x + y$ subject to $x - y \le -1, -x + y \le 0, x, y \ge 0$

Rewrite constraints:
1. $x - y \le -1 \implies y \ge x + 1$
2. $-x + y \le 0 \implies y \le x$
Can a real number $y$ be simultaneously $\ge x + 1$ and $\le x$? No! $(x + 1 \le y \le x \implies 1 \le 0$, impossible!).
Conclusion: The lines $y = x + 1$ and $y = x$ are parallel and their half-planes point in opposite directions. The feasible region is empty, so there is no maximum value.

Chapter 12 • Historical Note & Summary Slide 17 of 21

Chapter Summary & 85-Year Historical Milestone

Linear Programming revolutionized economics, computing, and industrial engineering. Let us review the core mathematical principles and trace the historical milestones that shaped modern decision science.

Chapter 12 Key Takeaways

• LPP Components: Objective function $Z = ax + by$, linear constraints, and non-negative restrictions $x, y \ge 0$.
• Feasible Region: Always a convex polygon formed by overlapping half-planes.
• Corner Point Principle: Optimal values must occur at vertices of the feasible region.
• Bounded vs Unbounded: Bounded regions always guarantee both maximum and minimum; unbounded regions require the open half-plane test ($ax + by > M$ or $< m$).
• Multiple Solutions: When two vertices share the optimal value, the entire connecting segment is optimal.
• Infeasibility: Conflicting constraints lead to an empty feasible region $\emptyset$.

Historical Timeline (1941–1975)

• 1941: Leonid Kantorovich (USSR) and Frank L. Hitchcock (USA) formulate the transportation problem to optimize resource allocations during wartime.
• 1945: English economist George Stigler formulates the 'diet problem' to minimize food expenditure while satisfying nutritional guidelines.
• 1947: American mathematician George B. Dantzig invents the world-famous Simplex Method, an iterative finite-step algorithm that powers modern optimization solvers.
• 1975: Leonid Kantorovich and Tjalling C. Koopmans receive the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for their pioneering contributions to the theory of optimum allocation of resources.
