Direction Angles & Direction Cosines of a Line

If a directed line $L$ makes angles $lpha, eta, \gamma$ with the coordinate axes, their cosines $l = \cos\alpha, m = \cos\beta, n = \cos\gamma$ are its unique direction cosines.

Definition & Sense Reversal

1. Direction Angles & Cosines:

Angles: $\alpha, \beta, \gamma \in [0, \pi]$. Cosines: $l = \cos\alpha, \; m = \cos\beta, \; n = \cos\gamma$.

2. Line Direction Reversal:

Reversing the direction of $L$ transforms angles to their supplements:

\pi - \alpha, \quad \pi - \beta, \quad \pi - \gamma

Cosines flip signs: $\cos(\pi - \theta) = -\cos\theta \implies (-l, -m, -n)$.

An undirected line has TWO sets of direction cosines: $(l, m, n)$ and $(-l, -m, -n)$.

Lines Not Passing Through Origin (Page 378):
To find direction cosines of any line in space, draw a parallel line through origin $O$. Parallel lines share identical direction cosines!

§ 11.2 Direction Ratios Direction Numbers (a, b, c), Conversion Constant k, and Axes d.c.'s

Direction Ratios & Direction Cosine Conversion

Direction ratios $a, b, c$ are any numbers proportional to $l, m, n$. They allow direction cosines to be computed without knowing angles: $l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}$.

Derivation of Conversion Formula (Page 378)

Let $\frac{l}{a} = \frac{m}{b} = \frac{n}{c} = k$. Then:
l = ak, \quad m = bk, \quad n = ck
Since $l^2 + m^2 + n^2 = 1$:
k^2(a^2 + b^2 + c^2) = 1 \implies k = \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{a^2 + b^2 + c^2}}
l = \pm \frac{a}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}, \; m = \pm \frac{b}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}, \; n = \pm \frac{c}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}

Direction Cosines of Coordinate Axes (Example 4)

(1, 0, 0)

(0, 1, 0)

(0, 0, 1)

Direction Cosines of a Line Passing Through Two Points

Exactly one line passes through two distinct points $P(x_1, y_1, z_1)$ and $Q(x_2, y_2, z_2)$. Right triangle projections directly yield its direction cosines.

Geometric Derivation (Fig 11.2)

Drop perpendiculars to XY plane ($R, S$) and $P$ to $QS$ ($N$).
In right triangle $\triangle PNQ$: $\angle PQN = \gamma$.
\cos\gamma = \frac{NQ}{PQ} = \frac{z_2 - z_1}{PQ}
Similarly projecting onto X and Y axes gives:
\cos\alpha = \frac{x_2 - x_1}{PQ}, \quad \cos\beta = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{PQ}
PQ = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2 + (z_2 - z_1)^2}

Example 3: P(-2, 4, -5) and Q(1, 2, 3)

Differences:
x_2 - x_1 = 1 - (-2) = 3
y_2 - y_1 = 2 - 4 = -2
z_2 - z_1 = 3 - (-5) = 8
Distance: $PQ = \sqrt{3^2 + (-2)^2 + 8^2} = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 64} = \sqrt{77}$
\text{d.c.'s} = \left(\frac{3}{\sqrt{77}}, \; -\frac{2}{\sqrt{77}}, \; \frac{8}{\sqrt{77}}\right)

11.3 Equation of a Line: Point-Direction Vector Form

A line in space is uniquely pinned down by a fixed position point $A(\vec{a})$ and a parallel direction vector $\vec{b}$. For every point $P(\vec{r})$, $\vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}$.

Derivation from Triangle Vectors (Fig 11.3)

Let $P(\vec{r})$ be any point on line $l$.
Vector $\vec{AP}$ is parallel to $\vec{b} \implies \vec{AP} = \lambda\vec{b}$.
From triangle $\triangle OAP$: $\vec{OP} = \vec{OA} + \vec{AP}$.
\vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b} \quad (\lambda \in \mathbb{R})
As scalar parameter $\lambda$ varies from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, $\vec{r}$ sweeps every point along the infinite line.

§ 11.3 Cartesian Equation Symmetric Cartesian Form & Line Passing Through Two Points

Cartesian Form & Line Passing Through Two Given Points

Equating components in $\vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda\vec{b}$ and eliminating parameter $\lambda$ yields the symmetrical Cartesian equation $\frac{x - x_1}{a} = \frac{y - y_1}{b} = \frac{z - z_1}{c}$.

Symmetric Cartesian Form

Parametric Equations:
x = x_1 + \lambda a, \quad y = y_1 + \lambda b, \quad z = z_1 + \lambda c
Eliminating parameter $\lambda$:
\frac{x - x_1}{a} = \frac{y - y_1}{b} = \frac{z - z_1}{c} = \lambda
Using direction cosines: $\frac{x - x_1}{l} = \frac{y - y_1}{m} = \frac{z - z_1}{n}$.

Line Through Two Points A(a) & B(b)

Direction vector is $\vec{b} - \vec{a}$.
• Vector Equation:
\vec{r} = \vec{a} + \lambda(\vec{b} - \vec{a})
• Cartesian Equation:
\frac{x - x_1}{x_2 - x_1} = \frac{y - y_1}{y_2 - y_1} = \frac{z - z_1}{z_2 - z_1}

§ 11.4 Line Angles Acute Angle θ Between Lines via Dot Product of Direction Vectors

11.4 Angle Between Two Lines in Space

The angle between two lines equals the angle between their direction vectors $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$. By convention, we measure the acute angle using absolute value.

Acute Angle Master Formulas

• Vector Equation:
\cos\theta = \left| \frac{\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1||\vec{b}_2|} \right|
• Cartesian Direction Ratios Form:
\cos\theta = \left| \frac{a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2}{\sqrt{a_1^2+b_1^2+c_1^2}\sqrt{a_2^2+b_2^2+c_2^2}} \right|
• Direction Cosines Form:
\cos\theta = |l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2|

Benchmark Example 7 (Page 384–385)

$\vec{b}_1 = \hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 2\hat{k} \implies |\vec{b}_1| = \sqrt{1 + 4 + 4} = 3$
$\vec{b}_2 = 3\hat{i} + 2\hat{j} + 6\hat{k} \implies |\vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 36} = 7$
$\vec{b}_1 \cdot \vec{b}_2 = (1)(3) + (2)(2) + (2)(6) = 3 + 4 + 12 = 19$
\cos\theta = \frac{19}{3 \times 7} = \frac{19}{21} \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{19}{21}\right)

§ 11.4 Line Criteria Orthogonal Condition (Sum of Products = 0) vs Parallel Condition (Proportionality)

Perpendicular & Parallel Lines in Three-Dimensional Space

Two lines are perpendicular when their direction dot product vanishes. They are parallel when their direction vectors are scalar multiples.

Dual Analytical Criteria

1. Perpendicular Lines (θ = 90°):
a_1 a_2 + b_1 b_2 + c_1 c_2 = 0
Or in direction cosines: $l_1 l_2 + m_1 m_2 + n_1 n_2 = 0$.
2. Parallel Lines (θ = 0°):
\frac{a_1}{a_2} = \frac{b_1}{b_2} = \frac{c_1}{c_2}
Direction vectors are scalar multiples: $\vec{b}_2 = \lambda \vec{b}_1$.

Example 8: Cartesian Direction Ratios

Line 1: $\frac{x+3}{3} = \frac{y-1}{5} = \frac{z+3}{4} \implies (a_1, b_1, c_1) = (3, 5, 4)$
Line 2: $\frac{x+1}{1} = \frac{y-4}{1} = \frac{z-5}{2} \implies (a_2, b_2, c_2) = (1, 1, 2)$
Numerator: $3(1) + 5(1) + 4(2) = 16$
Denominator: $\sqrt{9+25+16}\sqrt{1+1+4} = \sqrt{50}\sqrt{6} = 5\sqrt{2}\sqrt{6} = 10\sqrt{3}$
\cos\theta = \frac{16}{10\sqrt{3}} = \frac{8\sqrt{3}}{15} \implies \theta = \cos^{-1}\left(\frac{8\sqrt{3}}{15}\right)

11.5 The Geometry of Skew Lines in Three Dimensions

Unlike 2D planes, lines in space can be neither parallel nor intersecting. Such non-coplanar lines are called skew lines.

The 3D Room Box Model (Page 385–386)

1. Skew Lines Definition:

Lines in space that are neither intersecting nor parallel. They lie in different parallel planes and never meet.

2. Room Diagonal Example (Fig 11.5):

In a room of size $1 \times 3 \times 2$ units:

• Line $GE$ goes diagonally across the ceiling ($z = 2$).

• Line $DB$ goes diagonally down the wall from ceiling to floor.

They will never intersect, nor are their directions parallel: they are skew lines!

Fig 11.5: 3D Room Skew Lines

The shortest distance between skew lines is along the unique line segment perpendicular to both lines simultaneously.

11.5.1 Shortest Distance Between Two Skew Lines: Vector Proof

Because the common perpendicular is orthogonal to both direction vectors $\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{b}_2$, its direction is $\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2$. Projecting $\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$ yields $d$.

Rigorous Step-by-Step Vector Proof (Page 386)

Lines: $\vec{r} = \vec{a}_1 + \lambda\vec{b}_1$ and $\vec{r} = \vec{a}_2 + \mu\vec{b}_2$.
Common perpendicular $\vec{PQ}$ is $\perp \vec{b}_1$ and $\perp \vec{b}_2$.
\hat{n} = \frac{\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|}
Connect point $S(\vec{a}_1)$ on $l_1$ and $T(\vec{a}_2)$ on $l_2$: $\vec{ST} = \vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1$.
Shortest distance $d = |\vec{ST} \cdot \hat{n}|$:
d = \left| \frac{(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)}{|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|} \right|

Example 9: Skew Distance Calculation

$\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1 = (2\hat{i}+\hat{j}-\hat{k}) - (\hat{i}+\hat{j}) = \hat{i} - \hat{k}$
$\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2 = (2\hat{i}-\hat{j}+\hat{k}) \times (3\hat{i}-5\hat{j}+2\hat{k}) = 3\hat{i} - \hat{j} - 7\hat{k}$
$|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2| = \sqrt{9 + 1 + 49} = \sqrt{59}$
$(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) = 3(1) - 1(0) - 7(-1) = 10$
d = \frac{|10|}{\sqrt{59}} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{59}} \text{ units}

§ 11.5.1 Cartesian Form Scalar Triple Product Determinant & Coplanarity Test

Cartesian Formula for Skew Distance & Coplanarity

Expressing the scalar triple product $(\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2) \cdot (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)$ as a $3 \times 3$ determinant provides the direct Cartesian distance formula.

The 3x3 Determinant Form (Page 387)

d = \frac{\begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix}}{\sqrt{(b_1 c_2 - b_2 c_1)^2 + (c_1 a_2 - c_2 a_1)^2 + (a_1 b_2 - a_2 b_1)^2}}
The denominator is the expansion of $|\vec{b}_1 \times \vec{b}_2|$ using Lagrange's identity.

Coplanarity & Intersection Criterion

Two lines intersect if and only if their shortest distance $d = 0$.
Definitive Condition for Coplanarity:
\begin{vmatrix} x_2 - x_1 & y_2 - y_1 & z_2 - z_1 \\ a_1 & b_1 & c_1 \\ a_2 & b_2 & c_2 \end{vmatrix} = 0
If the determinant vanishes, the lines lie in the same plane and intersect at a common point!

11.5.2 Distance Between Two Parallel Lines in Space

Parallel lines share the same direction vector $\vec{b}$ and are coplanar. Their perpendicular distance is calculated using the cross product: $d = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|}$.

Trigonometric Cross-Product Proof (Fig 11.7)

Lines: $\vec{r} = \vec{a}_1 + \lambda\vec{b}$ and $\vec{r} = \vec{a}_2 + \mu\vec{b}$.
Perpendicular distance $PT = ST \sin\theta = |\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1| \sin\theta$.
From cross product definition: $|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)| = |\vec{b}||\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1| \sin\theta$.
d = |PT| = \frac{|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)|}{|\vec{b}|}

Example 10: Step-by-Step Evaluation

$\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1 = 2\hat{i} + \hat{j} - \hat{k}$
Direction vector $\vec{b} = 2\hat{i} + 3\hat{j} + 6\hat{k} \implies |\vec{b}| = \sqrt{4+9+36} = 7$
$\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1) = -9\hat{i} + 14\hat{j} - 4\hat{k}$
$|\vec{b} \times (\vec{a}_2 - \vec{a}_1)| = \sqrt{81 + 196 + 16} = \sqrt{293}$
d = \frac{\sqrt{293}}{7} \text{ units}
