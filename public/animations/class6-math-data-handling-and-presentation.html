Data Basics

Collection, Facts & Purpose

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Data is any collection of facts, numbers, measures, observations, or descriptions gathered to answer questions and make decisions. From student favorite sports to weather records, raw data provides the raw ingredients for informed reasoning.

Every day, we make decisions based on information: What game does our class want to play? Which book is most borrowed from the library? What was the hottest day this month? All these questions begin with data.

📋 What Counts as Data?
  • • Definition: Any collection of facts, measurements, or observations gathered systematically.
  • • Numerical Data: Heights in centimeters, test scores, monthly rainfall in millimeters.
  • • Categorical Data: Favorite ice cream flavor, mode of travel to school (bus, bicycle, walk).
🎯 The Collection Process
  • • Step 1: Formulate the Question: e.g., "What is the most popular sport among Grade 6 students?"
  • • Step 2: Gather Unbiased Responses: Survey every student once and record the raw answers.
  • • Step 3: Raw Data vs Organized Data: Unsorted lists are confusing; organizing them into tables unlocks meaning!
💡 Father of Indian Statistics: Professor P. C. Mahalanobis founded the Indian Statistical Institute (ISI) in Kolkata and pioneered large-scale sample surveys across India, transforming national economic planning!

Tally Counts

Grouping by Fives & Counting Occurrences

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Tally marks provide an efficient, error-free method to record survey counts on the fly. By drawing 4 vertical strokes and a diagonal cross for the 5th mark, counts group neatly into bunches of five. Frequency is the total count in each category.

When collecting survey data in a busy classroom, counting tick marks one by one leads to mistakes. Mathematicians use tally marks grouped into bundles of five for rapid, reliable counting.

🥢 The Bundle-of-Five Rule
  • • Count 1 to 4: Draw one vertical stroke for each response: | , || , ||| , ||||.
  • • Count 5: Draw a diagonal slash across the four strokes: |||| (one full bundle of 5!).
  • • Count 6 to 9: Start new vertical strokes beside the bundle: |||| | = 6.
  • • Count 10: Two complete bundles: |||| ||||.
📊 What is Frequency?
  • • Frequency: The total number of times a particular observation, item, or response occurs.
  • • Example: If 12 students choose Cricket, the frequency of Cricket is 12 (represented as |||| |||| ||).
  • • The sum of all frequencies must equal the total number of survey participants!
💡 Counting Shortcut: Because each crossed bundle equals 5, counting tally totals is as easy as skip-counting by fives: 5, 10, 15, 20... then adding the remaining single marks!

Sorting Data

Ascending Order & Letter Frequency in Language

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Raw numbers become intuitive when sorted in ascending order. Students sort shoe sizes to quickly pinpoint minimum, maximum, and mode, and tally letter occurrences in text to uncover English language frequency patterns.

Looking at an unorganized jumble of 30 shoe sizes like 5, 4, 7, 5, 6, 4, 5, 8, 6, 5... makes it difficult to see what is happening. By sorting into ascending order, patterns emerge immediately!

👟 Sorted Shoe Sizes Table

  • • Smallest Value: Instantly visible at the start (Size 4).
  • • Largest Value: Instantly visible at the end (Size 8).

  • • Rare letters like Q, X, Z appear almost zero times.
  • • Codebreakers, keyboard designers (QWERTY), and Morse code creators used this exact data handling method!

Picture Graphs

Visual Icons & The Key/Scale Factor

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
A pictograph represents data using pictures or icons. Instead of reading tables of numbers, readers absorb relative comparisons at a glance. The scale or key defines how many real units a single drawn symbol represents.

A picture is worth a thousand words—and in statistics, a pictograph is worth a thousand numbers! A pictograph uses repeated icons or symbols to display counts visually.

🔑 The Critical Key (Scale)
  • • Drawing 100 bicycles on paper is impossible.
  • • Solution: Define a Key where 1 symbol represents multiple items!
  • • Example: 🚲 = 10 Bicycles.
  • • To represent 40 bicycles, we simply draw 4 symbols (4 × 10 = 40).
  • • To represent 70 bicycles, we draw 7 symbols (7 × 10 = 70).
👀 At-a-Glance Interpretation
  • • Longest row of symbols = Highest count.
  • • Shortest row of symbols = Lowest count.
  • • Readers can instantly see which category dominates without performing mental math.
⚠️ Golden Rule: A pictograph without a declared Key is meaningless! Always check the Key at the top or bottom of the graph before counting symbols.

Half Symbols

Fractions of Scale & Limitations

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
When counts are not exact multiples of the scale, partial or half-symbols are drawn (e.g. half a face for 5 students when 1 symbol = 10). We explore the utility and visual limitations of drawing fractions of icons.

What happens if your key is 1 Smiley = 10 Students, but a class has 25 students? Drawing two symbols gives 20, and three symbols gives 30. We need partial symbols!

✂️ Half-Symbols
  • • Full symbol: 1 Symbol = 10 units.
  • • Half symbol: 1/2 Symbol = 5 units.
  • • 25 students = 2 full symbols + 1 half symbol (10 + 10 + 5 = 25).
  • • Quarter symbol: representing 2.5 or 3 units (very tricky to draw accurately!).
⚠️ Inherent Limitations of Pictographs
  • • How do you draw 27 students? 7/10 of a face is almost impossible to draw or interpret accurately.
  • • Drawing dozens of complex figures by hand is extremely tedious and time-consuming.
  • • For very large, diverse numbers (like census data in lakhs), pictographs become impractical.
💡 The Bridge to Bar Graphs: These exact limitations of pictographs led mathematicians to invent the bar graph, where continuous height accurately represents any exact number!

Bar Charts

Axes, Uniform Width & Equal Gaps

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
A bar graph represents data using rectangular bars of uniform width drawn horizontally or vertically. The length/height of each bar corresponds to its frequency based on a chosen axis scale. Gaps between bars must be uniform.

When data sets grow large, bar graphs (column charts) provide the most versatile and precise visual tool. Let us examine the anatomical rules of a standard bar graph.

📐 The Three Sacred Rules of Bars
  • • Rule 1: Uniform Width: All bars must have the exact same width! A wider bar falsely tricks the eye into thinking it has more value.
  • • Rule 2: Uniform Spacing: The gap between adjacent bars must be constant throughout.
  • • Rule 3: Height Proportional to Frequency: The bar height is determined strictly by the scale on the numerical axis.
🧭 The Two Axes
  • • Horizontal Axis (X-axis): Typically displays the categories (e.g., Sports, Days, Fruit types).
  • • Vertical Axis (Y-axis): Contains the numerical scale, marked in equal intervals starting from zero (e.g., 0, 10, 20, 30...).
  • • Both axes meet at a perpendicular right angle (90° origin).
💡 Scale Definition: The scale tells you what 1 unit length on the grid represents (e.g., 1 unit length = 10 students or 1 unit length = 100 vehicles).

Reading Bars

Finding Maxima, Minima & Comparing Intervals

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Master the art of reading bar graphs: identifying which category has the highest or lowest frequency, reading heights against the scale, calculating differences between bars, and computing aggregate totals across categories.

Reading a bar graph requires looking horizontally across from the top of each bar to the numerical axis. Let us practice extracting precise conclusions from a graph.

🔍 Key Analytical Inquiries
  • • Maximum Category: Look for the tallest bar (e.g. Cricket at 40 runs).
  • • Minimum Category: Look for the shortest bar (e.g. Tennis at 10 runs).
  • • Comparative Difference: How much higher is Cricket than Tennis? 40 − 10 = 30 runs.
  • • Total Sum: Add the heights of all bars to find the overall sample size.
📈 Real-World Example: Hourly Traffic
  • • Measuring vehicles crossing a bridge between 7 AM and 11 AM:
  • • Morning Peak: 8 AM to 9 AM shows the tallest bar (commuters traveling to work/school).
  • • Trough: 10 AM to 11 AM drops as rush hour concludes.
  • • City planners use these bar graph insights to schedule traffic police and bus frequencies!
💡 Reading Technique: Use a straight edge or ruler held horizontally from the top of a bar across to the vertical axis to avoid misreading in-between values!

Plotting Bars

The 4-Step Protocol: Axes, Scale, Labels & Bars

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Constructing a bar graph follows 4 sequential steps: draw perpendicular horizontal and vertical axes, choose and mark a uniform scale starting at zero, label categories with equal spacing, and draw bars of uniform width.

Let us master the four-step engineering protocol for drafting a clean, mathematically accurate bar graph on graph paper or plain paper.

1️⃣ & 2️⃣ Steps 1 & 2: Axes and Scale
  • • Step 1: Draw Perpendicular Axes: Draw a horizontal baseline and a vertical baseline meeting at a 90° corner labeled '0'.
  • • Step 2: Choose Scale: Inspect your maximum frequency (e.g. 52). Choose a convenient step like 1 unit = 10, marking 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60.
3️⃣ & 4️⃣ Steps 3 & 4: Categories & Bars
  • • Step 3: Mark Equal Widths: Along the horizontal axis, mark equal intervals for your categories, leaving equal gaps between them.
  • • Step 4: Draw Bars: Draw vertical rectangles up to the exact frequency height for each category. Shade or color each bar neatly!
💡 Scale Selection Secret: If data values range from 5 to 50, use steps of 5 or 10. If values range from 100 to 1,000, use steps of 100. Always ensure the scale fits comfortably on your page!

Chart Layout

Vertical Columns vs Horizontal Bars

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Should bars be drawn vertically or horizontally? Vertical bars suit upward-growing quantities like mountain heights or tree growth, while horizontal bars suit ground-parallel measurements like river lengths, race times, and long category names.

Did you know that bars can be drawn either standing upright (vertical columns) or lying flat (horizontal bars)? Choosing the right orientation makes graphs intuitive and easy to read.

🏔️ When to Use Vertical Bars
  • • Best for quantities that naturally grow upward from the ground.
  • • Examples: Mountain heights (Everest 8,848 m, K2 8,611 m), skyscraper heights, human height over age.
  • • Best for chronological time sequences (years: 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023 along the X-axis).
🌊 When to Use Horizontal Bars
  • • Best for quantities that stretch horizontally across land.
  • • Examples: River lengths (Ganga 2,525 km, Godavari 1,465 km), train distances, highway lengths.
  • • Design Advantage: When category names are very long (e.g. "Thiruvananthapuram Central"), horizontal bars allow labels to be read without tilting your head!
💡 Mathematical Equivalence: Both vertical and horizontal bar graphs convey the exact same mathematical data; orientation is an aesthetic and communication choice!

Graph Traps

Distorted Scales, Area Traps & Mathematical Honesty

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
Visual graphics can deceive if drawn carelessly or dishonestly. We examine common graphical traps: scaling widths alongside heights (area distortion), truncated baselines not starting at zero, and uneven intervals.

Data never lies—but badly drawn graphics can deceive! To become statistically literate citizens, students must learn to spot and avoid misleading visual presentations.

⚠️ Trap 1: The Area Illusion
  • • Imagine Company A sold 100 laptops and Company B sold 200 laptops (ratio 1 : 2).
  • • An artist doubles the height AND doubles the width of Company B's laptop icon.
  • • The Distortion: Doubling both height and width quadruples the visual area (2 × 2 = 4 times bigger!).
  • • The viewer mistakenly believes Company B sold 4 times as many laptops!
📉 Trap 2: Truncated Baselines
  • • A graph comparing 98% vs 99% starting at baseline 95% makes 99% look twice as tall as 98%!
  • • Remedy: Always inspect the Y-axis. Ensure the baseline starts at zero, or clearly mark broken axis breaks (≈).
  • • Keep bar widths strictly identical. Only height should reflect value!
💡 NCERT Guideline: In Ganita Prakash, students are cautioned: never alter the width or natural shape of objects (like stretching triangular mountains) in ways that distort underlying mathematical truth!

Graph Optimization Slide 14 of 20

Scale Choice

Balancing Space, Clarity & Precision

Mentors: Prof. P. C. Mahalanobis & Florence Nightingale
The choice of scale determines whether a bar graph fits neatly on a page or becomes cramped and illegible. We master selecting step intervals (1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100) based on page height and maximum frequency.

Selecting the right scale is like choosing the right magnification on a microscope. A scale that is too small makes the bars shoot off the paper; a scale that is too large squashes the bars into flat pancakes!

📏 The 3-Step Scale Formula
  1. Identify the Maximum Value in your data (e.g., Max = 78).
  2. Count how many grid lines (steps) fit comfortably on your page (typically 8 to 10 marks).
  3. Divide: 78 ÷ 8 ≈ 9.75. Round up to the nearest friendly benchmark: 1 unit = 10!
🌟 Friendly Scale Multiples
  • • Always choose scales in friendly multiples: 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 500.
  • • Avoid awkward scales like 1 unit = 3, 7, or 13, which make estimating intermediate heights confusing.
  • • Clearly write your chosen scale at the top right of the graph: Scale: 1 unit length = 10 students.
💡 Quick Test: If your maximum value is 380 and you have 10 marks available, what scale should you use? 380 ÷ 10 = 38 → round up to 1 unit = 50 (marks: 0, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400)!

Collection, Tally, Pictograph, Bar Graph & Inferences

Congratulations! You have mastered the complete journey of Data Handling and Presentation. Let us review the five interconnected stages of statistical investigation.

🔄 The 5-Stage Data Cycle
