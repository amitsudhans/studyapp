"Elements cannot be grouped by physical properties alone, as there are many notable exceptions among metals and non-metals."

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

Non-Metals Overview: Solids, Gases & Liquid Bromine

There are far fewer non-metals than metals. Most non-metals exist either as solids (carbon, sulphur, iodine, phosphorus) or gases (oxygen, nitrogen, hydrogen, chlorine). Bromine ($Br_2$) is the only non-metal that exists as a liquid at room temperature.

The 4 Major Physical Property Exceptions (NCERT In-text)

• State & Melting Points: Mercury ($Hg$) is the only liquid metal at room temperature. Gallium ($Ga$) and Caesium ($Cs$) have extremely low melting points ($29.76^\circ\text{C}$ and $28.4^\circ\text{C}$) and literally melt into liquid upon being placed on your warm palm!
• Lustre: Iodine is a non-metal, yet its crystals display a dark purple lustrous shine.
• Allotropes of Carbon: Diamond is the hardest natural substance known with colossal mp/bp; Graphite is a soft non-metal that conducts electricity due to free delocalised pi electrons.
• Density/Hardness: Alkali metals (Li, Na, K) are soft, light, and float or cut with a knife.

Oxides of Metals vs Non-Metals: Activity 3.8 Combustion & Litmus Testing

"Most non-metals produce acidic oxides when dissolved in water. On the other hand, most metals give rise to basic oxides."

When magnesium metal burns, its ash dissolves in water to turn red litmus blue. When sulphur burns, its pungent fumes dissolve to turn blue litmus red! This fundamental chemical divergence splits the periodic table into basic metal oxides and acidic non-metal oxides.

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Watch closely as we burn magnesium ribbon—it blazes with a dazzling white glare, forming basic magnesium oxide that turns red litmus paper blue! But burn non-metallic sulphur powder, and the fumes form sulphurous acid, turning blue litmus paper red! This proves: metals form basic oxides, while non-metals form acidic oxides!"

Activity 3.8 Part 1: Magnesium Combustion (Basic Oxide)

Magnesium ribbon burns in atmospheric oxygen with a dazzling white flame: $2Mg(s) + O_2(g) \to 2MgO(s)$. When white magnesium oxide powder is dissolved in water, it forms magnesium hydroxide: $MgO(s) + H_2O(l) \to Mg(OH)_2(aq)$. Testing with litmus confirms it is basic: Red litmus turns BLUE, while blue litmus shows no change.

Activity 3.8 Part 2: Sulphur Combustion (Acidic Oxide)

Sulphur powder heated on a deflagrating spoon burns with a blue flame, producing pungent choking sulphur dioxide gas: $S(s) + O_2(g) \to SO_2(g)$. Trapping the gas and dissolving it in water forms sulphurous acid: $SO_2(g) + H_2O(l) \to H_2SO_3(aq)$. Testing with litmus proves it is acidic: Blue litmus turns RED, while red litmus shows no change.

Section 3.2.1 • Oxidation Chemistry Slide 6 of 21

Burning Metals in Air: Amphoteric Oxides & Soluble Alkalis (Section 3.2.1 & Activity 3.9)

"Metal oxides that react with both acids as well as bases to produce salts and water are known as amphoteric oxides."

Almost all metals combine with oxygen to form metal oxides ($Metal + O_2 \to Metal\ oxide$). Copper forms black copper(II) oxide, aluminium forms aluminium oxide, and special amphoteric oxides can wear two hats: acting like a base in front of an acid, and acting like an acid in front of a base!

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Meet the chemical chameleons: amphoteric oxides! While copper oxidises into black copper oxide, aluminium oxide and zinc oxide can react with both strong acids and strong bases! When aluminium oxide reacts with sodium hydroxide, it forms sodium aluminate and water. Furthermore, sodium oxide and potassium oxide dissolve in water to generate potent caustic alkalis!"

Oxidation of Copper and Aluminium

• When copper wire is strongly heated in air, it does not burn with a flame, but forms a superficial black coat of copper(II) oxide: $2Cu(s) + O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2CuO(s)$ (black oxide).
• Aluminium rapidly forms aluminium oxide: $4Al(s) + 3O_2(g) \to 2Al_2O_3(s)$.

The Chemistry of Amphoteric Oxides ($Al_2O_3$ & $ZnO$)

Most metal oxides are basic, but Aluminium oxide ($Al_2O_3$) and Zinc oxide ($ZnO$) exhibit both basic and acidic properties:
1. Acting as a Base (with acid): $Al_2O_3 + 6HCl \to 2AlCl_3 + 3H_2O$
2. Acting as an Acid (with base): $Al_2O_3 + 2NaOH \to 2NaAlO_2 \text{ (Sodium aluminate)} + H_2O$
• Soluble Alkalis: While most metal oxides are insoluble in water, $Na_2O$ and $K_2O$ dissolve vigorously: $Na_2O(s) + H_2O(l) \to 2NaOH(aq)$ and $K_2O(s) + H_2O(l) \to 2KOH(aq)$.

Section 3.2.1 • Protective Oxide Layers Slide 7 of 21

Metal Reactivity with Oxygen, Kerosene Storage & Anodising Aluminium (Section 3.2.1)

"Metals such as potassium and sodium react so vigorously that they catch fire if kept in the open. Hence, to prevent accidental fires, they are kept immersed in kerosene oil."

Not all metals react with oxygen at the same tempo. Potassium and sodium spontaneously ignite in air, magnesium burns with a white blaze, iron filings sparkle in a flame, while silver and gold refuse to react even at white-hot temperatures. Meanwhile, aluminium protects itself with an engineered anodised oxide armour!

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Why do chemists store sodium and potassium under kerosene oil? Because the moment they touch air or water, they spontaneously ignite in flames! In contrast, aluminium forms a micro-thin self-protective oxide shield. Through anodising, we make aluminium the anode in dilute sulphuric acid, growing a diamond-hard oxide armour that can be dyed in brilliant colours!"

Reactivity Hierarchy with Oxygen & Kerosene Storage

• Potassium ($K$) & Sodium ($Na$): Spontaneously ignite in atmospheric oxygen and moisture forming explosive exothermic flames. Stored strictly under kerosene oil.
• Self-Passivation ($Mg, Al, Zn, Pb$): At room temperature, their lustrous surfaces oxidise to form an impermeable, microscopic protective oxide coating that shields underlying metal from further oxidation.
• Iron: Bulky iron bars do not burn on heating, but iron filings sprinkled over a Bunsen flame sparkle brilliantly.
• Noble Metals ($Ag, Au$): Do not oxidise even at elevated temperatures.

Do You Know? The Anodising Process of Aluminium

Anodising is an electrolytic technique to artificially thicken the natural protective $Al_2O_3$ skin. A clean aluminium article is made the anode (+) and electrolysed in dilute sulphuric acid ($H_2SO_4$). Oxygen gas liberated at the anode reacts vigorously with aluminium to build a durable, corrosion-resistant, porous oxide layer that readily absorbs dyes for vibrant decorative finishes.

Reactivity with Water: Cold, Hot & Steam Dynamics (Section 3.2.2, Activity 3.10 & Fig 3.3)

"Metals react with water to produce metal oxide and hydrogen. Soluble metal oxides dissolve further to form metal hydroxides."

Pour water on sodium and it explodes into lilac-tinged flames! Drop calcium in water and it bobs up to the surface floating on bubbles of hydrogen! Magnesium demands boiling water, iron requires red-hot steam, while copper and gold sit completely inert.

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Watch the drama of metals reacting with water! Potassium and sodium detonate on cold water, setting hydrogen gas ablaze! Calcium reacts more gently, bobbing up to float as hydrogen bubbles cling to its skin. Magnesium ignores cold water but reacts with hot water. Cold Water Reactions: Sodium, Potassium & Calcium

• Potassium & Sodium: React violently and exothermically with cold water; the evolved $H_2$ gas instantly catches fire: $2K(s) + 2H_2O(l) \to 2KOH(aq) + H_2(g) + \text{Heat}$; $2Na(s) + 2H_2O(l) \to 2NaOH(aq) + H_2(g) + \text{Heat}$.
• Calcium: Less violent; heat evolved is insufficient to ignite $H_2$: $Ca(s) + 2H_2O(l) \to Ca(OH)_2(aq) + H_2(g)$. Phenomenon: Calcium starts floating because bubbles of hydrogen gas adhere to its surface!

Hot Water & Steam Reactions: Magnesium, Aluminium & Iron

• Magnesium ($Mg$): Does not react with cold water; reacts with hot water forming $Mg(OH)_2$ and $H_2$, and floats like calcium due to attached $H_2$ bubbles.
• Steam Reactions ($Al, Zn, Fe$): In Figure 3.3, steam generated from wet glass wool passes over red-hot iron: $3Fe(s) + 4H_2O(g) \to Fe_3O_4(s) \text{ (iron(II,III) oxide)} + 4H_2(g)$; $2Al(s) + 3H_2O(g) \to Al_2O_3(s) + 3H_2(g)$.
• Inert Metals: Lead, copper, silver, and gold show zero reaction with cold water, hot water, or steam.

Reaction of Metals with Acids, The Nitric Acid Exception & Aqua Regia (Section 3.2.3 & Activity 3.11)

"Hydrogen gas is not evolved when a metal reacts with nitric acid because HNO3 is a strong oxidising agent. But magnesium and manganese are exceptions!"

When metals plunge into dilute hydrochloric acid, magnesium effervesces violently with rapid hydrogen bubbles, aluminium follows, then zinc, then iron, while copper yields nothing. But what happens with nitric acid, and how does the royal liquid Aqua Regia dissolve even solid gold?

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Notice how magnesium bubbles furiously in dilute acid, while copper lies dormant! But here is a famous board exam trap: nitric acid does NOT evolve hydrogen with most metals because it oxidises hydrogen into water! Only two metals—magnesium and manganese—can evolve hydrogen with very dilute nitric acid. Activity 3.11: Dilute Acid Reactivity Order

Dropping metals into dilute $HCl$ shows bubbling kinetics and temperature spikes decreasing in the exact order: $Mg > Al > Zn > Fe$. Magnesium reaches peak exothermicity and fastest bubble evolution ($Mg + 2HCl \to MgCl_2 + H_2$). Copper shows zero bubbles and zero temperature change, proving it cannot displace hydrogen from dilute acids.

The $HNO_3$ Oxidising Exception & Aqua Regia

• Nitric Acid Exception: $HNO_3$ is a fierce oxidising agent. It oxidises evolved $H_2$ into $H_2O$ and is itself reduced to nitrogen oxides ($N_2O, NO, NO_2$). However, very dilute ($~1\%$) $HNO_3$ reacts with Magnesium ($Mg$) and Manganese ($Mn$) to liberate true $H_2$ gas!
• Aqua Regia ('Royal Water'): A freshly prepared, highly fuming mixture of concentrated $HCl$ and concentrated $HNO_3$ in a 3:1 ratio. Neither acid alone can dissolve gold or platinum, but together aqua regia forms nascent chlorine and nitrosyl chloride ($NOCl$) that effortlessly dissolves noble gold.

Displacement Reactions & The Complete Reactivity Series (Sections 3.2.4, 3.2.5 & Table 3.2)

"The reactivity series is a list of metals arranged in the order of their decreasing activities: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au."

Can copper steal sulphate from iron, or can iron steal sulphate from copper? By systematically pitting metals against other metal salt solutions, chemists established the definitive pecking order of chemical reactivity: The Reactivity Series.

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Behold the royal hierarchy of metals: The Reactivity Series! Potassium stands supreme at the top, followed by sodium, calcium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron, lead, and hydrogen. Below hydrogen sit the noble metals: copper, mercury, silver, and gold. When an iron nail enters copper sulphate, iron kicks out copper, turning the blue solution pale green!"

Activity 3.12: Metal Salt Displacement Test

• Iron nail placed in blue copper sulphate ($CuSO_4$): Iron is more reactive than copper; it displaces copper: $Fe(s) + CuSO_4(aq) \to FeSO_4(aq) + Cu(s)$. The deep blue solution fades to light green ($Fe^{2+}$) and reddish-brown copper coats the nail.
• Copper wire placed in iron sulphate ($FeSO_4$): Zero reaction! Copper is less reactive than iron and cannot displace it.

Table 3.2: The Definitive Reactivity Series

K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au
• Metals positioned above hydrogen ($K$ to $Pb$) displace $H_2$ from dilute mineral acids.
• Metals positioned below hydrogen ($Cu, Hg, Ag, Au$) are unreactive with dilute acids and do not displace hydrogen.
• Any metal higher up will displace any metal below it from its aqueous salt solution.

Section 3.3 • Electron Transfer Slide 11 of 21

How Metals and Non-Metals React: Electronic Configurations & Octet Theory (Section 3.3 & Table 3.3)

"The reactivity of elements is explained as a tendency to attain a completely filled valence shell octet. Metals lose electrons to form cations; non-metals gain electrons to form anions."

Why do atoms react at all? Noble gases like Helium ($2$), Neon ($2,8$), and Argon ($2,8,8$) have serene, complete outer shells and remain chemically inert. All other elements engage in chemical transactions—donating or accepting electrons—to achieve that same blissful noble gas stability.

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Why do metals and non-metals unite? To achieve the octet of the noble gases! Sodium has one lonely electron in its outer shell; chlorine is ravenous for just one. Sodium transfers its electron to chlorine! Sodium becomes a positive cation, chlorine becomes a negative anion, and powerful electrostatic attraction locks them into crystalline table salt!"

Formation of Sodium Chloride ($NaCl$)

• Sodium ($Na$, atomic number 11: $2,8,1$) has 1 valence electron in its M-shell. Losing it yields a stable neon-like octet ($2,8$) with 11 protons and 10 electrons: $Na \to Na^+ + e^-$ (Sodium cation).
• Chlorine ($Cl$, atomic number 17: $2,8,7$) needs 1 electron to complete its argon-like octet ($2,8,8$): $Cl + e^- \to Cl^-$ (Chloride anion).
• Oppositely charged $Na^+$ and $Cl^-$ attract via intense electrostatic forces: $Na^+ + Cl^- \to NaCl$. Sodium chloride exists not as isolated molecules, but as an infinite three-dimensional crystal lattice of alternating ions!

Formation of Magnesium Chloride ($MgCl_2$)

Magnesium ($Mg$, atomic number 12: $2,8,2$) loses 2 electrons to attain neon configuration: $Mg \to Mg^{2+} + 2e^-$. Two separate chlorine atoms each accept one electron: $2Cl + 2e^- \to 2Cl^-$. The resulting formula is $Mg^{2+}[Cl^-]_2$ ($MgCl_2$, Fig 3.6).

Properties of Ionic Compounds: Melting Points, Brittleness & Conductivity (Section 3.3.1 & Activity 3.13)

"Ionic compounds conduct electricity in the molten state or aqueous solution, but NEVER in the solid state because movement of ions is impossible in a rigid lattice."

Why does table salt melt only at a blistering 801°C (1074 K)? Why does a salt crystal shatter under a hammer blow? And why does dry solid salt insulate against electricity, while dissolved saltwater lights up an electric bulb like a beacon?

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Examine these fascinating properties of ionic compounds! Their melting points are sky-high—sodium chloride melts at over a thousand Kelvin! They dissolve in water but refuse to dissolve in petrol. And notice the golden rule of conductivity: solid salt cannot conduct electricity because its ions are trapped rigid, but the second you melt it or dissolve it in water, the ions are liberated to carry current!"

The 4 Hallmarks of Ionic Compounds

1. Physical Nature & Brittleness: Hard solids due to strong inter-ionic electrostatic bonds. However, when shear force is applied, like-charged ion layers slide over each other; repulsive forces shatter the crystal—making them brittle.
2. High Melting & Boiling Points: Enormous thermal energy is required to overcome the omnidirectional electrostatic attractions ($NaCl$ m.p. $1074\text{ K}$, $CaO$ m.p. $2850\text{ K}$, Table 3.4).
3. Solubility: Readily soluble in polar solvents (water, dielectric constant 80 hydrates ions) and insoluble in non-polar organic solvents like petrol, kerosene, and benzene.

Electrical Conduction: Solid vs Molten/Solution

Electric current requires mobile charge carriers. In solid state, ions are frozen in fixed lattice sites by rigid electrostatic forces; thus solid $NaCl$ is an electrical insulator. In molten state or aqueous solution, thermal energy or water hydration breaks the lattice; ions are liberated and migrate freely toward electrodes, conducting electricity readily (Activity 3.13 & Fig 3.8).

Occurrence of Metals: Minerals, Ores, Gangue & Metallurgy Overview (Sections 3.4 & 3.4.1)

"All ores are minerals, but all minerals are not ores. An ore contains a sufficiently high percentage of metal that can be profitably extracted."

The Earth's crust is humanity's subterranean treasure chest. While gold and platinum linger as pristine native metals, aluminium and iron are locked in oxides, carbonates, and sulphides contaminated with rocky gangue. How do metallurgical engineers plan extraction based on the reactivity series?

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Let us journey deep into the Earth's crust! Minerals are naturally occurring compounds, but only minerals rich enough to be mined profitably are called ores. Everything unwanted—sand, soil, rocky clay—is termed gangue. Notice how a metal's place on the reactivity ladder dictates how we extract it: heating alone for unreactive metals, carbon reduction for medium metals, and raw electricity for reactive metals!"

Minerals, Ores, and Gangue Definitions

• Mineral: Naturally occurring inorganic elements or compounds found in the Earth's crust.
• Ore: A mineral containing a high concentration of a specific metal that can be extracted commercially, conveniently, and profitably (e.g., Bauxite $Al_2O_3 \cdot 2H_2O$, Haematite $Fe_2O_3$).
• Gangue: The unwanted earthly, rocky, and sandy impurities (silica, clay) contaminating mined ores that must be removed prior to chemical reduction.

Metallurgical Roadmap by Reactivity (Fig 3.9 & 3.10)

1. Top of Series ($K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al$): High affinity for oxygen; extracted strictly by electrolysis of molten chlorides/oxides.
2. Middle of Series ($Zn, Fe, Pb, Cu$): Moderate reactivity; extracted by converting sulphides/carbonates into oxides via roasting or calcination, followed by chemical reduction using carbon.
3. Bottom of Series ($Ag, Au, Pt$): Low reactivity; found in native free state or extracted from sulphides ($HgS, Cu_2S$) by heating alone.

Extracting Metals Low in the Activity Series: Mercury & Copper Glance (Section 3.4.3)

"Metals low in the activity series are very unreactive. Their oxides can be reduced to metals by heating alone!"

Metals at the bottom of the activity ladder hold onto oxygen with frail grip. When red cinnabar ($HgS$) or black copper glance ($Cu_2S$) are roasted in air, thermal energy alone is potent enough to strip away sulphur and oxygen, leaving pure glistening liquid mercury and molten copper!

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Isn't chemistry elegant? Metals low on the reactivity series, like mercury and copper, do not need expensive reducing agents! Take cinnabar, the red ore of mercury. Roast it in air to form mercuric oxide, heat it a little more, and behold: pure liquid mercury flows out! Copper glance undergoes self-reduction: copper oxide reacts with its own sulphide to yield metallic copper!"

Extraction of Mercury from Cinnabar ($HgS$)

Cinnabar is the natural sulphide ore of mercury. When roasted in air, it converts first into mercuric oxide ($HgO$), which upon further heating thermally decomposes spontaneously into liquid mercury metal:
• Step 1 (Roasting): $2HgS(s) + 3O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2HgO(s) + 2SO_2(g)$
• Step 2 (Thermal Decomposition): $2HgO(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2Hg(l) + O_2(g)$

Extraction of Copper from Copper Glance ($Cu_2S$)

Copper occurring as copper(I) sulphide (copper glance) is extracted by roasting in air without needing any external reducing agent like carbon:
• Step 1 (Partial Roasting): $2Cu_2S(s) + 3O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2Cu_2O(s) + 2SO_2(g)$
• Step 2 (Auto-reduction / Self-reduction): Unreacted $Cu_2S$ reacts with newly formed $Cu_2O$: $2Cu_2O(s) + Cu_2S(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 6Cu(s) + SO_2(g)$.

Extracting Metals in the Middle of the Series: Roasting, Calcination & The Thermit Reaction (Section 3.4.4)

"Sulphide ores are converted into oxides by roasting in excess air. Carbonate ores are converted into oxides by calcination in limited air."

Why is it mandatory to convert zinc blende ($ZnS$) and calamine ($ZnCO_3$) into zinc oxide before reduction? And how does a pyrotechnic mixture of aluminium powder and rust weld broken railway tracks together on the spot?

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Remember this crucial rule: Sulphides get Roasted in excess air; Carbonates undergo Calcination in limited air! Both produce metal oxides ready for carbon reduction. And look at the thermit reaction: aluminium powder reacts with iron oxide with such ferocious heat that molten liquid iron pours out at thousands of degrees, fusing cracked railway tracks together seamlessly!"

Roasting vs Calcination for Middle-Series Metals

It is far easier to reduce a metal oxide than its sulphide or carbonate:
• Roasting (for Sulphides): Heating strongly in excess air: $2ZnS(s) + 3O_2(g) \xrightarrow{\Delta} 2ZnO(s) + 2SO_2(g)$
• Calcination (for Carbonates): Heating strongly in limited air: $ZnCO_3(s) \xrightarrow{\Delta} ZnO(s) + CO_2(g)$
• Carbon Reduction: Heating with coke: $ZnO(s) + C(s) \to Zn(s) + CO(g)$.

The Thermit Reaction: Welding Railway Tracks (Fig 3.11)

Highly reactive metals like aluminium can displace metals of lower reactivity with volcanic exothermicity! When iron(III) oxide is ignited with aluminium powder, the heat evolved is so astronomical that iron is produced in the molten white-hot liquid state:
$$Fe_2O_3(s) + 2Al(s) \to 2Fe(l) + Al_2O_3(s) + \text{Tremendous Heat}$$
This thermit reaction is used worldwide to weld cracked railway tracks and massive broken machine shafts directly on site.

Electrolytic Reduction & Electrolytic Refining of Copper: Anode Mud Chemistry (Sections 3.4.5, 3.4.6 & Fig 3.12)

"In electrolytic refining of copper, impure copper is made the anode and a thin strip of pure copper is made the cathode. Precious metals settle as anode mud!"

Metals produced by carbon reduction still harbor impurities that impair electrical conductivity. In electrolytic refining, raw blister copper dissolves into an acidified copper sulphate bath, leaving pure 99.99% copper at the cathode while gold and silver precipitate as valuable anode mud!

Dr. Sophia • Science Teacher
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Behold the pinnacle of modern purification: Electrolytic Refining! We hang a heavy slab of impure copper at the anode, and a slender strip of pure copper at the cathode. As electric current flows through acidified copper sulphate, pure copper migrates across to thicken the cathode! And at the bottom of the tank sits anode mud—containing precious gold and silver!"

Electrolytic Reduction of Molten Chlorides

Sodium and aluminium have immense affinity for oxygen and cannot be reduced by carbon. Sodium is obtained by electrolysing molten $NaCl$:
• At Cathode (-): $Na^+ + e^- \to Na$ (Reduction: sodium metal deposits)
• At Anode (+): $2Cl^- \to Cl_2 + 2e^-$ (Oxidation: chlorine gas bubbles out)

Electrolytic Refining of Copper (Fig 3.12)

• Anode (+): Thick slab of crude, impure copper.
• Cathode (-): Thin strip of ultra-pure copper.
• Electrolyte: Aqueous solution of acidified copper sulphate ($CuSO_4 + \text{dil. } H_2SO_4$).
• Process: When current flows, $Cu$ from the anode dissolves: $Cu \to Cu^{2+} + 2e^-$. An equivalent amount of pure $Cu^{2+}$ from the electrolyte deposits onto the cathode: $Cu^{2+} + 2e^- \to Cu$.
• Anode Mud: Soluble impurities dissolve in solution, while insoluble noble impurities like gold, silver, and platinum drop to the tank bottom underneath the anode as valuable anode mud.

Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

Corrosion Chemistry: Silver, Copper & Iron

• Silver: Reacts with trace atmospheric sulphur ($H_2S$) to form black silver sulphide ($Ag_2S$).
• Copper: Reacts with moist $CO_2$ to form a protective green coat of basic copper carbonate ($CuCO_3 \cdot Cu(OH)_2$).
• Iron Rusting (Activity 3.14): Requires BOTH air and water. In Test Tube A (air + water), iron nails rust heavily. In Test Tube B (boiled distilled water + oil barrier), no air $\to$ no rust. In Test Tube C (dry air over anhydrous $CaCl_2$), no moisture $\to$ no rust!

Prevention: Galvanisation, Alloys & The Delhi Iron Pillar

• Galvanisation: Coating steel/iron with a thin layer of zinc. Even if the coating is scratched, zinc corrodes preferentially (sacrificial protection).
• Alloys: Homogeneous mixtures of two or more metals or metal+non-metal. Pure iron is soft; adding $0.05\%$ carbon makes it strong; adding $Ni$ and $Cr$ makes stainless steel (hard & rustless). 22-carat gold ($22/24$ gold + $2/24$ Cu/Ag) gives rigidity for jewelry. Amalgam = alloy containing mercury. Brass ($Cu+Zn$), Bronze ($Cu+Sn$), Solder ($Pb+Sn$, low mp for welding wires).
• Delhi Iron Pillar: 8 m tall, 6 tonnes, forged 1600+ years ago near Qutub Minar, possessing an ancient passive surface layer that completely prevents rusting!

In Activity 3.14, why do iron nails in Test Tube B (containing boiled distilled water covered with an oil layer) NOT rust?

Boiling water drives out dissolved air/oxygen, and the floating oil barrier seals out atmospheric air. Without oxygen, iron cannot rust, demonstrating that water alone is insufficient for rusting.

Why does a galvanised iron pipe remain protected against rusting even when its outer zinc coating is scratched or broken?

Zinc has a higher oxidation potential than iron ($Zn > Fe$). In electrochemical contact, zinc acts as a sacrificial anode and oxidises preferentially, preserving the iron even if the zinc film is breached.
