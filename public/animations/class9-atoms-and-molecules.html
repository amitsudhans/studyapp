Welcome, students, to Chapter 7, Work, Energy, and Simple Machines. In Chapters 4 and 6, we studied kinematics and Newton's laws, but analyzing complex or time-varying forces directly can become tricky. Work, energy, and power give us a remarkably simpler framework! Notice first how everyday words differ from physics: in daily life, energy powers walking from food, spinning fans from electricity, and driving cars from fuel, while studying for exams feels like hard work. Scientifically, work has a strict definition. Imagine lifting five-kilogram wheat bags onto a shelf. Lifting three bags by one metre takes three times the force, and lifting one bag by three metres takes force over three times the distance! Therefore, work done by a constant force equals force multiplied by displacement in the direction of the force: W equals F times s. Its SI unit is the joule, where one joule equals one newton times one metre. Work is a scalar quantity—it has magnitude and sign, but no direction. Even Even when force varies along a path, the total area under a Force-Displacement graph equals total work done! Zero, Positive, Negative

Sections 7.1.1 & 7.1.2: Zero Work, Examples 7.1 & 7.2

Slide 2 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

In physics, pushing with all your might does not guarantee positive work! Let us check when scientific work equals zero, positive, or negative. Work is strictly zero in three situations: first, when net applied force is zero; second, when displacement is zero, such as pushing a rigid concrete wall until you are exhausted without moving it an inch; and third, when force acts perpendicular—at ninety degrees—to displacement! For example, when you carry a heavy box horizontally across a room, your upward support force is at ninety degrees to horizontal motion, so your support force does zero work! Now compare positive and negative work. Positive work occurs when force and displacement point in the same direction, like pushing a wheelchair forward. Negative work occurs when force opposes displacement. Look at Example 7.1 with a dumbbell: lifting it upward means upward force and upward displacement, giving positive work; lowering it slowly means your upward holding force opposes downward displacement, giving negative work! In Example 7.2, a goalkeeper applies a two-hundred-newton backward force over zero point one five metres to stop a football, doing minus thirty joules of work on the ball! Remember, negative work removes kinetic energy from a moving object.

Work-Energy Theorem

Sections 7.2 & 7.3: Energy Capacity, Joule, Example 7.3 & Forms

Slide 3 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

What exactly is energy? Scientists define energy as the capacity to do work! A fast cricket ball carries energy because it can knock down wooden wickets on impact; a flowerpot perched on a high balcony stores energy because it can dent the ground if it falls. This brings us to one of physics' greatest bridges—the Work-Energy Theorem: the net work done on an object or system equals the change in its energy! In Meet a Scientist, James Prescott Joule proved that mechanical work and thermal energy are directly interchangeable, earning his name for the SI unit of both work and energy. Watch Example 7.3 on a carrom board: your hand does positive work accelerating the striker, transferring energy to it; the striker hits a white coin, doing positive work on it; and the white coin strikes a black coin, passing energy onward! Beyond motion, Section 7.3 reminds us that energy takes many forms—mechanical, thermal, light, sound, electrical, nuclear, and chemical—and constantly converts between them, such as electrical energy heating water in a geyser or chemical food energy powering muscular movement during sports, walking, and everyday household chores. Energy is never created out of nothing; it simply transfers and transforms.

Kinetic Energy

Section 7.4.1 & Examples 7.4–7.6: K = ½mv² & Jet Landing

Slide 4 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Let us examine Mechanical Energy—energy possessed due to motion or position—starting with Kinetic Energy, denoted by K, the energy of motion! Let us derive its exact formula cleanly. Suppose a force F accelerates mass m from initial velocity u to final velocity v over displacement s. From Chapter 4, v squared equals u squared plus two a s, so s equals v squared minus u squared over two a. Multiply by Newton's second law, F equals m a: work W equals m a times s, which simplifies to half m times v squared minus u squared! Starting from rest at u equals zero, kinetic energy becomes K equals half m v squared! Notice Example 7.4: because speed is squared, doubling a car's speed quadruples its kinetic energy fourfold! In Example 7.5, a zero-point-two-kilogram cricket ball bowled at forty-three metres per second carries half times zero point two times forty-three squared, or one hundred and eighty-four point nine joules. And in Example 7.6, an aircraft carrier's tail-hook exerts a retarding force of three hundred sixty-seven thousand five hundred newtons over one hundred metres to stop a fifteen-thousand-kilogram jet, revealing an initial landing speed of seventy metres per second!

Potential Energy

Section 7.4.2, Activity 7.1 & Example 7.7: Stored Energy U = mgh

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Can a stationary object still hold energy? Yes! Potential energy, denoted by U, is energy stored due to deformation, configuration, or relative position. Stretch a rubber slingshot, bend an archer's wooden bow, or compress a coiled spring—you store elastic potential energy ready to launch a projectile! Similarly, two repelling magnets pushed close together, electric charges, or a stone lifted above Earth store potential energy in their system. Let us focus on gravitational potential energy. In Activity 7.1, drop an iron ball into a tray of soft sand from a height of one metre, then drop it from two metres. The higher drop carves a noticeably deeper crater in the sand, proving greater height stores greater energy! How much energy? Raising mass m steadily to height h requires lifting force equal to weight m g over vertical distance h, so work done—and stored gravitational potential energy—is U equals m g h! Look at Example 7.7: throwing a two-hundred-gram, or zero-point-two-kilogram, ball ten metres high using g equals ten metres per second squared stores zero point two times ten times ten, or twenty joules of gravitational potential energy at its peak! Energy Conservation

Section 7.4.3 & Activity 7.2: Free Fall & Pendulum ME = K + U

Slide 6 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now comes one of the most elegant laws in all of science: the Conservation of Mechanical Energy! In a closed system with only conservative forces like gravity and zero friction, total mechanical energy—the sum of kinetic energy K and potential energy U—remains strictly constant! Let us prove this for free fall from height h. At the top before release, velocity is zero, so K is zero and U equals m g h, giving total energy m g h. After falling for time t, the object drops distance half g t squared, so it loses potential energy equal to half m g squared t squared. Meanwhile, its speed grows to v equals g t, giving kinetic energy half m times g t squared, which is half m g squared t squared! Loss in potential energy equals gain in kinetic energy, keeping K plus U equal to m g h every single instant! Watch Activity 7.2 with a swinging simple pendulum: at the high extreme points P and R, the bob pauses momentarily with maximum potential energy and zero kinetic energy; swinging through the lowest point Q, all that stored potential energy transforms smoothly into maximum kinetic energy!

Examples 7.8 & 7.9: Frictionless Slide Speed & Runaway Truck Escape Ramp

Slide 7 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Conservation of mechanical energy solves tricky real-world engineering problems effortlessly! Look at Example 7.8: a child slides down a curved, frictionless playground slide of vertical height h. Setting potential energy at the top, m g h, equal to kinetic energy at the bottom, half m v squared, mass m cancels out completely—yielding v equals square root of two g h! Amazingly, the child's exit speed depends only on vertical height h, NOT on the slide's curve shape or the child's mass! Now what when friction IS present, like a braking vehicle? Look at Example 7.9: a ten-thousand-kilogram runaway truck barrels down a mountain road at seventy-two kilometres per hour, or twenty metres per second, and enters a thirty-degree upward sand escape ramp! Its initial kinetic energy is half times ten thousand times twenty squared, which equals two million joules. As the truck climbs distance L up the thirty-degree ramp, vertical height rises by L sine thirty degrees, or half L, storing potential energy, while fifty-thousand-newton sand drag removes energy. Solving the complete energy balance shows a minimum ramp length of twenty metres stops the heavy truck safely! Power & Watt

Section 7.5 & Examples 7.10–7.11: Rate of Work, Watt & Horsepower

Slide 8 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Suppose two cranes lift identical steel beams to the same roof: Crane A finishes in ten seconds, while Crane B takes fifty seconds. Both do the exact same work, yet Crane A is far more powerful! Power, denoted by P, measures the rate of doing work—or energy transferred per unit time: P equals W divided by t. Its SI unit is the watt, named after James Watt, where one watt equals one joule per second. In Meet a Scientist, James Watt dramatically improved steam engine efficiency, and introduced the practical unit horsepower to compare engines against workhorses—where one horsepower equals seven hundred and forty-six watts! Let us solve Examples 7.10 and 7.11. In Example 7.10, a weightlifter raises a seventy-five-kilogram barbell by two metres in five seconds using g equals ten metres per second squared. Work done is seventy-five times ten times two, or fifteen hundred joules; dividing by five seconds gives three hundred watts! In Example 7.11, a one-thousand-kilogram car accelerates from rest to twenty metres per second in ten seconds, gaining two hundred thousand joules of kinetic energy—giving an average engine power output of twenty thousand watts, or twenty kilowatts! Always remember that power tells us how fast energy is transferred.

Simple Machines

Sections 7.6 & 7.6.1: Mechanical Advantage & Pulley Systems

Slide 9 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

How did ancient builders lift massive temple stones without electric motors, and how do modern cranes hoist steel girders? They use simple machines! A simple machine is a device that changes either the magnitude or the direction of an applied force to make a task easier—remembering that it NEVER reduces the total work required! We measure a machine's force-multiplying ability by its Mechanical Advantage, or MA, defined as Load divided by Effort. When MA is greater than one, a small effort lifts a heavy load! Start with our first simple machine in Section 7.6.1: the pulley, a grooved wheel with a rope running over it. A single fixed pulley mounted to a ceiling beam simply redirects your pull—you pull downward using your body weight to lift a bucket upward, giving an ideal Mechanical Advantage of one. By contrast, movable pulley systems—where one pulley moves along with the load supported by multiple rope segments—split the load across those ropes, giving a Mechanical Advantage greater than one! That is why construction cranes and building elevators rely on multi-pulley block and tackle systems every day. Remember, smaller effort always comes with pulling a longer length of rope to conserve total mechanical work.

Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Imagine rolling a heavy oil drum onto the back of a truck. Lifting it straight up vertically demands enormous force, yet pushing it up a slanted wooden plank feels surprisingly manageable! That slanted ramp is an inclined plane—a flat surface tilted at an angle to raise heavy loads using a smaller force applied over a longer distance. In Activity 7.3, pull a toy cart up wooden planks set at different steepness angles using a spring balance. As the slope angle becomes gentler, the required pulling force drops steadily, while the ramp length you must pull along grows longer! Because ideal work along the ramp equals work lifting vertically—Effort times ramp length L equals Load times vertical height h—we derive the ideal Mechanical Advantage of an inclined plane cleanly: MA equals Load divided by Effort, which equals ramp length L divided by vertical height h! Dividing length fifty by height thirty gives a Mechanical Advantage of one point six seven—cutting your required lifting effort significantly! Principle of Levers

Section 7.6.3, Activities 7.4–7.5 & Example 7.13: Moments & Seesaws

Slide 11 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Give me a place to stand, and I shall move the Earth—so spoke ancient Greek thinker Archimedes about the lever! A lever is a rigid bar free to pivot about a fixed turning point called the fulcrum. In Activities 7.4 and 7.5, balance a wooden ruler over a pencil fulcrum to lift a heavy stapler or stack of coins using just a light finger tap! Every lever has three key features: the Fulcrum, the Load sitting at distance Load Arm from the fulcrum, and the Effort applied at distance Effort Arm from the fulcrum. When balanced horizontally, the lever obeys the Principle of Moments: Effort times Effort Arm equals Load times Load Arm, written as F1 times d1 equals F2 times d2! Rearranging this gives the Mechanical Advantage of a lever: MA equals Effort Arm L1 divided by Load Arm L2. Look at Example 7.13 on a playground seesaw: if a thirty-kilogram child sits one metre from the central fulcrum, where must a fifteen-kilogram child sit to balance? Half the weight needs double the arm—so fifteen times two equals thirty times one, placing the lighter child two metres out from the centre pivot to achieve perfect rotational balance!

Classes of Levers

Section 7.6.3 & Table 7.2: Class I, Class II & Class III Levers

Slide 12 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Look around your home, toolbox, and kitchen—almost every hand tool is a lever! Scientists sort all levers into three neat classes in Table 7.2 based on one simple rule: which component sits IN THE MIDDLE between the other two? Remember the mnemonic F-L-E for Classes one, two, and three! In a Class I lever, the Fulcrum is in the middle, with Load on one side and Effort on the other—examples include a playground seesaw, a crowbar prying a rock, scissors, and pliers. Depending on where the fulcrum sits, Class I can multiply force or speed. In a Class II lever, the Load sits in the middle between the Fulcrum at one end and the Effort at the far end! Think of a gardener's wheelbarrow, a lemon squeezer, or a bottle opener: because the Effort Arm is always longer than the Load Arm, Class II levers ALWAYS give Mechanical Advantage greater than one! Finally, in a Class III lever, Effort is applied in the middle—like kitchen tongs, tweezers, a broom, or a boat oar—giving MA less than one to gain precision and speed! Long before modern electrical grids reached high mountain valleys, communities across the Himalayas engineered an ingenious clean-energy machine: the traditional gharat, also called a panchakki or water mill! In Bridging Science and Society, see how the exact same physics powers both ancient gharats and giant modern hydroelectric dams. Mountain streams flowing down steep slopes carry huge gravitational potential energy at higher elevations. Channeled through a wooden chute called a flume, the falling water accelerates, converting potential energy into rapid kinetic energy! When that fast water jet strikes the angled wooden blades of a horizontal water wheel below the millhouse, it does positive work, spinning a vertical shaft connected directly to a heavy circular grinding stone above—milling wheat, maize, and spices with zero smoke or fossil fuel! Modern hydroelectric power stations scale up this exact chain: high dams store vast reservoirs of gravitational potential energy, penstock pipes rush water onto steel turbines to create rotational kinetic energy, and generators convert that motion into clean electricity lighting millions of homes across India! Both traditional gharats and modern dams prove how timeless physics principles power sustainable communities while protecting mountain rivers and clean air for future generations across India.

Let us wrap up Chapter 7 with our complete At a Glance toolkit! First, work W equals F times s measured in joules—positive when force aligns with displacement, negative when opposite, and zero when F is zero, s is zero, or force is perpendicular at ninety degrees.
