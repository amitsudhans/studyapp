Algebraic Equations vs Differential Equations

Algebraic Equations:
x² - 3x + 3 = 0, \quad \sin x + \cos x = 0, \quad x + y = 7

Involve static variables only. Solutions are discrete numbers (real or complex).

Differential Equation:
x \frac{dy}{dx} + y = 0 \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0

Involves independent variable $x$, dependent variable $y$, and derivatives $\frac{dy}{dx}, \frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$. Solutions are functions (integral curves)!

Ordinary vs Partial & Notation Protocol

Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE):

Involves derivatives of dependent variable with respect to only one independent variable.

Standard Derivative Notations:
First derivative: $\frac{dy}{dx} = y'$
Second derivative: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = y''$
Third derivative: $\frac{d^3y}{dx^3} = y'''$
$n$-th derivative: $\frac{d^ny}{dx^n} = y_n$ (supersuffix notation)

§ 9.2.1 Core Metric Order of Highest Derivative Present in the Equation

Order of a Differential Equation

The order of a differential equation is defined as the order of the highest-order derivative of the dependent variable with respect to the independent variable involved in the equation.

Order 1 (First Order):
\frac{dy}{dx} = e^x

Highest derivative is first order $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

Order 2 (Second Order):
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0

Highest derivative is second order $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$.

Order 3 (Third Order):
\frac{d^3y}{dx^3} + x^2 \left(\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}\right)^3 = 0

Highest derivative is $y'''$. The power 3 on $y''$ does NOT affect order!

Section 9.2.2 • Degree Part - 2

9.2.2 Degree of a Differential Equation: Polynomial Condition & Traps

Part - 2

Highest Power of Highest Derivative, The Polynomial in Derivatives Prerequisite, and Undefined Degrees

§ 9.2.2 Core Metric Polynomial in Derivatives Prerequisite & Exam Traps

Degree of a Differential Equation: The Polynomial Test

By the degree of a differential equation, when it is a polynomial equation in derivatives, we mean the highest power (positive integral index) of the highest order derivative involved.

When Degree IS Defined:

(y''')^2 + 2(y'')^3 - y' + y = 0
Polynomial in $y''', y'', y'$. Highest derivative $y'''$ has power 2 ⟹ Order = 3, Degree = 2.
y'' + (y')^2 + 2y = 0
Highest derivative is $y''$ with power 1 ⟹ Order = 2, Degree = 1.

When Degree is NOT Defined:

\frac{dy}{dx} + \sin\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = 0
Derivative trapped inside $\sin(\dots)$! Not a polynomial in derivatives ⟹ Degree NOT Defined.
y''' + y^2 + e^{y'} = 0
Derivative trapped in exponential $e^{y'}$ ⟹ Degree NOT Defined.

Part - 2

§ 9.3 Solution Types General Solution (Primitive) vs Particular Solution (Integral Curves)

General and Particular Solutions

A solution of a differential equation is a function $\phi(x)$ that reduces the equation to an identity. The graph of a solution is called an integral curve.

General Solution (Primitive)

n Constants

A solution which contains as many arbitrary constants as the order of the differential equation.

For $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} + y = 0$ (Order 2):
y = a \sin(x + b) \quad \text{or} \quad y = A \cos x + B \sin x
Contains exactly 2 arbitrary parameters ($a, b$ or $A, B$). Represents a family of sinusoids.

Particular Solution

0 Constants

A solution obtained from the general solution by assigning specific numerical values to arbitrary constants based on boundary/initial conditions.

Setting $a = 2, b = \pi/4$:
y = 2 \sin\left(x + \frac{\pi}{4}\right)
Free from arbitrary constants! Identifies a unique single curve from the family.

9.4.1 Method 1: Differential Equations with Variables Separable

Part - 2

§ 9.4.1 Method 1 Leibniz's 1691 Separation of Variables Protocol

Differential Equations with Variables Separable

If the right-hand side can be factored as $F(x, y) = h(y) \cdot g(x)$, the differential equation is of variable separable type.

The 3-Step Separation Protocol

\frac{dy}{dx} = h(y) \cdot g(x)
1. Separate: $\frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = g(x)\,dx$
2. Integrate both sides:
\int \frac{1}{h(y)}\,dy = \int g(x)\,dx + C
H(y) = G(x) + C
Where $H(y)$ is anti-derivative of $1/h(y)$ and $G(x)$ of $g(x)$.

Footnote on Leibniz Notation (Page 309)

The notation $\frac{dy}{dx}$ due to Leibniz is extremely flexible and useful. We can treat $dy$ and $dx$ as separate algebraic entities, giving clean, elegant expressions to calculus operations!

e.g. $\frac{dy}{dx} = e^{x+y} = e^x e^y \implies e^{-y}\,dy = e^x\,dx$

Example 9: Continuous Compound Interest

Principal $P$ increases continuously at 5% per year. Find time to double Rs 1000:

9.4.2 Method 2: Homogeneous Functions of Degree n

Part - 2

Definition: F(λx, λy) = λⁿ F(x, y), Testing Homogeneity, and Degree Zero Representation g(y/x)

§ 9.4.2 Method 2 Homogeneous Functions & Degree Zero Criteria

Homogeneous Functions & Equations

A function $F(x, y)$ is homogeneous of degree $n$ if $F(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^n F(x, y)$. A differential equation is homogeneous when its right-hand side has degree zero!

Testing Homogeneity: The $\lambda$ Scaling Test

1. $F_1(x, y) = y^2 + 2xy$:
F_1(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^2(y^2 + 2xy) = \lambda^2 F_1 \quad (n = 2)
2. $F_2(x, y) = 2x - 3y$:
F_2(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \lambda^1(2x - 3y) = \lambda^1 F_2 \quad (n = 1)
3. $F_3(x, y) = \cos(y/x)$:
F_3(\lambda x, \lambda y) = \cos(\lambda y / \lambda x) = \lambda^0 F_3 \quad (n = 0)
4. $F_4(x, y) = \sin x + \cos y$:
\ne \lambda^n F_4 \implies \text{NOT homogeneous!}

Homogeneous Differential Equation Condition

A differential equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = F(x, y)$ is called homogeneous if $F(x, y)$ is a homogeneous function of degree zero.

\frac{dy}{dx} = g\left(\frac{y}{x}\right) \quad \text{or} \quad \frac{dx}{dy} = h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)

Because all powers cancel to zero, the function depends solely on the ratio $y/x$ or $x/y$!

Homogeneous Protocol The Substitution y = vx & Product Rule Differential

Solving via Substitution: $y = vx$

Substituting $y = vx$ reduces any homogeneous equation $\frac{dy}{dx} = g(y/x)$ into a separable equation in $v$ and $x$.

The 4-Step Homogeneous Protocol

Step 1: Substitute $y = vx$
\frac{dy}{dx} = v + x\frac{dv}{dx}
Step 2: Equate to $g(v)$
v + x\frac{dv}{dx} = g(v) ⟹ x\frac{dv}{dx} = g(v) - v
Step 3: Separate Variables
\frac{dv}{g(v) - v} = \frac{dx}{x}
Step 4: Integrate & Replace $v = y/x$
\int \frac{dv}{g(v) - v} = \log|x| + C

Reverse Homogeneous Form dx/dy = h(x/y) & Substitution x = vy (Example 12)

The Reverse Homogeneous Form: $\frac{dx}{dy} = h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)$

When equations feature functions of $x/y$ (like $e^{x/y}$), solving for $\frac{dx}{dy}$ and putting $x = vy$ is vastly simpler than using $y = vx$.

The $x = vy$ Protocol

If $\frac{dx}{dy} = h\left(\frac{x}{y}\right)$, put $x = vy$:
\frac{dx}{dy} = v + y\frac{dv}{dy}
v + y\frac{dv}{dy} = h(v) ⟹ y\frac{dv}{dy} = h(v) - v
\frac{dv}{h(v) - v} = \frac{dy}{y}

Example 12: $2y e^{x/y} dx + (y - 2x e^{x/y}) dy = 0$

Given $x = 0$ when $y = 1$:

\frac{dx}{dy} = \frac{2x e^{x/y} - y}{2y e^{x/y}}
Put $x = vy ⟹ 2e^v\,dv = -\frac{dy}{y}$
2e^v = -\log|y| + C ⟹ 2e^{x/y} + \log|y| = C
Apply $x=0, y=1: 2(1) + 0 = C ⟹ C = 2$
2 e^{x/y} + \log|y| = 2

9.4.3 Method 3: First Order Linear Differential Equations

Part - 2

§ 9.4.3 Method 3 First Order Linear Form 1: dy/dx + Py = Q & Integrating Factor

First Order Linear Differential Equations

A differential equation of the form $\frac{dy}{dx} + P(x) y = Q(x)$ is solved by multiplying through by the Integrating Factor $\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P\,dx}$.

Derivation of the Integrating Factor (Page 323)

Multiply by $g(x)$: $g(x) y' + P g(x) y = Q g(x)$
We desire LHS = $\frac{d}{dx}[y \cdot g(x)] = g(x) y' + y g'(x)$
⟹ $g'(x) = P(x) g(x) ⟹ \frac{g'}{g} = P(x)$
\log(g(x)) = \int P\,dx ⟹ g(x) = e^{\int P\,dx}
\text{Integrating Factor (I.F.) } = e^{\int P\,dx}

Universal Solution Formula & Example 15

y \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int (Q \cdot \text{I.F.})\,dx + C
Example 15: $x \frac{dy}{dx} + 2y = x^2$
Divide by x: $y' + \frac{2}{x}y = x ⟹ P = 2/x, Q = x$
\text{I.F.} = e^{\int (2/x)dx} = e^{2\log x} = x^2
y \cdot x^2 = \int x \cdot x^2\,dx + C = \frac{x^4}{4} + C
y = \frac{x^2}{4} + C x^{-2}

Linear Form 2 dx/dy + P₁x = Q₁ & Integrating Factors in y

Linear Form 2: $\frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y) x = Q_1(y)$

When $x$ is the dependent variable and $y$ is the independent variable, the equation is linear in $x$.

Form 2 Solution Protocol

\frac{dx}{dy} + P_1(y) x = Q_1(y)
\text{I.F.} = e^{\int P_1(y)\,dy}
x \cdot (\text{I.F.}) = \int (Q_1 \cdot \text{I.F.})\,dy + C

Notice the symmetric duality: interchange $x \leftrightarrow y$ and $P \leftrightarrow P_1$!

Example 16: $y dx - (x + 2y^2) dy = 0$

Divide by $y dy$: $\frac{dx}{dy} - \frac{1}{y}x = 2y$
P_1 = -1/y, \quad Q_1 = 2y
\text{I.F.} = e^{\int (-1/y)dy} = e^{-\log y} = \frac{1}{y}
x \left(\frac{1}{y}\right) = \int (2y)\left(\frac{1}{y}\right)\,dy + C = 2y + C
x = 2y^2 + Cy

Historical Note: 350 Years of Differential Equations

Tracing the birth and rapid evolution of differential equations from Leibniz and the Bernoulli brothers to Lagrange and Henri Poincaré.

Date of Birth: November 11, 1675

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz first penned the identity $\int y\,dy = \frac{1}{2}y^2$, creating both $\int$ and $dy$. Within 25 years, Leibniz discovered separation of variables (1691), homogeneous methods (1692), and first-order linear solutions!

John Bernoulli & $x^2 y'' = 2y$ (1715)

In a famous 1715 letter to Leibniz, John Bernoulli revealed that the innocent equation $x^2 y'' = 2y$ generates three diverse classes of curves: parabolas, hyperbolas, and cubic curves, initiating modern qualitative analysis!
