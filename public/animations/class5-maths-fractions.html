Equal Wholes

Why Comparing Fractions Requires Identical Wholes (Chocolates & Parathas)

A fraction represents a part of a whole. But here is the fundamental rule of fractions: to compare two fractions fairly, the whole items from which they are taken must be identical in size!

The Paratha Dilemma

If Kabir eats 1/2 of a small baby paratha and Ananya eats 1/2 of a large family paratha, do they eat the same amount? No! Ananya eats much more, even though both fractions say 1/2, because the whole parathas were different sizes!

Chocolate Bar Sharing

When two friends share from the same identical chocolate bar, 1/4 of that bar is truly equal for both, and 1/2 is always bigger than 1/4.

Always remember: a fraction is a relative comparison. Always check the size of the whole before comparing fractional shares!

Grid Shading

Visualizing Fractions on Square Grids (Grids A, B, and C)

Square grids are wonderful geometric tools for visualizing fractions. When a square grid is divided into equal smaller squares, each tiny square is a unit fraction of the whole grid.

Grid A (16 Squares)

Shade 1/8 in Red:
1/8 of 16 = 2 squares

Grid B (24 Squares)

Shade 1/6 in Blue:
1/6 of 24 = 4 squares

Grid C (36 Squares)

Shade 1/12 in Yellow:
1/12 of 36 = 3 squares

To find a fraction of a grid: multiply the total number of squares by the numerator, then divide by the denominator. Notice how different patterns can represent the same fraction!

Piece Relations

Discovering Equivalence and Part-to-Part Proportions

By comparing different pieces from our Fraction Kit, we uncover precise mathematical relationships connecting halves, fourths, eighths, and sixteenths.

Fitting Halves and Fourths

• Two 1/4 pieces = 1/2 (2/4 = 1/2)
• Four 1/4 pieces = 1 Whole (4/4 = 1)
• Four 1/8 pieces = 1/2 (4/8 = 1/2)

Fitting Eighths and Sixteenths

• Two 1/8 pieces = 1/4 (2/8 = 1/4)
• Two 1/16 pieces = 1/8 (2/16 = 1/8)
• Eight 1/8 pieces = 1 Whole (8/8 = 1)

Notice the pattern: as the denominator doubles, the size of each piece is halved. Therefore, you need twice as many pieces to cover the same length!

Equivalent Fractions

Visualizing Different Names for the Exact Same Shaded Region

Imagine a chocolate bar divided into 3 equal columns. If you shade 1 column, you have shaded 1/3. Now draw a horizontal cut dividing the bar into 2 equal rows: you now have 6 smaller rectangles, and 2 are shaded! The shaded region has not changed at all, but its name is now 2/6.

The Equivalence Chain
1/3 = 2/6 = 3/9 = 4/12 = 5/15

Rule: Multiply or divide both numerator and denominator by the same non-zero number to generate equivalent fractions.

Equivalent fractions look different because they have different numbers, but they represent the exact same value and area.

Equal Names

Finding Missing Numerators and Denominators in Equivalence Equations

To find a missing numerator or denominator in an equivalent fraction statement, find the multiplier connecting the known terms and apply it to the other term.

Example 1: 2/5 = ?/10

Look at the denominators: from 5 to 10, we multiplied by 2 (5 × 2 = 10).
Therefore, multiply the numerator by 2 as well:
2 × 2 = 4.
So, 2/5 = 4/10!

Example 2: 3/4 = 9/?

Look at the numerators: from 3 to 9, we multiplied by 3 (3 × 3 = 9).
Therefore, multiply the denominator by 3 as well:
4 × 3 = 12.
So, 3/4 = 9/12!

Cross-multiplication check: in any true equivalent fraction pair a/b = c/d, the cross products are equal: a × d = b × c!

Same Denominator

Comparing Fractions When Part Sizes are Equal (Chikki Slices)

When two fractions have the same denominator, it means the whole has been divided into parts of the exact same size.

Comparing 1/3 and 2/3 of a Chikki

Imagine a crunchy peanut chikki divided into 3 equal pieces. Each piece is 1/3.
• Aarav gets 1 piece = 1/3
• Meera gets 2 pieces = 2/3
Since each piece is identical in size, having 2 pieces is clearly more than having 1 piece!
Therefore: 2/3 > 1/3 (or 1/3 < 2/3).

Golden Rule for Same Denominator: Simply compare the numerators! The fraction with the greater numerator is the larger fraction. (e.g., 5/8 > 3/8 because 5 > 3).

Same Numerator

Comparing Fractions When Part Counts are Equal but Sizes Differ

What happens when two fractions have the same numerator but different denominators, such as comparing 4/6 and 4/5 of a paratha?

Part Size Comparison

A paratha cut into 5 equal pieces has larger slices than a paratha cut into 6 equal pieces.
Therefore, 1/5 piece > 1/6 piece.

Taking 4 Pieces of Each

Taking 4 pieces of the smaller size (4 × 1/6) is less than taking 4 pieces of the larger size (4 × 1/5).
Therefore: 4/6 < 4/5 (or 4/5 > 4/6)!

Golden Rule for Same Numerator: The fraction with the smaller denominator has larger individual pieces, making it the larger fraction! (e.g., 3/4 > 3/8 because fourths are bigger than eighths).

"Now here is a fascinating question that surprises many students: what happens when two fractions have the exact same numerator, but different denominators? For example, which is bigger: four-sixths of a paratha, or four-fifths of an identical paratha? Let us think carefully about the meaning of the denominator. Greater Than One

Discovering Fractions Beyond One Whole (5/2, 9/4, and Sharing Parathas)

Can a fraction be larger than 1 whole? Yes! In everyday life, when we share multiple items, we frequently encounter quantities greater than 1.

Sharing 5 Halves of Paratha

Suppose you have 5 half-parathas (5 × 1/2). That is 5/2.
• Two halves make 1 whole paratha.
• Another two halves make a 2nd whole paratha.
• 1 half is left over.
So 5/2 = 2 whole parathas and 1/2!

Improper Fractions

When the numerator is greater than or equal to the denominator (like 5/2, 9/4, 7/3), it is called an improper fraction, and its value is greater than or equal to 1.

Fractions where the numerator is smaller than the denominator (like 1/2, 3/4) are called proper fractions, and they are always less than 1.

Mixed Numbers

Plotting Improper Fractions on the Number Line and Converting to Mixed Numbers

A mixed number combines a whole number and a proper fraction together (e.g., 2 1/2, read as two and a half).

Conversion Steps: Improper Fraction to Mixed Number

Divide the numerator by the denominator:
• Quotient becomes the Whole Number.
• Remainder becomes the new Numerator.
• Divisor remains the Denominator.
Example: Convert 9/4:
9 ÷ 4 = 2 with remainder 1 → 9/4 = 2 1/4 (two and one-fourth).

Number Line Position: 2 1/4 lies between 2 and 3 on the number line, exactly one-quarter of the way past 2!

Benchmark One

Using Whole Number 1 as a Benchmark to Compare Fractions

When comparing two fractions, you do not always need to calculate common denominators! Often, you can simply compare both fractions to the benchmark of 1 (a whole).

Less Than 1 Whole

If numerator < denominator, the fraction is less than 1.
Example: 7/8 < 1 (needs 1/8 more to make a whole).

Greater Than 1 Whole

If numerator > denominator, the fraction is greater than 1.
Example: 8/6 > 1 (equals 1 2/6, more than a whole).

Instant Comparison: Since 7/8 is less than 1, and 8/6 is greater than 1, we know immediately that 7/8 < 8/6 without any common denominator calculations!

Another powerful benchmark is one-half (1/2). To check whether a fraction is less than, equal to, or greater than 1/2, compare its numerator to half of its denominator.

Equal to 1/2

Numerator = Half of Denominator
• 3/6 (3 is half of 6)
• 4/8 (4 is half of 8)

Greater than 1/2

Numerator > Half of Denominator
• 5/8 (5 > 4)
• 7/10 (7 > 5)

Less than 1/2

Numerator < Half of Denominator
• 2/6 (2 < 3)
• 3/8 (3 < 4)

Ant Parade

Word Problem: Calculating the Total Length of 16 Marching Ants

In the Try This section of Chapter 2, students encounter a charming nature puzzle: A line of 16 tiny ants is walking one after the other in a straight parade. If each ant measures 1/4 cm in length, what is the total length of the entire ant parade?

Mathematical Solution Steps

Each ant = 1/4 cm.
Total number of ants = 16.
Total parade length = 16 × 1/4 cm = 16/4 cm.
Now divide 16 by 4:
16 ÷ 4 = 4 cm!

Number Line Hop: On a centimetre ruler, 4 ants make 1 cm (4 × 1/4 = 1). The next 4 ants make 2 cm, the next 4 make 3 cm, and the final 4 ants reach exactly 4 cm!

Family Riddle

Fun with Relations: Deductive Logic and Relationship Riddles

In the Fun with Relations section, the textbook presents a classic Indian family logic riddle: “If the only brother of your father's sister had a child, what would be the child's relationship with you?”

Step-by-Step Logical Deduction
  1. Step 1: “Your father's sister” is your paternal aunt (often called Bua in Hindi).
  2. Step 2: Who is the “only brother” of your father's sister? Since your father is her brother, and she has only one brother, that only brother is your father himself!
  3. Step 3: Now, the riddle asks about “the child of your father”. Who is your father's child? You yourself, or your brother/sister (sibling)!

Just like simplifying a fraction step-by-step to its lowest terms, analyzing relationship riddles step-by-step leads directly to an elegant, logical answer.
