Introduction: Sour vs Bitter, Natural Indicators & The Lichen Litmus Dye

"“Acids turn blue litmus red; bases turn red litmus blue. Litmus is a natural purple dye extracted from lichens.”"

From ancient times, taste served as the earliest chemical test: acids taste sour, while bases taste bitter. When overeating causes acidity, we take a mild base like baking soda because acids and bases cancel out each other's effects.

Everyday Acidity & The Neutralisation Remedy

Overeating triggers excess hydrochloric acid in the stomach. Choosing between lemon juice (acidic), vinegar (acidic), or baking soda solution (basic) relies on the principle that a base nullifies the effect of an acid.

Natural Indicators

• Litmus: Extracted from lichens (division Thallophyta). Its neutral colour is purple. Turns red in acid and blue in base.
• Turmeric: Yellow natural indicator. A curry stain on white cloth turns reddish-brown when scrubbed with alkaline soap, and turns yellow again when rinsed with water.
• Other Plant Indicators: Red cabbage leaves, coloured petals of Hydrangea, Petunia, and Geranium.

Synthetic Indicators

• Phenolphthalein: Colourless in acidic solution, turns pink in basic solution.
• Methyl Orange: Turns red in acidic solution and yellow in basic solution.

Reactions with Metals: Hydrogen Gas, Pop Test & Sodium Zincate (Activities 2.3 & 2.4)

"“Metal + Acid → Salt + Hydrogen gas. Hydrogen burns with a characteristic pop sound.”"

When active metals react with acids, they displace hydrogen atoms as $H_2$ gas. Certain amphoteric metals like zinc can even react with strong bases to form unique complex salts.

Activity 2.3: Zinc Granules + Dilute Sulphuric Acid

• Add dilute sulphuric acid ($H_2SO_4$) to zinc granules in a test tube.
• Gas bubbles evolve on the zinc surface.
• Pass the gas through soap solution $\implies$ soap bubbles filled with gas rise into the air.
• Bringing a burning candle near a bubble makes it burst with a sharp 'pop' sound:
$$\text{Zn}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) + \mathbf{\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow}$$
General Rule: $$\mathbf{\text{Acid} + \text{Metal} \longrightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{Hydrogen gas}}$$

Activity 2.4: Reaction of Zinc with Strong Base ($NaOH$)

Warm zinc granules with sodium hydroxide solution:
$$2\text{NaOH}(aq) + \text{Zn}(s) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2(aq)} + \mathbf{\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow}$$
The salt formed is Sodium Zincate. Note: Such reactions with bases are not possible with all metals; only amphoteric metals like zinc and aluminium exhibit this behaviour.

Metal Carbonates & Hydrogencarbonates: The Lime Water Milkiness Test (Activity 2.5)

"“Carbon dioxide turns lime water milky due to insoluble calcium carbonate; excess CO2 dissolves the milkiness!”"

Acids decompose carbonates and hydrogencarbonates with brisk effervescence, producing carbon dioxide gas that exhibits a two-stage reaction with lime water.

Activity 2.5: Reactions in Test Tubes A & B

• Test Tube A (Sodium carbonate):
$$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow 2\text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \mathbf{\text{CO}_2(g) \uparrow}$$
• Test Tube B (Sodium hydrogencarbonate):
$$\text{NaHCO}_3(s) + \text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \mathbf{\text{CO}_2(g) \uparrow}$$
General Law: $$\text{Metal Carbonate/Hydrogencarbonate} + \text{Acid} \longrightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Stage 1: Lime Water Turns Milky

Passing $CO_2$ through clear lime water [calcium hydroxide, $Ca(OH)_2$] forms an insoluble white precipitate of calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$):
$$\text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq) + \text{CO}_2(g) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3(s) \downarrow} \text{ (milky white)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Stage 2: Excess $CO_2$ Clears the Milkiness

On passing excess $CO_2$, the insoluble $CaCO_3$ converts into water-soluble calcium hydrogencarbonate, making the solution clear again:
$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Ca(HCO}_3\text{)}_2(aq)} \text{ (soluble)}$$
Limestone, chalk, and marble are all natural allotropic forms of $CaCO_3$.

Neutralisation Reactions (Activity 2.6) & The Basic Nature of Metal Oxides (Activity 2.7)

"“Base + Acid → Salt + Water. Metallic oxides react with acids to form salt and water, proving they are basic oxides.”"

Acids and bases nullify each other's effects in neutralisation. Because metal oxides react with acids to form salt and water, they are classified as basic oxides.

Activity 2.6: The Phenolphthalein Indicator Cycle

• Take 2 mL dilute $NaOH$ in a test tube and add 2 drops of phenolphthalein $\implies$ turns bright pink.
• Add dilute $HCl$ drop by drop $\implies$ pink colour suddenly disappears (neutralised!).
• Add a few drops of $NaOH$ $\implies$ pink colour reappears!
$$\mathbf{\text{Acid} + \text{Base} \longrightarrow \text{Salt} + \text{Water}}$$
$$\text{HCl}(aq) + \text{NaOH}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{NaCl}(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Activity 2.7: Copper Oxide + Dilute Hydrochloric Acid

• Take black copper(II) oxide ($CuO$) in a beaker and add dilute $HCl$ while stirring.
• Black powder dissolves, forming a vibrant blue-green solution of copper(II) chloride ($CuCl_2$):
$$\mathbf{\text{CuO}(s)} \text{ (black)} + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{CuCl}_2(aq)} \text{ (blue-green)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Conclusion: Metal Oxides are Basic

Since metallic oxides react with acids to produce salt and water (identical to a base + acid neutralisation), metallic oxides are basic oxides.

Sections 2.1.6 & 2.2 • Mobile Ions & Acidity Slide 6 of 21

Non-Metallic Oxides & Electrical Conductivity: Why Glucose and Alcohol Fail to Conduct (Activity 2.8)

"“All acids contain hydrogen, but not all compounds containing hydrogen are acids! Ions conduct current.”"

While non-metallic oxides are acidic in nature, having hydrogen atoms is not enough to make a substance an acid: it must dissociate into free mobile $H^+$ ions in water.

Non-Metallic Oxides are Acidic Oxides

Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$, non-metallic oxide) reacts with calcium hydroxide [$Ca(OH)_2$, a base] to produce calcium carbonate (salt) and water:
$$\text{CO}_2(g) + \text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq) \longrightarrow \text{CaCO}_3(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
Since non-metallic oxides react with bases to give salt and water, non-metallic oxides are acidic in nature.

Activity 2.8: The Electric Conductivity Circuit

• Connect two iron nails on a rubber cork to a 6V battery through a bulb and switch.
• In Dilute $HCl$ & $H_2SO_4$: The bulb glows brightly! Acids ionise into free mobile ions [$H^+$ and anions like $Cl^-, SO_4^{2-}$] that carry electric charge.
• In Glucose ($C_6H_{12}O_6$) & Alcohol ($C_2H_5OH$): The bulb does not glow! Although they contain hydrogen atoms, they do not ionise to produce free $H^+$ ions.

The Fundamental Principle

Acidic behaviour is strictly due to the presence of free, mobile hydrated hydrogen ions [$H^+(aq)$] in solution.

Section 2.2.1 • Water is Essential Slide 7 of 21

Dry vs Moist HCl Gas (Activity 2.9) & Hydronium Ion Formation ($H_3O^+$)

"“Hydrogen ions cannot exist alone: H+ + H2O → H3O+ (Hydronium ion).”"

Acids manifest their acidic powers ONLY in the presence of water. Dry $HCl$ gas has zero effect on dry blue litmus paper!

Activity 2.9: Generating HCl Gas

• React solid $NaCl$ with concentrated $H_2SO_4$ in a dry test tube:
$$2\text{NaCl}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(l) \longrightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4(s) + 2\mathbf{\text{HCl}(g) \uparrow}$$
• Hold dry blue litmus paper near the escaping gas $\implies$ No colour change!
• Hold moist (wet) blue litmus paper near the gas $\implies$ Turns bright red!

Why Does Water Matter?

Separation of $H^+$ from $HCl$ molecules cannot occur in the absence of water. Polar water molecules pull the $H^+$ ion away from chlorine.

The Hydronium Ion ($H_3O^+$)

A bare proton ($H^+$) has an immense charge density and cannot exist independently in solution. It instantly coordinates with a lone pair on a water molecule to form the hydronium ion:
$$\text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+} + \text{Cl}^-$$
$$\text{H}^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{H}_3\text{O}^+}$$
Hydrogen ions in solution are always written as $\text{H}^+(aq)$ or $\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$.

Section 2.2.1 • Laboratory Safety & Heat Slide 8 of 21

Alkalis & The Dilution Hazard: Why NEVER Add Water to Acid (Activity 2.10)

"“Always add acid slowly to water with constant stirring. NEVER add water to concentrated acid!”"

Dissolving concentrated acids or bases in water generates violent quantities of heat. Understanding dilution physics is vital to laboratory safety.

What is an Alkali?

Bases generate hydroxide ions ($OH^-$) in water:
$$\text{NaOH}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{Na}^+(aq) + \mathbf{\text{OH}^-(aq)}$$
$$\text{KOH}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{K}^+(aq) + \mathbf{\text{OH}^-(aq)}$$
Rule: All alkalis are bases, but all bases are not alkalis (e.g., $Cu(OH)_2$ is a base but insoluble in water, hence not an alkali).

The Dangerous Dilution Rule (Activity 2.10)

Dissolving concentrated sulphuric acid or nitric acid in water is immensely exothermic.
• Correct Procedure: Add acid slowly to water in small streams with constant stirring.
• Fatal Error: If water is poured into concentrated acid, the sudden massive local heat will vaporize water instantly, causing boiling acid to splash out onto your face and hands, shattering the glass container!

Dilution Defined

Mixing an acid or base with water decreases the concentration of ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$ or $\text{OH}^-$) per unit volume. This process is called dilution.

Section 2.3 • The Sörensen Scale Slide 9 of 21

Universal Indicator & The pH Scale: Quantifying Hydrogen Ion Concentration

"“The 'p' in pH stands for 'potenz' in German, meaning power. pH = -log[H+].”"

While litmus only tells us whether a solution is acidic or basic, the Universal Indicator and the pH Scale measure exactly how strong an acid or base is.

What is Universal Indicator?

A universal indicator is a calibrated mixture of several organic indicators. It exhibits a continuous spectrum of distinct colours corresponding to specific hydrogen ion concentrations from pH 0 to 14.

The pH Scale (0 to 14)

Developed by Danish biochemist S. P. L. Sörensen in 1909. 'p' stands for potenz (German for power).
• Neutral Solution: $\text{pH} = 7$ (e.g., pure water). $[H^+] = [OH^-]$.
• Acidic Solution: $\text{pH} < 7$. Lower the pH, higher the $H^+$ concentration (stronger acid).
• Basic Solution: $\text{pH} > 7$. Higher the pH, higher the $OH^-$ concentration (stronger base).

Strong vs Weak Acids

• Strong Acids: Completely ionise in water to yield a high concentration of $H^+$ ions (e.g., $HCl, H_2SO_4, HNO_3$).
• Weak Acids: Only partially ionise in water, yielding fewer $H^+$ ions (e.g., acetic acid $CH_3COOH$, carbonic acid $H_2CO_3$).

Section 2.3.1 • Biological & Environmental pH Slide 10 of 21

Everyday pH: Living Organisms (7.0–7.8), Acid Rain (< 5.6) & Backyard Soil (Activity 2.12)

"“When rain water pH drops below 5.6, it is called acid rain, threatening aquatic life and river ecosystems.”"

Biological systems function within a knife-edge pH window. Atmospheric pollution causes acid rain, while soil pH dictates plant fertility.

Human Body pH Range

Our bodies work optimally within a narrow pH range of 7.0 to 7.8. Human blood is slightly alkaline at $\approx 7.4$. Even minor deviations can disrupt cellular enzymes and metabolism.

Acid Rain ($pH < 5.6$)

Industrial emissions of sulphur dioxide ($SO_2$) and nitrogen oxides ($NO_x$) dissolve in cloud droplets to form sulphuric and nitric acids. When rainwater has a pH lower than 5.6, it is called acid rain.
Impact: Lowers the pH of rivers and lakes, making the survival of aquatic organisms (fish, phytoplankton) difficult.

Soil pH & Agricultural Treatment (Activity 2.12)

Plants require a specific neutral soil pH (around 6.5–7.5) to absorb minerals.
• If soil is too acidic (due to excessive chemical fertilizers or acid rain), farmers treat it with bases: quick lime ($CaO$), slaked lime [$Ca(OH)_2$], or chalk ($CaCO_3$).
• If soil is too alkaline, organic manure/compost is added to release acids.

Section 2.3.1 • Human Health & Warfare Slide 11 of 21

Everyday pH: Stomach Digestion, Tooth Decay (< 5.5) & Chemical Warfare of Stings

"“Tooth decay begins when mouth pH falls below 5.5. Nettle and bee stings inject methanoic acid; baking soda provides relief.”"

From digestion in our stomachs to bacterial tooth decay and defensive stings in nature, acid-base neutralisation governs human health and survival.

1. pH in Our Digestive System

The stomach gastric glands secrete hydrochloric acid ($HCl$, pH $\sim 1.2$ to 2.0) to activate the enzyme pepsin. Overeating causes excess acid secretion, leading to hyperacidity, burning, and pain.
• Remedy: Antacids (mild bases) like Milk of Magnesia [$Mg(OH)_2$] or baking soda ($NaHCO_3$) neutralise excess acid.

2. pH Change as the Cause of Tooth Decay

Tooth enamel is composed of calcium hydroxyapatite (crystalline calcium phosphate), the hardest substance in the human body. When oral bacteria ferment leftover sugars, they produce organic acids.
When mouth pH drops below 5.5, tooth enamel begins to corrode. Brushing with basic toothpaste neutralises these acids and halts decay.

3. Chemical Warfare: Bee, Wasp & Nettle Stings

• Honey-bee sting: Injects an acidic liquid (formic/methanoic acid) $\implies$ Applying a mild base like baking soda paste relieves pain.
• Wasp sting: Injects an alkaline liquid $\implies$ Relieved by mild acid like vinegar.
• Nettle plant: Leaves have stinging hairs that inject methanoic acid. Nature provides its own antidote: rubbing the affected area with the leaf of the dock plant (which grows right beside nettle!) neutralises the sting.

Sections 2.4.2 & 2.4.3 • Salt Hydrolysis & History Slide 13 of 21

The pH of Salts & Common Salt as a Historical Asset (Activity 2.14 & Dandi March)

"“Not all salts are neutral! The pH of a salt depends on the relative strengths of its parent acid and parent base.”"

A salt is not always neutral. Depending on whether its parent acid or base was strong or weak, an aqueous salt solution can be neutral, acidic, or basic.

Salt pH Classification Matrix (Activity 2.14)

• Strong Acid + Strong Base $\longrightarrow$ Neutral Salt ($ ext{pH} = 7$):
e.g., $HCl + NaOH \to \mathbf{NaCl} + H_2O$, $HNO_3 + KOH \to \mathbf{KNO_3} + H_2O$.
• Strong Acid + Weak Base $\longrightarrow$ Acidic Salt ($ ext{pH} < 7$):
e.g., $HCl + NH_4OH \to \mathbf{NH_4Cl} + H_2O$, $H_2SO_4 + Cu(OH)_2 \to \mathbf{CuSO_4} + 2H_2O$.
• Weak Acid + Strong Base $\longrightarrow$ Basic Salt ($ ext{pH} > 7$):
e.g., $CH_3COOH + NaOH \to \mathbf{CH_3COONa} + H_2O$, $H_2CO_3 + 2NaOH \to \mathbf{Na_2CO_3} + 2H_2O$.

Rock Salt & Mahatma Gandhi's Dandi March

• Rock Salt: Large brownish crystals deposited when ancient inland seas dried up over geological epochs. Mined underground just like coal.
• Dandi March (1930): Mahatma Gandhi marched 240 miles to Dandi to produce salt from seawater, defying the British colonial salt tax and turning common salt into a historic symbol of national freedom and self-reliance.

Section 2.4.3 • Industrial Electrolysis Slide 14 of 21

The Chlor-Alkali Process: Industrial Synthesis of NaOH, Cl2, and H2

"“Electrolysis of brine yields three invaluable industrial chemicals: chlorine gas, hydrogen gas, and sodium hydroxide.”"

Passing an electric current through concentrated aqueous sodium chloride (called brine) drives the chlor-alkali process, the cornerstone of the modern chemical industry.

Chemical Equation of Chlor-Alkali Process

$$2\text{NaCl}(aq) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \xrightarrow{\text{Electricity}} 2\mathbf{\text{NaOH}(aq)} + \mathbf{\text{Cl}_2(g) \uparrow} + \mathbf{\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow}$$
• Named 'chlor-alkali' because the products are chlor for chlorine gas and alkali for sodium hydroxide.

Electrode Discharges & Locations

• Anode (Positive Electrode): Chlorine gas ($\text{Cl}_2$) is liberated.
• Cathode (Negative Electrode): Hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) is liberated.
• Near the Cathode: Sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) solution collects.

Industrial Uses of the Three Products

1. $H_2$ Gas: Rocket fuels, margarine hydrogenation, ammonia synthesis for fertilizers.
2. $Cl_2$ Gas: Water purification, disinfectant for swimming pools, PVC plastic manufacture, CFCs, pesticides.
3. $NaOH$ Alkali: De-greasing metals, soaps and detergents, paper manufacturing, synthetic rayon fibres.

Section 2.4.3 • Everyday Essentials Slide 15 of 21

Bleaching Powder (CaOCl2) & Baking Soda (NaHCO3): Solvay Synthesis & Culinary Chemistry

"“Baking powder is a mixture of baking soda and tartaric acid; carbon dioxide makes cakes soft and spongy!”"

Chlorine from brine manufactures Bleaching Powder, while the Solvay process yields Baking Soda, transforming baking and household emergency safety.

1. Bleaching Powder [$CaOCl_2$]

Produced by the action of chlorine gas on dry slaked lime:
$$\text{Ca(OH)}_2(s) + \text{Cl}_2(g) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaOCl}_2(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
Chemical name: Calcium oxychloride.
Uses: Bleaching cotton and linen in textile mills, bleaching wood pulp in paper factories, oxidising agent in chemical synthesis, and disinfecting drinking water to kill germs.

2. Baking Soda [Sodium Hydrogencarbonate, $NaHCO_3$]

Synthesized using brine, carbon dioxide, and ammonia:
$$\text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 + \text{NH}_3 \longrightarrow \text{NH}_4\text{Cl} + \mathbf{\text{NaHCO}_3}$$
Thermal decomposition during cooking releases carbon dioxide gas:
$$2\text{NaHCO}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \mathbf{\text{CO}_2 \uparrow}$$

Baking Powder vs Baking Soda

Baking soda alone leaves bitter, alkaline sodium carbonate in food. Baking powder is a mixture of baking soda and a mild edible acid like tartaric acid. Tartaric acid neutralises the bitter sodium carbonate into a pleasant-tasting sodium salt while $CO_2$ gas bubbles make cakes rise, soft and spongy!

Section 2.4.3 • Cleaning & Fire Safety Slide 16 of 21

Washing Soda (Na2CO3·10H2O) & The Soda-Acid Fire Extinguisher

"“Washing soda contains 10 molecules of water of crystallisation and removes permanent hardness of water.”"

Heating baking soda and recrystallizing it yields Washing Soda, a vital builder for detergents, glass manufacturing, and emergency fire extinguishers.

Synthesis of Washing Soda

Heating baking soda produces anhydrous sodium carbonate (soda ash):
$$2\text{NaHCO}_3 \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$$
Recrystallization with 10 water molecules produces washing soda:
$$\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 + 10\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \cdot 10\text{H}_2\text{O}}$$
Chemical name: Sodium carbonate decahydrate.

Four Major Industrial Uses

1. Used in glass, soap, and paper manufacturing industries.
2. Used in the manufacture of sodium borate compounds such as borax.
3. Used as a heavy-duty domestic cleaning agent for laundering.
4. Softening Water: Removes permanent hardness caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium sulphates and chlorides by precipitating them as insoluble carbonates.

Soda-Acid Fire Extinguisher (Activity 2.10 Group II)

Contains sodium hydrogencarbonate solution and a suspended tube of dilute sulphuric acid. Inverting the canister mixes the two, generating pressurized carbon dioxide gas that blankets flames and suffocates fire.

Section 2.4.4 • Hydrates & Plaster of Paris Slide 17 of 21

Water of Crystallisation: Blue Vitriol, Gypsum & Plaster of Paris (Activity 2.15)

"“Gypsum heated at 373 K loses water to become Plaster of Paris (CaSO4·½H2O), the doctor’s bone-setting shield.”"

Salt crystals that seem bone-dry can harbor precise stoichiometric ratios of bound water molecules. Heating drives off this Water of Crystallisation, giving rise to medical plaster.

Activity 2.15: Reversible Heating of Copper Sulphate

• Hydrated copper sulphate crystals are bright blue: $\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
• On heating in a dry boiling tube, water droplets condense near the rim and the crystals turn chalky white anhydrous $\text{CuSO}_4$:
$$\text{CuSO}_4 \cdot 5\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \text{CuSO}_4(s) \text{ (white)} + 5\text{H}_2\text{O}(g)$$
• Adding 2–3 drops of water to the white powder instantly restores the vivid blue colour!
Definition: Water of crystallisation is the fixed number of water molecules present in one formula unit of a salt.

Gypsum to Plaster of Paris (POP)

• Gypsum: Calcium sulphate dihydrate [$\text{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$].
• On careful heating at 373 K ($100^\circ\text{C}$), it loses three-fourths of its water:
$$\text{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{373\text{ K}} \mathbf{\text{CaSO}_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}} + 1\frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
Chemical name: Calcium sulphate hemihydrate (two formula units share one $H_2O$ molecule).

POP Setting & Uses

When mixed with water, POP rehydrates back into gypsum, setting into a rock-hard solid in 15 minutes:
$$\text{CaSO}_4 \cdot \frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O} + 1\frac{1}{2}\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaSO}_4 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}} \text{ (hard gypsum)}$$
Uses: Orthopaedic bone casts, decorative ceiling moulds, sculptures, making surfaces smooth.
