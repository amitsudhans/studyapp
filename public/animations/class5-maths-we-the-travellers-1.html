We the Travellers

Human Fascination with Travel from Ancient Times to Spacecraft

Since the dawn of human history, people have loved to travel, explore new lands, meet distant communities, and discover the unknown. Thousands of years ago, the only way to travel was by walking on foot. Gradually, humans tamed animals like bullocks, horses, camels, and yaks to pull carts and carry heavy loads across deserts, grasslands, and mountain trails.

Ancient Travel (0 to 15 km/h)

Walking at 3–5 km/h, riding bullock carts at 4 km/h, or sailing timber boats propelled by wind and oars across rivers and coastal bays.

Modern Era (80 to 28,000 km/h)

High-speed electric trains travelling at 160 km/h, commercial aeroplanes at 850 km/h, and spacecraft orbiting at 28,000 km/h.

As travel speeds and distances increased, humans needed larger numbers to measure travel distances, count passengers, calculate vehicle fuel, and map trade routes across vast continents.

Ten Thousand

Discovering 5-Digit Numbers: Moving from 9,000 to 10,000

In Grade 4, you mastered 4-digit numbers up to 9,999. Now let us discover what happens when we add thousands successively. Imagine counting passenger seats on long-distance trains:

1,000 → 2,000 → 3,000 → 4,000 → 5,000 → 6,000 → 7,000 → 8,000 → 9,000
9,000 + 1,000 = 10,000 (Ten Thousand)

Just as 10 Hundreds bundle together to make 1 Thousand (10 × 100 = 1,000), bundling 10 Thousands together gives us 1 Ten Thousand (10 × 1,000 = 10,000).

Key Milestone: 10,000 is the smallest 5-digit number. It is written as 1 followed by four zeroes (1, 0, 0, 0, 0). The largest 4-digit number is 9,999, and adding 1 to 9,999 gives exactly 10,000!

Place Value

The Indian Place Value Chart: TTh, Th, H, T, and O

To read and write large numbers systematically, we use the Indian Place Value System. With 5-digit numbers, we add a brand-new column to the left of Thousands: Ten Thousands (TTh).

TTh (Ten Thousands) Th (Thousands) H (Hundreds) T (Tens) O (Ones)
3 5 4 2 7

In the number 35,427:

  • Digit 3 is in Ten Thousands place → Value = 3 × 10,000 = 30,000
  • Digit 5 is in Thousands place → Value = 5 × 1,000 = 5,000
  • Digit 4 is in Hundreds place → Value = 4 × 100 = 400
  • Digit 2 is in Tens place → Value = 2 × 10 = 20
  • Digit 7 is in Ones place → Value = 7 × 1 = 7

Expanded Form: 30,000 + 5,000 + 400 + 20 + 7 = 35,427.

Writing Numbers

Comma Placement and Reading Number Names in the Indian System

To read and write large numbers easily without getting confused by a long line of digits, we use commas to separate groups of place values called periods.

Thousands Period (TTh, Th)

The first two digits on the left represent thousands. Read together: e.g., 48 thousands in 48,215.

Ones Period (H, T, O)

The last three digits represent hundreds, tens, and ones: e.g., 215 (two hundred fifteen).

Indian Comma Rule: In the Indian system, the first comma is placed after 3 digits from the right (separating the Ones period: H, T, O). Subsequent commas are placed after every 2 digits! For a 5-digit number, we have exactly one comma: XX,XXX.

Examples:

  • 24,560 = Twenty-four thousand, five hundred sixty
  • 70,008 = Seventy thousand, eight
  • 99,999 = Ninety-nine thousand, nine hundred ninety-nine

Ordering Numbers

Comparing 5-Digit Numbers and Sorting in Increasing and Decreasing Order

When planning travel routes or checking train odometer readings, we often compare numbers to see which distance is greater or sort them from shortest to longest.

Rules for Comparing 5-Digit Numbers
  1. Compare Highest Place First: Look at the Ten Thousands (TTh) digit. The number with the greater TTh digit is larger (e.g., 62,400 > 48,900).
  2. If TTh digits are equal: Compare the Thousands (Th) digits (e.g., 58,200 > 53,400).
  3. If Th digits are equal: Move to Hundreds, then Tens, and finally Ones.
Increasing (Ascending) Order

From smallest to largest:
12,450 < 23,800 < 45,120 < 78,900

Decreasing (Descending) Order

From largest to smallest:
78,900 > 45,120 > 23,800 > 12,450

Digit Swap

Digit Exchange Puzzles and Making Largest and Smallest Numbers

What happens when you swap two digits in a number? Because of our positional place value system, swapping digits drastically alters the number's total value!

The Power of Position

Take the number 25,480. If you swap the digits in the Ten Thousands (2) and Thousands (5) places, you get 52,480.
The value increased by: 52,480 − 25,480 = 27,000!

Forming the Largest Number

Arrange given digits in descending order (highest digit in highest place):
Digits 3, 8, 1, 9, 5 → 98,531

Forming the Smallest Number

Arrange digits in ascending order (if 0 is present, put it in second place):
Digits 4, 0, 7, 2, 9 → 20,479 (not 02,479!)

Nearest Tens

Rounding Off Numbers to the Nearest 10 Using Number Lines

In everyday travel, we often use approximate numbers. For example, if a bus has 48 passengers, we say it has "about 50 passengers". This practical mathematical skill is called Rounding Off.

The Rabbit and Carrot Number Line

Imagine a rabbit sitting on number 43 between two carrot patches at tens: 40 and 50.
Distance to 40 = 3 hops. Distance to 50 = 7 hops.
The rabbit will hop to the closer food at 40! So 43 rounded to nearest 10 is 40.

The Halfway Rule: Look at the Ones digit:
• If the Ones digit is 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 (less than 5) → Round DOWN to the previous ten.
• If the Ones digit is 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9 (5 or more) → Round UP to the next ten.
Example: 45 is exactly halfway between 40 and 50. By mathematical convention, we round UP to 50.

Nearest Hundreds

Rounding to the Nearest 100 Using Benchmarks and Highway Milestones

When travellers drive down a highway, milestones often show distances rounded to hundreds of kilometres. To round a number to the nearest hundred, we find which multiple of 100 it is closest to.

Highway Milestone Benchmarks

Multiples of 100 are: 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, 700, 800, 900, 1,000, etc.
Between 300 and 400, the exact halfway mark is 350.

Rule for Nearest 100: Look at the Tens digit:
• If the last two digits are 00 to 49 (Tens digit < 5) → Round DOWN to the lower hundred.
• If the last two digits are 50 to 99 (Tens digit ≥ 5) → Round UP to the higher hundred.
Examples:
• 328 → Tens digit is 2 (< 5) → rounds to 300.
• 372 → Tens digit is 7 (≥ 5) → rounds to 400.
• 350 → Exactly halfway → rounds UP to 400.

Nearest Thousands

Rounding to 1,000 and When Rounding to 100 Gives the Same Result

To round a number to the nearest thousand, we check which multiple of 1,000 it is closest to on a number line (e.g., 1,000, 2,000, 3,000, 4,000, etc.). The halfway mark between consecutive thousands is 500.

Rule for Nearest 1,000

Look at the Hundreds digit:
• If Hundreds digit is 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 (< 500) → Round DOWN to lower thousand.
• If Hundreds digit is 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9 (≥ 500) → Round UP to higher thousand.
Example: In 2,346, hundreds digit is 3 (< 5) → rounds to 2,000.
In 7,820, hundreds digit is 8 (≥ 5) → rounds to 8,000.

Let Us Think (Fascinating Puzzle): Can rounding a number to the nearest 100 give the exact same answer as rounding it to the nearest 1,000?
YES! Take 2,980:
• Rounded to nearest 100 → 3,000 (since 80 ≥ 50, rounds up from 2,900 to 3,000).
• Rounded to nearest 1,000 → 3,000 (since 980 ≥ 500, rounds up from 2,000 to 3,000).
Both give 3,000!

Scenario: Grade 5 Nature Excursion

A total of 210 students and teachers are going on a study trip to a wildlife reserve.
• A large bus can carry 50 passengers.
• A mini-van can carry 15 passengers.

Step 1: How many full 50-seater buses can we fill?
210 ÷ 50 = 4 full buses (carrying 4 × 50 = 200 passengers).
Step 2: Remaining passengers = 210 − 200 = 10 passengers.
Step 3: Can 10 passengers fit in one 15-seater mini-van? Yes!
Optimal Fleet: 4 Buses and 1 Mini-van (with 5 empty spare seats).

If we hired only 50-seater buses, we would need 5 buses because 4 buses only hold 200, leaving 10 behind!

The Dilemma

A boatman must ferry a lion, a sheep, and a bundle of grass across a river in a small rowboat.
• The boat can hold only the boatman and ONE item at a time.
• Danger 1: If left alone without the boatman, the lion will eat the sheep!
• Danger 2: If left alone without the boatman, the sheep will eat the grass bundle!

The 7-Step Winning Solution:
1. Take the Sheep across (leaves Lion and Grass safely together).
2. Row back empty.
3. Take the Grass across.
4. Bring the Sheep BACK to the start (crucial move! Don't leave Sheep with Grass).
5. Take the Lion across (leaves Sheep alone at start; Lion & Grass are safe together).
6. Row back empty.
7. Take the Sheep across! All three arrive safely across the river!

Pebble Game

Pastime Math: The 2-Player Nim Strategy and Multiples of 4

When travellers sit together on long journeys, they often play strategy games using simple pebbles, coins, or matchsticks. One classic game is the Pile of Pebbles Game:

Game Rules

• Start with a pile of pebbles (e.g., 15 pebbles).
• Two players take turns taking 1, 2, or 3 pebbles from the pile.
• The player forced to take the very last pebble loses (or the player who takes the last pebble wins, depending on variant).

The Secret Mathematical Strategy: Multiples of 4!
Notice that 1 + 3 = 4, and 2 + 2 = 4. Whatever number x the opponent takes, you can always take 4 − x so that every round removes exactly 4 pebbles!
• If the goal is to take the last pebble: Always leave a multiple of 4 for your opponent (4, 8, 12, 16...).
• With 15 pebbles: Player 1 takes 3 pebbles, leaving 12 (a multiple of 4). Whatever Player 2 takes, Player 1 complements to 4, guaranteeing victory!

Digit Difference

Pastime Math: The Magic 9 Subtraction Mystery and Reversal Logic

Pick any two different digits, say 7 and 2. What happens when you explore their difference? Try this fascinating numerical trick:

1. Form the two 2-digit numbers: 72 and 27.

2. Subtract smaller from larger: 72 − 27 = 45.

3. Now take the digits of 45 (4 and 5), form 54 and 45: 54 − 45 = 9!

Try another pair: Digits 8 and 3 → 83 − 38 = 45 → 54 − 45 = 9!

Digits 9 and 1 → 91 − 19 = 72 → 72 − 27 = 45 → 54 − 45 = 9!

Why does this always yield a multiple of 9?
Any two-digit number with digits a and b has value: 10a + b.
Reversing the digits gives: 10b + a.
Subtracting: (10a + b) − (10b + a) = 9a − 9b = 9 × (a − b)!
The difference is always a multiple of 9, which inexorably collapses down to 9!

Pastime Math: The Stable Trick Puzzle and Corner-Sharing Logic

A classic recreational math story tells of a king who had a square stable with 9 rooms (a 3×3 grid). The king kept horses in the outer 8 rooms, leaving the center room empty. He checked that each of the 4 outer sides contained exactly 5 horses, believing 4 × 5 = 20 horses were safely in the stable.

Original Stable (20 Horses)

0   5   0  → Top = 5
5   [ ] 5  → Middle sides = 5 each
0   5   0  → Bottom = 5
Total horses = 5 + 5 + 5 + 5 = 20 horses.

Caretaker Steals 2 Horses (18 Horses)

1   3   1  → Top = 1+3+1 = 5
4   [ ] 4  → Left/Right = 1+4+0 = 5
0   5   0  → Bottom = 0+5+0 = 5
Total horses = 2 corners + 16 middles = 18 horses!

The Secret Corner-Sharing Formula:
Let C be the total horses placed in the 4 corners.
Since each corner horse is shared by two sides, it is counted twice:
Total Horses in Stable = 20 − C.
• When C = 0 (no corner horses), Total = 20.
• When C = 1 (1 horse in a corner), Total = 19 (1 horse stolen!).
• When C = 2 (2 horses in corners), Total = 18 (2 horses stolen!).
On every count, each side still equals 5!
