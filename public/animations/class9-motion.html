Mass Conservation

Section 9.1, Activities 9.1–9.2 & Antoine Lavoisier (1789)

Slide 2 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Suppose you dissolve a spoonful of salt in water, or mix baking soda and vinegar in a flask. Does any matter vanish or appear out of thin air? Section 9.1 answers this with the Law of Conservation of Mass! First, in Activity 9.1, we test a physical change: weigh a beaker of water and a packet of salt before mixing, dissolve the salt completely, and weigh again—the total mass stays completely unchanged! Next, in Activity 9.2, we test a chemical change: mixing baking soda and vinegar produces fizzing carbon dioxide gas. In Set-up 1, using an open flask lets the carbon dioxide gas escape into the room, so the scale shows a lower final weight—tricking an observer! In Set-up 2, stretching a rubber balloon over the flask mouth traps all the carbon dioxide gas inside; weighing the closed system before and after proves the total mass is strictly identical! Formulated by French chemist Antoine Lavoisier—the Father of Modern Chemistry—in 1789, this law states that matter can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction: total mass of reactants always equals total mass of products, with not a single atom lost or created inside any sealed reaction vessel!

Constant Proportions

Section 9.2: Proust's Law, Cinnabar & Example 9.3

Slide 4 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now we can answer our first Core Inquiry: are pure water samples from a Himalayan glacier, a river, and a laboratory synthesis chemically identical? Yes! Section 9.2 introduces the Law of Constant Proportions, also called Proust's Law or the Law of Definite Proportions, discovered by French chemist Joseph Louis Proust! It states that a pure chemical compound always contains the exact same elements combined in a fixed ratio by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation. For example, pure water always decomposes into hydrogen and oxygen in a fixed one-to-eight mass ratio! Similarly, copper carbonate always maintains fixed proportions of copper, carbon, and oxygen whether mined naturally or made in a lab. Our Threads of Curiosity note highlights ancient extraction of mercury and sulfur from red cinnabar pigment—known as hingula in India—always yielding eighty-six point two two percent mercury to thirteen point seven eight percent sulfur! Look at Example 9.3: in sodium chloride, sodium and chlorine combine in a twenty-three to thirty-five point five mass ratio, so forty-six grams of sodium require exactly seventy-one grams of chlorine! Unlike mixtures, compounds never vary their mass ratios, no matter how or where they are prepared in nature!

Dalton's Atomic Theory

Section 9.3: Six Foundational Postulates (1808)

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Why do chemical reactions conserve total mass and obey fixed elemental ratios? In 1808, English schoolteacher and scientist John Dalton—who taught in Manchester and built meteorology and chemistry foundations—gave a brilliant microscopic explanation in Dalton's Atomic Theory! Let us review all six postulates in Section 9.3. First, all matter consists of microscopic, indivisible particles called atoms. Second, atoms can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction—which immediately explains Lavoisier's Law of Conservation of Mass because reactions only rearrange existing atoms! Third, atoms of a given element are identical in mass and chemical properties. Fourth, atoms of different elements have different masses and chemical properties. Fifth, atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios to form compounds. Sixth, the relative number and kinds of atoms are constant in any given compound—which directly explains Proust's Law of Constant Proportions! Even though we learned in Chapter 8 that atoms contain smaller subatomic particles and isotopes, Dalton's postulates still explain every macroscopic mass law of chemical combination with remarkable power and clarity! Always connect these six postulates directly back to Lavoisier's mass balance and Proust's fixed elemental ratios in every chemical reaction and pure compound you study in this chapter.

Why Atoms Combine

Section 9.4: Molecules, Potential Energy & Bonding Pathways

Slide 6 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now let us bridge Chapter 8's electronic configurations directly into chemical bonding! Why do isolated atoms—except noble gases like Helium, Neon, and Argon—rarely stay alone in nature, preferring instead to join into molecules or crystals? Section 9.4 explains the driving force: atoms combine to lower their potential energy and achieve maximum stability by completing a valence octet of eight electrons, or a duplet of two electrons in the K shell! Think of a ball rolling down a hill into a deep valley: bonded atoms sit in a lower-energy, more stable state than free unbonded atoms! That also answers our second Core Inquiry: a single oxygen atom O has only six valence electrons and is unstable, whereas an oxygen molecule O-two pairs two oxygen atoms together so both achieve stable octets! How do atoms reach this stable octet? Nature uses two primary bonding pathways: first, sharing pairs of valence electrons between non-metal atoms to form a Covalent Bond; and second, completely transferring electrons from metals to non-metals to form oppositely charged ions held by an Ionic Bond! Both pathways guide atoms toward stable noble-gas configurations with completely filled outer shells and much lower overall potential energy.

Covalent Element Bonds

Section 9.4.1(A): Single, Double & Triple Covalent Bonds

Slide 7 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Let us zoom in on the first bonding pathway in Section 9.4.1: Covalent Bonding by sharing electrons between atoms of the SAME element! When two hydrogen atoms meet, each has only one electron in its K shell and needs one more for a stable Helium duplet. Neither wants to give up its electron, so they share their two electrons equally! One shared pair of electrons forms a single covalent bond, written H dash H, making the diatomic hydrogen molecule H-two! Similarly, two chlorine atoms (2, 8, 7) or two fluorine atoms (2, 7) each need one electron for an octet, so each shares one pair to form single-bonded Cl-two and F-two! Now what about oxygen (2, 6)? Each oxygen atom has six valence electrons and needs two more to reach eight. So two oxygen atoms share TWO pairs of electrons—forming a double covalent bond, written O double-equals O! Finally, look at Pause and Ponder for nitrogen (2, 5): needing three electrons each, two nitrogen atoms share THREE pairs of electrons, creating an extra-strong triple covalent bond in N-two! Ionic Bond Formation

Section 9.4.2: Cations, Anions & 3D Crystal Lattice

Slide 9 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

What happens when a metal meets a non-metal—like reactive sodium metal meeting poisonous chlorine gas? Section 9.4.2 explores the second bonding pathway: Ionic Bonding by electron transfer! Metals typically have fewer than four valence electrons—for example, sodium (2, 8, 1) has just one outermost electron. Instead of struggling to gain seven electrons, sodium easily loses one electron to form a positively charged cation, Na-plus, attaining Neon's stable (2, 8) octet! Non-metals typically have more than four valence electrons—chlorine (2, 8, 7) has seven. Chlorine eagerly gains that single transferred electron to form a negatively charged anion, Cl-minus, attaining Argon's stable (2, 8, 8) octet! Strong electrostatic attraction immediately locks Na-plus and Cl-minus together into an ionic bond, forming edible common salt, NaCl! Look at Threads of Curiosity: ionic compounds do NOT exist as isolated single pairs! Instead, billions of ions pack into a giant, repeating three-dimensional Crystal Lattice—in solid NaCl, every Na-plus ion is surrounded by six Cl-minus neighbours, and every Cl-minus is surrounded by six Na-plus ions! This regular cubic packing explains why salt crystals form sharp tiny cubes and have high melting points and why salt crystals shatter cleanly along flat crystal planes.

Criss-Cross Formulae

Sections 9.5.1 & 9.5.2: Covalent & Ionic Formula Writing

Slide 11 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now let us turn ion charges and valencies into exact chemical formulae using the famous Criss-Cross Method in Section 9.5! First, for covalent compounds in 9.5.1, write the two element symbols side by side, write their valencies beneath them, and cross-over the numbers as subscripts! For Hydrogen Sulfide: H has valency 1, S has valency 2—crossing gives H-two-S! For Carbon Tetrachloride: C has valency 4, Cl has valency 1—crossing gives C-Cl-four! Next, for ionic compounds in 9.5.2, write the cation symbol on the left and anion on the right, write their charge magnitudes beneath, and criss-cross! Watch two crucial rules: Rule one, ALWAYS simplify subscripts to the lowest whole-number ratio! For Magnesium Oxide, Mg-two-plus and O-two-minus cross to Mg-two-O-two, which simplifies by dividing by two to MgO! Rule two, when a polyatomic ion needs a subscript greater than one, enclose the ENTIRE polyatomic ion in brackets! Thus Magnesium Hydroxide is Mg-(OH)-two, and Aluminium Sulfate is Al-two-(SO-four)-three! For Calcium Carbonate, 2+ and 2- cancel cleanly to CaCO-three without brackets! Always double-check that total positive charge equals total negative charge, ensuring every ionic formula is electrically neutral, simplified, and properly balanced in every single chemical compound you write.

Ionic vs Covalent

Section 9.6 & Activity 9.4: Solubility, Conductivity & Melting Points

Slide 12 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now we are ready to answer our third Core Inquiry: why does salt water conduct electricity while sugar water does not? Look at Activity 9.4 in Section 9.6, where we test camphor, sodium chloride, copper sulfate, sugar, and naphthalene across two experiments! First, solubility: ionic compounds like NaCl and CuSO-four dissolve readily in polar water, but do NOT dissolve in organic solvents like kerosene or petrol. Conversely, covalent compounds like camphor and naphthalene dissolve in kerosene or petrol, while sugar dissolves in water as intact neutral molecules! Second, test electrical conductivity using a nine-volt battery, a bulb, and two electrodes! Solid NaCl does NOT conduct electricity because its ions are locked tightly in a rigid crystal lattice. Dissolve NaCl in water or melt it, and the bulb glows brightly because free mobile Na-plus and Cl-minus ions carry electric current! Dissolve sugar in water, and the bulb stays dark because covalent sugar produces zero free ions! Finally, strong inter-ionic electrostatic forces give ionic solids high melting and boiling points, whereas weaker intermolecular forces give covalent compounds much lower melting and boiling points! Molecular Mass

Section 9.7, Examples 9.4–9.5 & Pause and Ponder (21–22)

Slide 13 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Once we know a covalent compound's formula, how do we find the mass of one molecule? Section 9.7 defines Molecular Mass as simply the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms present in one molecule of that covalent substance, expressed in unified mass units u! Let us work through every textbook example step by step. In Example 9.4, calculate the molecular mass of water, H-two-O, given H equals 1 u and O equals 16 u: multiply hydrogen's mass by two atoms to get 2 u, add one oxygen atom at 16 u, and you get 18 u! In Example 9.5, calculate carbon dioxide, CO-two, given C equals 12 u and O equals 16 u: take 12 plus two times 16, which is 12 plus 32, giving 44 u! Now solve Pause and Ponder 21 and 22! For Nitric Acid, H-N-O-three (with H=1, N=14, O=16): add 1 plus 14 plus three times 16 (which is 48)—giving 1 plus 14 plus 48 equals 63 u! And for Methane, C-H-four: add 12 plus four times 1, giving 16 u! Just multiply each element's atomic mass by its subscript and sum them all carefully without skipping any atom!

Formula Unit Mass

Section 9.8, Examples 9.6–9.7 & Pause and Ponder (23–24)

Slide 14 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Remember from Section 9.4.2 that ionic compounds do NOT exist as discrete molecules—they form giant 3D crystal lattices! So instead of calling it molecular mass, Section 9.8 uses the exact term Formula Unit Mass: the sum of atomic masses of all atoms in the simplest empirical ratio, or formula unit, of an ionic crystal! The arithmetic works the same way—just watch brackets carefully! Look at Example 9.6 for Sodium Oxide, Na-two-O (Na = 23 u, O = 16 u): multiply 23 by 2 to get 46, add 16, and you get 62 u! Look at Example 9.7 for Calcium Nitrate, Ca-(NO-three)-two (Ca = 40 u, N = 14 u, O = 16 u): first compute inside the nitrate bracket: 14 plus three times 16 (48) equals 62 u! Since there are two nitrate groups outside the bracket, multiply 62 by 2 to get 124 u, then add Calcium's 40 u—giving 164 u! Now check Pause and Ponder 23 and 24: Potassium Chloride, KCl (K = 39 u, Cl = 35.5 u), equals 74.5 u; and Magnesium Hydroxide, Mg-(OH)-two (Mg = 24 u, O = 16 u, H = 1 u), equals 24 plus 2 times 17, giving 58 u!

Chapter Summary

At a Glance: Laws, Bonding, Formulae & Mass Calculations

Slide 16 of 20
👨‍🏫

Let us wrap up Chapter 9 with our complete At a Glance summary! First, Antoine Lavoisier's 1789 Law of Conservation of Mass proves total mass of reactants equals total mass of products in any closed system. Second, Joseph Proust's Law of Constant Proportions proves every pure compound has a fixed elemental mass ratio, such as 1:8 in water and 86.22% to 13.78% in cinnabar. Third, John Dalton's 1808 Atomic Theory explained both laws through indestructible atoms rearranging in simple whole-number ratios. Fourth, atoms bond to lower potential energy and complete octets: non-metals share electrons in Covalent Bonds (single H-two, double O-two, triple N-two, HCl, H-two-O), while metals transfer electrons to non-metals in Ionic Bonds forming 3D crystal lattices (NaCl). Fifth, the Criss-Cross Method writes formulae cleanly—simplifying ratios (MgO) and using brackets for polyatomic ions (Ca(NO-three)-two). Sixth, ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct electricity when dissolved or molten, unlike covalent compounds. Finally, Molecular Mass and Formula Unit Mass sum atomic masses in unified units u.
