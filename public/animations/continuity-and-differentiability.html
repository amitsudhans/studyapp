Intuitive Meaning: The "Pen-Lifting" Principle

In everyday language, continuous means uninterrupted without breaks or abrupt jumps. In mathematics, as Sir Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz formulated, we study dynamic quantities in continuous variation. Albert Einstein famously remarked: "The whole of science is nothing more than a refinement of everyday thinking."

5.2 Formal Definition of Continuity at a Point

Part - 1

Limit equals Function Value: LHL = RHL = f(c), Examples 1 to 6

§ 5.2 Definition 1 The 3-Pillar Continuity Test

Formal Definition of Continuity at a Point

Suppose $f$ is a real function on a subset of the real numbers and let $c$ be a point in the domain of $f$. Then $f$ is continuous at $c$ if and only if:

lim_{x → c} f(x) = f(c)  ⟺  lim_{x → c⁻} f(x) = lim_{x → c⁺} f(x) = f(c)
Pillar 1: Defined at Point

$f(c)$ must be a well-defined real number. If $f(c)$ is division by zero or imaginary, continuity fails immediately!

Pillar 2: Two-Sided Limit Exists

Both one-sided limits must exist and match: $\text{LHL} = \lim_{x \to c^-} f(x) = \lim_{x \to c^+} f(x) = \text{RHL}$.

Pillar 3: Limit Matches Value

The target limit must land exactly on the function value $f(c)$. No isolated punctures or displaced dots!

Example 3: Absolute Value Function f(x) = |x|

By definition, $|x| = -x$ for $x < 0$ and $|x| = x$ for $x \ge 0$.

• LHL = lim_{x→0⁻} (-x) = -0 = 0
• RHL = lim_{x→0⁺} (x) = +0 = 0
• f(0) = |0| = 0
⟹ LHL = RHL = f(0) = 0. Continuous everywhere!
⚠️ Student Trap: A sharp "V-corner" does NOT break continuity! It only breaks differentiability.

Example 2 & 4: Polynomials & Constants

• Constant Function: $f(x) = k$. For every $c$, $\lim_{x \to c} k = k = f(c)$. Continuous everywhere.
• Identity Function: $f(x) = x$. For every $c$, $\lim_{x \to c} x = c = f(c)$. Continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.
• Polynomial Function: $P(x) = a_n x^n + ... + a_0$. Limits evaluate by direct substitution, so all polynomials are continuous everywhere!

lim_{x→c} P(x) = P(c)  ∀ c ∈ ℝ

§ 5.2 Definition 2 Intervals & Pathological Discontinuities

Continuity on Closed Intervals & The [x] Function

A real function $f$ is continuous on a closed interval $[a, b]$ if it is continuous on $(a, b)$, right-continuous at $a$ ($\lim_{x o a^+} f(x) = f(a)$), and left-continuous at $b$ ($\lim_{x o b^-} f(x) = f(b)$).

⚡ The Rational Function f(x) = 1/x (Tables 5.1 & 5.2)

The domain of $f(x) = 1/x$ is $D = \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}$. At every non-zero point $c \in D$: $$\lim_{x \to c} \frac{1}{x} = \frac{1}{c} = f(c)$$ Therefore, $f(x) = 1/x$ is continuous on its entire domain!

Behavior Near Zero (Not in domain):
x → 0⁻ : f(x) → -∞
-0.1 ⟹ -10
-0.001 ⟹ -1000
x → 0⁺ : f(x) → +∞
+0.1 ⟹ +10
+0.001 ⟹ +1000

🪜 The Greatest Integer Function f(x) = [x] (Example 15)

$[x]$ denotes the largest integer less than or equal to $x$. At any integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$:

• LHL = lim_{x→k⁻} [x] = k - 1 (e.g. [2.999] = 2)
• RHL = lim_{x→k⁺} [x] = k      (e.g. [3.001] = 3)
⟹ LHL ≠ RHL ! The limit does not exist.
📌 Takeaway: $[x]$ is discontinuous at every single integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$, but is continuous on every open interval $(k, k+1)$!

Part - 1

§ 5.2.1 Theorems 1 & 2 Algebraic Operations & Composite Continuity

Theorems on Algebra & Composite Functions

Continuity is preserved under arithmetic combinations and functional compositions. These two master theorems allow us to effortlessly prove the continuity of complex expressions without computing epsilon-delta limits!

Theorem 1: Arithmetic Combinations

If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at a real point $c$, then:

  • 1. (f + g)(x) is continuous at c
  • 2. (f - g)(x) is continuous at c
  • 3. (f · g)(x) is continuous at c
  • 4. (f / g)(x) is continuous at c  (provided g(c) ≠ 0)
Trigonometric Functions: Since $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are continuous everywhere, $\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$, $\cot x$, $\sec x$, $\csc x$ are continuous everywhere in their respective domains!

Theorem 2: Composite Functions (f ∘ g)

Suppose $f$ and $g$ are real-valued functions such that $(f \circ g)$ is defined at $c$. If $g$ is continuous at $c$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(c)$, then $(f \circ g)$ is continuous at $c$.

Classic Textbook Applications:
• Example 19: $h(x) = \sin(x^2)$. Let $g(x) = x^2$ and $f(u) = \sin u$. Both are continuous everywhere ⟹ $h(x)$ is continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$.
• Example 20: $h(x) = |1 - x + |x||$. Composed of nested absolute values and polynomials ⟹ continuous on all of $\mathbb{R}$.

Mastering Piecewise Continuity Problems

Board exam questions consistently ask you to find parameters so that a piecewise function is continuous at its transition point. The key: equate LHL, RHL, and $f(c)$ at the boundary!

Problem 1: Finding k (Exercise 5.1 Q26)

f(x) = { k cos(x) / (π - 2x)  (x ≠ π/2) ; 3  (x = π/2) }

1. Compute $\lim_{x \to \pi/2} \frac{k \cos x}{\pi - 2x}$. Substitute $x = \pi/2 + h$.
2. As $x \to \pi/2$, $h \to 0$. $\cos(\pi/2 + h) = -\sin h$.
3. $\pi - 2(\pi/2 + h) = -2h$.
4. Limit = $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{k(-\sin h)}{-2h} = \frac{k}{2} \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sin h}{h} = \frac{k}{2}$.
Equate to f(π/2) = 3:   k/2 = 3 ⟹ k = 6!

Problem 2: Dual Boundaries (Exercise 5.1 Q30)

f(x) = { 5 (x ≤ 2) ; ax + b (2 < x < 10) ; 21 (x ≥ 10) }

• At $x = 2$: LHL = 5, RHL = $2a + b$ ⟹ 2a + b = 5 (Eq 1)
• At $x = 10$: LHL = $10a + b$, RHL = 21 ⟹ 10a + b = 21 (Eq 2)
• Subtract (Eq 1) from (Eq 2): $8a = 16 \implies \mathbf{a = 2}$.
• Substitute into (Eq 1): $2(2) + b = 5 \implies \mathbf{b = 1}$.
Final Solution: a = 2, b = 1 makes f(x) continuous on ℝ!

§ 5.3 Theorem 3 Slope of Tangent & Fundamental Relation

Differentiability & The Asymmetry Theorem

Suppose $f$ is a real function and $c$ is a point in its domain. The derivative of $f$ at $c$ is defined by:

f'(c) = lim_{h → 0} \frac{f(c + h) - f(c)}{h}

Theorem 3: Differentiability ⟹ Continuity

If a function $f$ is differentiable at a point $c$, then it is also continuous at $c$.

// Proof Sketch:
f(x) - f(c) = \frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c} · (x - c)
lim_{x→c} [f(x) - f(c)] = lim_{x→c} [\frac{f(x) - f(c)}{x - c}] · lim_{x→c} (x - c)
lim_{x→c} [f(x) - f(c)] = f'(c) · 0 = 0
⟹ lim_{x→c} f(x) = f(c)  [Continuous!]

⚠️ Converse is FALSE: The |x| Counterexample

A function can be continuous everywhere, yet fail to be differentiable at sharp points! Consider $f(x) = |x|$ at $c = 0$:

LHD: lim_{h→0⁻} \frac{|0+h| - 0}{h} = lim_{h→0⁻} \frac{-h}{h} = -1
RHD: lim_{h→0⁺} \frac{|0+h| - 0}{h} = lim_{h→0⁺} \frac{+h}{h} = +1
LHD (-1) ≠ RHD (+1) ⟹ f'(0) DOES NOT EXIST!

Geometrically, the slope abruptly pivots from -1 to +1 at the sharp corner. There is no unique tangent line at $x = 0$.

5.3.1 Chain Rule for Composite Functions

Part - 1

Theorem 4: dy/dx = (dy/dt) * (dt/dx), Multi-Layer Chain Rule

§ 5.3.1 Theorem 4 Russian Doll Principle of Calculus

Chain Rule for Composite Functions

Let $f$ be a real valued function which is a composite of two functions $u$ and $v$, i.e. $f = v \circ u$. If $t = u(x)$ and both $\frac{dt}{dx}$ and $\frac{dv}{dt}$ exist, then:

\frac{df}{dx} = \frac{dv}{dt} · \frac{dt}{dx}

Multi-Layer Nesting Chain

If $y = f(u)$, $u = g(v)$, and $v = h(x)$, differentiate layer by layer from the outermost shell to the innermost core:

\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} · \frac{du}{dv} · \frac{dv}{dx}

Example: For $y = \sin(x^2 + 5)$:
1. Differentiate outer $\sin(t) \implies \cos(t)$
2. Differentiate inner $x^2 + 5 \implies 2x$
3. Multiply: $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2x \cos(x^2 + 5)$.

Essential Standard Derivatives

d/dx(x^n) = n x^{n-1}
d/dx(sin x) = cos x
d/dx(cos x) = -sin x
d/dx(tan x) = sec² x
d/dx(sec x) = sec x tan x
d/dx(cot x) = -csc² x
💡 Teacher's Tip: Every "co" function ($\cos, \cot, \csc$) produces a negative derivative sign!

3-Stage Chain Rule: sec(tan(√x))

Let $y = \sec(u)$, where $u = \tan(v)$ and $v = \sqrt{x}$:

• dy/du = sec(u) tan(u) = sec(tan √x) tan(tan √x)
• du/dv = sec²(v) = sec²(√x)
• dv/dx = 1 / (2√x)
dy/dx = \frac{\sec(\tan \sqrt{x}) \tan(\tan \sqrt{x}) \sec^2(\sqrt{x})}{2\sqrt{x}}

Proof: f(x) = |x - 1| is Not Differentiable at 1

Exercise 5.2 Q9 requires calculating one-sided derivatives at $c = 1$:

LHD = lim_{h→0⁻} \frac{|1+h - 1| - 0}{h} = lim_{h→0⁻} \frac{|h|}{h} = \frac{-h}{h} = -1
RHD = lim_{h→0⁺} \frac{|1+h - 1| - 0}{h} = lim_{h→0⁺} \frac{|h|}{h} = \frac{+h}{h} = +1
Since LHD ≠ RHD, f'(1) does not exist! Q.E.D.

§ 5.3.2 Implicit Forms Explicit vs Implicit Relations

Derivatives of Implicit Functions

When an equation links $x$ and $y$ such that isolating $y$ explicitly as $y = f(x)$ is difficult or impossible, we differentiate every term with respect to $x$, applying the chain rule to terms with $y$, and collect all $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factors.

The 4-Step Implicit Algorithm

  1. 1. Differentiate: Apply $\frac{d}{dx}$ to both sides of the equation.
  2. 2. Attach dy/dx: Whenever differentiating any $y$-term, multiply by $\frac{dy}{dx}$ (e.g. $\frac{d}{dx}(y^3) = 3y^2 \frac{dy}{dx}$).
  3. 3. Collect Terms: Move all terms containing $\frac{dy}{dx}$ to the left-hand side, and all other terms to the right.
  4. 4. Factor & Divide: Factor out $\frac{dy}{dx}$ and divide by its coefficient.

Textbook Example 23: y + sin y = cos x

d/dx(y) + d/dx(sin y) = d/dx(cos x)
\frac{dy}{dx} + (\cos y) \frac{dy}{dx} = -\sin x
\frac{dy}{dx} (1 + \cos y) = -\sin x
\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{-\sin x}{1 + \cos y}  \text{(for } y \neq (2n+1)\pi\text{)}

Notice that the derivative expression contains both $x$ and $y$, which is natural and standard for implicit equations.

§ 5.3.3 Inverse Trigonometry Derivation via Implicit Differentiation

Derivatives of Inverse Trigonometric Functions

We find the derivatives of inverse trigonometric functions by inverting the relation and using implicit differentiation combined with the fundamental Pythagorean identities.

Derivation: y = sin⁻¹(x)

1. y = sin⁻¹(x) ⟹ sin y = x  for y ∈ (-π/2, π/2)
2. Differentiate w.r.t x: cos y · (dy/dx) = 1
3. dy/dx = 1 / cos y
4. Since y ∈ (-π/2, π/2), cos y > 0: cos y = √(1 - sin²y) = √(1 - x²)
\frac{d}{dx}(\sin^{-1}x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}  \text{for } x \in (-1, 1)

The Inverse Trig Derivatives Master Table

d/dx(cos⁻¹x) -1 / √(1 - x²)
d/dx(tan⁻¹x) 1 / (1 + x²)

§ 5.4 Exponential & Logarithm The Natural Invariant e and Reflection across y = x

Exponential & Logarithmic Functions

The exponential function $f(x) = b^x$ ($b > 1$) models explosive growth. The natural base $e = \lim_{n \to \infty} (1 + 1/n)^n \approx 2.71828...$ possesses the unique property that its rate of change equals its value. The natural logarithm $\ln x = \log_e x$ is its inverse.

Derivatives of e^x and ln(x)

\frac{d}{dx}(e^x) = e^x  \text{(Self-reproducing derivative!)}
\frac{d}{dx}(\ln x) = \frac{1}{x}  \text{for } x > 0
\frac{d}{dx}(a^x) = a^x \ln a  \text{for } a > 0
⭐ Growth Comparison: As $x \to \infty$, $e^x$ grows faster than any polynomial $x^n$. Conversely, $\log x$ grows slower than any root $x^{1/n}$!

Master Laws of Logarithms

  • • log(xy) = log x + log y  (Products ⟹ Sums)
  • • log(x/y) = log x - log y  (Quotients ⟹ Differences)
  • • log(xⁿ) = n log x        (Powers ⟹ Multipliers)
  • • log_a(x) = \frac{\log_b x}{\log_b a}     (Change of Base)
Notice: $\log(1) = 0$, $\ln(e) = 1$, and $\log x$ is only defined for strictly positive arguments ($x > 0$).

§ 5.5 Variable Exponents Power Tower Functions [u(x)]^[v(x)]

Logarithmic Differentiation Technique

When BOTH the base and exponent are functions of $x$, i.e. $y = [u(x)]^{v(x)}$, neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies! Taking the natural logarithm of both sides brings the variable exponent down as a multiplier.

The 4-Step Log Differentiation Protocol

  1. 1. Take Log: Apply $\ln$ to both sides: $\ln y = v(x) \ln(u(x))$.
  2. 2. Differentiate w.r.t x: The left side becomes $\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx}$.
  3. 3. Product Rule on Right: Differentiate $v(x) \ln(u(x))$ using standard product and chain rules.
  4. 4. Multiply by y: $\frac{dy}{dx} = y [\text{RHS expression}]$, then substitute back $y = [u(x)]^{v(x)}$.

The Grand Classic: y = x^x

Step 1: ln y = ln(x^x) = x ln x
Step 2: \frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = (1)·(\ln x) + x·(\frac{1}{x}) = \ln x + 1
Step 3: \frac{dy}{dx} = y (1 + \ln x)
\frac{d}{dx}(x^x) = x^x (1 + \log x)

Notice: Setting $1 + \log x = 0 \implies x = 1/e$, which is the exact minimum point of $y = x^x$!

Advanced Towers & Sum Equations

When a function is a sum of two tower functions, e.g. $y = u(x) + v(x)$, you CANNOT take the log of both sides directly, because $\log(u + v) \neq \log u + \log v$!

The "Split into Auxiliary Variables" Protocol

For $y = x^{\sin x} + (\sin x)^{\cos x}$ (Exercise 5.5 Q9):

1. Set y = u + v  ⟹  \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{du}{dx} + \frac{dv}{dx}
2. For u = x^(sin x): ln u = sin x ln x
  du/dx = x^(sin x) [ \frac{\sin x}{x} + \cos x \ln x ]
3. For v = (sin x)^(cos x): ln v = cos x ln(sin x)
  dv/dx = (sin x)^(cos x) [ \cos x \cot x - \sin x \ln(\sin x) ]
4. Add du/dx and dv/dx together!

Implicit Power Equations: x^y + y^x = 1 (Q12)

Let u = x^y and v = y^x, so u + v = 1 ⟹ du/dx + dv/dx = 0.
• du/dx = x^y [ \frac{y}{x} + (\ln x) \frac{dy}{dx} ]
• dv/dx = y^x [ (\ln y) + \frac{x}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} ]
Collect dy/dx:
\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{y x^{y-1} + y^x \ln y}{x^y \ln x + x y^{x-1}}

§ 5.6 & Exercise 5.6 Parameter t or θ

Derivatives of Functions in Parametric Forms

When coordinates $x$ and $y$ are both expressed in terms of an auxiliary parameter $t$, i.e. $x = f(t)$ and $y = g(t)$, we can obtain $\frac{dy}{dx}$ directly without eliminating the parameter!

\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy / dt}{dx / dt}  \text{(provided } \frac{dx}{dt} \neq 0\text{)}

The Cycloid Curve (Exercise 5.6 Q7)

x = a(θ + sin θ),   y = a(1 - cos θ)

• dx/dθ = a(1 + cos θ) = 2a cos²(θ/2)
• dy/dθ = a(sin θ) = 2a sin(θ/2) cos(θ/2)
• dy/dx = \frac{2a \sin(\theta/2) \cos(\theta/2)}{2a \cos^2(\theta/2)} = \tan(\theta/2)
Remarkably elegant: The slope is simply tan(θ/2)!

The Astroid: x = a cos³t, y = a sin³t

• dx/dt = -3a cos²t sin t
• dy/dt = 3a sin²t cos t
• dy/dx = \frac{3a \sin^2 t \cos t}{-3a \cos^2 t \sin t} = -\frac{\sin t}{\cos t} = -\tan t
At t = π/4: Slope dy/dx = -tan(π/4) = -1.

Second Order Derivatives & Proving Differential Relations

Let $y = f(x)$. If $f'(x)$ is differentiable, its derivative is called the second order derivative of $y$ with respect to $x$, denoted by $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}$, $y''$, or $y_2$.

⚠️ Fatal Parametric Trap for d²y/dx²

Many students incorrectly divide second derivatives: $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} \neq \frac{d^2y/dt^2}{d^2x/dt^2}$.

The Correct Chain Formula:
\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) · \frac{dt}{dx} = \frac{\frac{d}{dt}(\frac{dy}{dx})}{\frac{dx}{dt}}

For $x = at^2, y = 2at$: $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{1}{t}$. Then $\frac{d^2y}{dx^2} = \frac{-1/t^2}{2at} = \frac{-1}{2at^3}$.

Classic Board Question: y = sin⁻¹x

1. y₁ = 1 / √(1 - x²) ⟹ √(1 - x²) y₁ = 1
2. Square both sides: (1 - x²)(y₁)² = 1
3. Differentiate w.r.t x:
  (-2x)(y₁)² + (1 - x²) · 2y₁ y₂ = 0
4. Divide by 2y₁ (since y₁ ≠ 0):
(1 - x²) y₂ - x y₁ = 0  [Q.E.D.!]
