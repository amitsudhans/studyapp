Introduction: Everyday Chemical Changes & Martin H. Fischer's Insight

Every second, countless transformations occur around us: milk souring in summer, iron rusting in moist air, food digesting in our stomachs, and cells respiring. Whenever a chemical change occurs, we say a chemical reaction has taken place.

Physical vs Chemical Changes

• Physical Change: Only state or appearance changes; no new substance is formed (e.g., melting of ice, tearing paper).
• Chemical Change: Chemical bonds break and form new substances with completely different chemical properties (e.g., milk turning to curd, burning wood).

Everyday Situations Highlighted by NCERT

1. Milk left at room temperature during summers (lactic fermentation).
2. Iron tawa/pan/nail left exposed to humid atmosphere (rusting).
3. Grapes get fermented to form wine/alcohol.
4. Food is cooked & food gets digested in our body (hydrolysis by enzymes).
5. Cellular respiration (glucose oxidation releasing energy).

What is a Chemical Reaction?

A process in which one or more substances (reactants) are converted into one or more different substances (products) by the reorganization of atomic bonds.

Activity 1.1: Burning of Magnesium Ribbon in Air

Activity 1.1 is the iconic foundational experiment of Class 10 chemistry. It showcases a vigorous combination reaction between magnesium metal and atmospheric oxygen.

Why Clean the Magnesium Ribbon with Sandpaper?

Magnesium is a reactive metal. When stored, it reacts with atmospheric oxygen and carbon dioxide to form a protective layer of basic magnesium carbonate/oxide [$MgO$ / $MgCO_3$] on its surface. This layer prevents further contact with oxygen. Rubbing with sandpaper removes this protective crust so it can burn smoothly.

Observation & Word Equation

• The ribbon burns with a dazzling white flame (requiring eye protection).
• It converts into a fine white powder collected in a watch-glass.
$$\text{Magnesium} + \text{Oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{Magnesium oxide}$$

Chemical Equation

$$2\text{Mg}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{MgO}(s)$$
Magnesium oxide ($MgO$) is basic in nature. When dissolved in water, it forms magnesium hydroxide [$Mg(OH)_2$], which turns red litmus blue.

Activities 1.2 & 1.3: Yellow Precipitate, Gas Evolution & The 4 Characteristics

How do we know a chemical reaction has occurred? NCERT Activities 1.2 and 1.3 illustrate precipitation, gas evolution, and exothermic temperature spikes.

Activity 1.2: Lead Nitrate + Potassium Iodide

• Mixing colourless lead nitrate solution [$Pb(NO_3)_2(aq)$] with colourless potassium iodide [$KI(aq)$].
• Instant formation of a brilliant yellow precipitate of lead iodide [$PbI_2(s)$]:
$$\text{Pb(NO}_3\text{)}_2(aq) + 2\text{KI}(aq) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{PbI}_2(s) \downarrow} \text{ (yellow)} + 2\text{KNO}_3(aq)$$

Activity 1.3: Zinc Granules + Dilute Acid

• Adding dilute sulphuric acid [$H_2SO_4$] or hydrochloric acid [$HCl$] to zinc granules in a conical flask.
• Vigorous evolution of hydrogen gas ($H_2$) bubbles around the zinc.
• Touching the flask reveals a sharp rise in temperature (exothermic reaction!):
$$\text{Zn}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) + \mathbf{\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow} + \text{Heat}$$

The 4 Hallmarks of a Chemical Reaction

1. Change in State: (e.g., wax burning to $CO_2$ gas and water vapour).
2. Change in Colour: (e.g., lead iodide yellow precipitate).
3. Evolution of a Gas: (e.g., $H_2$ gas from zinc + acid).
4. Change in Temperature: (e.g., heat released in flask).

Section 1.1 • Representation & Law Slide 4 of 21

Chemical Equations: Word Equations, Skeletal Equations & Mass Conservation

A word equation is concise, but chemical formulas make reactions universally precise. The Law of Conservation of Mass mandates that every chemical equation must be balanced.

From Word Equation to Chemical Formula

• Word-equation: $\text{Magnesium} + \text{Oxygen} \longrightarrow \text{Magnesium oxide}$
• Chemical equation: $\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{MgO}$
Reactants are written on the left-hand side (LHS) with plus signs, products on the right-hand side (RHS), and an arrow indicates the reaction direction.

Skeletal Chemical Equation

In $\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow \text{MgO}$, the LHS has 2 oxygen atoms while the RHS has only 1! The mass is not equal on both sides. Such an unbalanced equation is called a skeletal chemical equation.

Why Must Chemical Equations Be Balanced?

According to the Law of Conservation of Mass (Antoine Lavoisier, Class IX):
Total mass of elements in products = Total mass of elements in reactants.
Therefore, the number of atoms of each element must remain strictly identical before and after a reaction.

Checkpoint 2 of 2

Step-by-Step Teacher Explanation:
The Law of Conservation of Mass states that mass cannot be created or destroyed, requiring the number of atoms of each element to be equal on both sides.
Scientific Principle
“Mass can neither be created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction.” — Law of Conservation of Mass

Section 1.1.2 • Step-by-Step Protocol Slide 5 of 21

The Hit-and-Trial Method: Step-by-Step Balancing of Fe + H2O → Fe3O4 + H2

Let us master the textbook's 7-step hit-and-trial method on the classic reaction between iron and steam to form magnetic iron oxide ($Fe_3O_4$) and hydrogen.

Step I: Draw Protective Boxes

Draw boxes around each formula: $\boxed{\text{Fe}} + \boxed{\text{H}_2\text{O}} \longrightarrow \boxed{\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4} + \boxed{\text{H}_2}$. Never change subscripts (e.g., $H_2O$ can never become $H_2O_4$!).

Step II: Element Inventory

• Fe: 1 (LHS) vs 3 (RHS)
• H: 2 (LHS) vs 2 (RHS)
• O: 1 (LHS) vs 4 (RHS)

Step III: Balance Compound with Maximum Atoms ($Fe_3O_4$)

Oxygen has 4 atoms on RHS and 1 on LHS. Put coefficient 4 in front of $H_2O$:
$$\text{Fe} + \mathbf{4}\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + \text{H}_2$$

Step IV & V: Balance Hydrogen and Iron

• LHS now has $4 \times 2 = 8$ H atoms. Put 4 before $H_2$ on RHS:
$$\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + \mathbf{4}\text{H}_2$$
• Balance Fe: RHS has 3 Fe atoms. Put 3 before Fe on LHS:
$$\mathbf{3}\text{Fe} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4 + 4\text{H}_2$$

Step VI: Final Verification

LHS: 3 Fe, 8 H, 4 O. RHS: 3 Fe, 8 H, 4 O. Perfectly balanced!

Physical States, Notation & Reaction Conditions (Photosynthesis & Methanol)

To convey full experimental reality, balanced equations specify physical states $(s, l, g, aq)$ and critical reaction parameters above and below the reaction arrow.

Step VII: Symbols of Physical States

• Solid state: $(s)$
• Liquid state: $(l)$
• Gaseous state: $(g)$
• Aqueous solution (dissolved in water): $(aq)$
$$3\text{Fe}(s) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) \longrightarrow \text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4(s) + 4\text{H}_2(g)$$
Notice that $(g)$ is used for $H_2O$ to emphasize that iron reacts with steam, not liquid water!

Reaction Conditions on the Arrow

1. Methanol Synthesis:
$$\text{CO}(g) + 2\text{H}_2(g) \xrightarrow{340\text{ atm}} \text{CH}_3\text{OH}(l)$$
2. Photosynthesis in Plants:
$$6\text{CO}_2(aq) + 12\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \xrightarrow[\text{Chlorophyll}]{\text{Sunlight}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6(aq) + 6\text{O}_2(aq) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Section 1.2.1 • Combination Reactions Slide 7 of 21

Types of Reactions: Combination Reactions & Quicklime (Activity 1.4)

Chemical reactions occur by breaking existing bonds and forming new ones. When multiple reactants merge into a single compound, we observe a Combination Reaction.

Activity 1.4: Quick Lime + Water

• Take calcium oxide (quick lime, $CaO$) in a beaker.
• Slowly add water ($H_2O$).
• It reacts vigorously, hissing and releasing intense heat:
$$\text{CaO}(s) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq)} + \text{Heat}$$
Reactants: 2 substances ($CaO$ and $H_2O$).
Product: 1 single substance (slaked lime, $Ca(OH)_2$).

More Examples of Combination Reactions

1. Burning of Coal:
$$\text{C}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2(g)$$
2. Formation of Water:
$$2\text{H}_2(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$

Section 1.2.1 • Heat-Releasing Reactions Slide 8 of 21

Whitewashing Chemistry & Exothermic Reactions (Respiration & Natural Gas)

Reactions that release heat along with product formation are Exothermic Reactions. From whitewashed walls gaining a marble sheen to cellular respiration, heat release drives life.

Do You Know? The Chemistry of Whitewashing Walls

• Slaked lime [$Ca(OH)_2$] solution is applied to walls.
• It reacts slowly with carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) in air over 2 to 3 days to form a hard, shiny, protective layer of calcium carbonate ($CaCO_3$):
$$\text{Ca(OH)}_2(aq) + \text{CO}_2(g) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3(s)} + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l)$$
Chemical formula for marble and limestone is also $\mathbf{\text{CaCO}_3}$!

Exothermic Reactions Defined

Reactions in which heat energy is released along with products.
• Burning of Natural Gas (Methane):
$$\text{CH}_4(g) + 2\text{O}_2(g) \longrightarrow \text{CO}_2(g) + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}(g) + \text{Heat}$$
• Respiration: Food carbohydrates break down into glucose, which burns with oxygen in cells:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6(aq) + 6\text{O}_2(aq) \longrightarrow 6\text{CO}_2(aq) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \mathbf{\text{Energy}}$$
• Composting: Decomposition of vegetable matter by microbes.

Section 1.2.2 • Heat-Driven Breakdown Slide 9 of 21

Decomposition Reactions: Thermal Breakdown of FeSO4 & Limestone

When a single compound absorbs energy and cleaves into two or more simpler substances, it undergoes a Decomposition Reaction. When driven by heat, it is Thermal Decomposition.

Activity 1.5: Heating Green Ferrous Sulphate Crystals

• Ferrous sulphate crystals are green because of water of crystallisation: $\text{FeSO}_4 \cdot 7\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
• On gentle heating, they lose water and turn white.
• On strong heating, they decompose into reddish-brown ferric oxide ($Fe_2O_3$) solid, releasing pungent suffocating gases with the smell of burning sulphur: sulphur dioxide ($SO_2$) and sulphur trioxide ($SO_3$):
$$2\text{FeSO}_4(s) \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \mathbf{\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3(s)} + \mathbf{\text{SO}_2(g)} + \mathbf{\text{SO}_3(g)}$$

Thermal Decomposition of Limestone in Industry

$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$$
Limestone decomposes into quicklime ($CaO$) and carbon dioxide. Quicklime is an indispensable raw material used worldwide in the manufacture of cement and glass.

Activity 1.7 • Electrical Cleavage & 2:1 Ratio Slide 11 of 21

Electrolytic Decomposition: Electrolysis of Water (Activity 1.7)

When an electric current passes through acidified water, it splits water molecules into hydrogen and oxygen gases in a strict $2:1$ volume stoichiometry.

Electrolysis Apparatus & Setup

• Plastic mug with carbon (graphite) electrodes fitted through rubber stoppers.
• Inverted water-filled test tubes over electrodes connected to a 6V DC battery.
• A few drops of dilute sulphuric acid are added to pure water to make it conducting (pure water is a poor conductor of electricity).

Electrode Reactions & Gas Identification

• Cathode (Negative Electrode): Attracts $H^+$ ions $\implies$ Hydrogen gas ($H_2$) collects.
• Anode (Positive Electrode): Attracts $O^{2-}$ species $\implies$ Oxygen gas ($O_2$) collects.
• 2:1 Volume Ratio: Volume of $H_2$ collected at cathode is exactly double the volume of $O_2$ at anode because each molecule of water contains 2 hydrogen atoms for every 1 oxygen atom:
$$2\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \xrightarrow{\text{Electricity}} 2\text{H}_2(g) \uparrow + \text{O}_2(g) \uparrow$$

Testing the Gases with a Burning Candle

• Near Cathode ($H_2$): Burns with an audible 'pop' sound.
• Near Anode ($O_2$): Causes the candle flame to burn brighter and rekindle.

Slide Checkpoint Questions & Step-by-Step Teacher Solutions

Checkpoint 1 of 2

Why is the volume of gas collected at the cathode double that of the anode during electrolysis of water?

Step-by-Step Teacher Explanation:
The stoichiometry of water decomposition is $2H_2O \to 2H_2 + O_2$. Thus, 2 volumes of hydrogen gas are produced for every 1 volume of oxygen gas.
Checkpoint 2 of 2

Why are a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid added to water before electrolysis?

Step-by-Step Teacher Explanation:
Pure water is a poor conductor of electricity; adding dilute sulphuric acid dissociates into ions and enables electrical conduction.

Activity 1.8 • Light-Driven Decomposition Slide 12 of 21

Photolytic Decomposition: Silver Salts, Black & White Photography & Endothermic Reactions

Reactions powered by light energy are called Photochemical or Photolytic Decomposition Reactions, historically vital in analog photography.

Activity 1.8: Silver Chloride in Sunlight

• Place white silver chloride ($AgCl$) in a china dish near a sunny window.
• In sunlight, white $AgCl$ slowly turns grey as silver metal precipitates and yellowish-green chlorine gas escapes:
$$2\text{AgCl}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{Sunlight}} 2\mathbf{\text{Ag}(s)} \text{ (grey)} + \text{Cl}_2(g)$$

Silver Bromide & Black-and-White Photography

Silver bromide behaves identically when exposed to light:
$$2\text{AgBr}(s) \xrightarrow{\text{Sunlight}} 2\text{Ag}(s) + \text{Br}_2(g)$$
This photolytic reaction forms the fundamental chemical basis of traditional black and white photography film.

Endothermic Reactions Defined

Reactions in which energy is absorbed from the surroundings in the form of heat, light, or electricity to break bonds are called endothermic reactions.
Example: Mixing barium hydroxide with ammonium chloride absorbs so much heat that the test tube bottom feels freezing cold:
$$\text{Ba(OH)}_2 + 2\text{NH}_4\text{Cl} \longrightarrow \text{BaCl}_2 + 2\text{NH}_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} - \text{Heat}$$

Section 1.2.3 • Single Displacement Slide 13 of 21

Displacement Reactions: Iron Nails in Copper Sulphate & The Reactivity Hierarchy (Activity 1.9)

When an iron nail is immersed in blue copper sulphate solution, a dramatic color swap unfolds: the solution fades to pale green while the nail is plated in brown copper.

Activity 1.9: Iron Nail in Copper Sulphate

• Immerse clean iron nails in blue copper sulphate solution [$CuSO_4(aq)$] for 20 minutes.
• Observation: Blue colour of solution fades and turns light green [$FeSO_4(aq)$].
• The iron nail becomes coated with a reddish-brown layer of metallic copper [$Cu(s)$]:
$$\mathbf{\text{Fe}(s)} + \text{CuSO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{FeSO}_4(aq)} \text{ (light green)} + \mathbf{\text{Cu}(s)} \text{ (reddish-brown)}$$

Why Does This Happen?

Iron is higher in the reactivity series than copper. Iron atoms lose electrons to form $Fe^{2+}$ ions entering solution, while $Cu^{2+}$ ions from solution gain electrons and deposit as copper metal on the nail.

Other Displacement Examples

• Zinc displaces Copper:
$$\text{Zn}(s) + \text{CuSO}_4(aq) \longrightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4(aq) \text{ (colourless)} + \text{Cu}(s)$$
• Lead displaces Copper:
$$\text{Pb}(s) + \text{CuCl}_2(aq) \longrightarrow \text{PbCl}_2(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$$
Both zinc and lead are more reactive than copper.

Section 1.2.4 • Ionic Exchange & Precipitates Slide 14 of 21

Double Displacement & Precipitation Reactions: Exchange of Ions (Activity 1.10)

When two aqueous ionic solutions are mixed, mutual exchange of cations and anions can produce an insoluble solid that separates out as a Precipitate.

Activity 1.10: Sodium Sulphate + Barium Chloride

• Mix colourless sodium sulphate [$Na_2SO_4(aq)$] with colourless barium chloride [$BaCl_2(aq)$].
• An instant opaque white precipitate of barium sulphate ($ ext{BaSO}_4$) forms:
$$\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4(aq) + \text{BaCl}_2(aq) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{BaSO}_4(s) \downarrow} \text{ (white precipitate)} + 2\text{NaCl}(aq)$$

Ionic Mechanism of Double Displacement

The barium cation ($\text{Ba}^{2+}$) binds with the sulphate anion ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$):
$$\text{Ba}^{2+}(aq) + \text{SO}_4^{2-}(aq) \longrightarrow \text{BaSO}_4(s) \downarrow$$
Sodium ($\text{Na}^+$) and chloride ($\text{Cl}^-$) ions remain spectator ions in solution as dissolved $NaCl$.

Precipitation Reaction Defined

Any chemical reaction that produces an insoluble solid precipitate is called a precipitation reaction.
Another Example (Activity 1.2):
$$\text{Pb(NO}_3\text{)}_2(aq) + 2\text{KI}(aq) \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{PbI}_2(s) \downarrow} \text{ (yellow precipitate)} + 2\text{KNO}_3(aq)$$

Section 1.2.5 • Electron & Oxygen Transfer Slide 15 of 21

Oxidation, Reduction & Redox Reactions (Activity 1.11 & Copper Heating)

Oxidation and reduction are twin complementary processes that always occur together. A reaction where one reactant is oxidised while the other is reduced is a Redox Reaction.

Activity 1.11: Heating Copper Powder in Air

• Heat brown copper powder in a china dish over a burner.
• Surface turns coated with black copper(II) oxide ($CuO$) because oxygen is added to copper:
$$2\text{Cu}(s) + \text{O}_2(g) \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} 2\mathbf{\text{CuO}(s)} \text{ (black)}$$
Copper gains oxygen $\implies$ Copper is oxidised.

Passing Hydrogen Gas Over Heated CuO

• If hydrogen gas is blown over hot black $CuO$, the surface turns back to shiny brown copper:
$$\mathbf{\text{CuO}} + \mathbf{\text{H}_2} \xrightarrow{\text{Heat}} \mathbf{\text{Cu}} + \mathbf{\text{H}_2\text{O}}$$
• $CuO$ loses oxygen $\implies$ $CuO$ is reduced to $Cu$ (and acts as the oxidising agent).
• $H_2$ gains oxygen $\implies$ $H_2$ is oxidised to $H_2O$ (and acts as the reducing agent).

More Examples of Redox Reactions

1. $\text{ZnO} + \text{C} \longrightarrow \text{Zn} + \text{CO}$ ($ZnO$ reduced to $Zn$; $C$ oxidised to $CO$).
2. $\text{MnO}_2 + 4\text{HCl} \longrightarrow \text{MnCl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Cl}_2$ ($MnO_2$ reduced; $HCl$ oxidised to $Cl_2$).

Section 1.3 • Everyday Impacts Slide 16 of 21

Everyday Oxidation: Corrosion of Metals & Rancidity of Foods

Oxidation is not confined to laboratory burners. It relentlessly damages metallic infrastructure through Corrosion and spoils edible fats through Rancidity.

1. Corrosion of Metals

When a metal is attacked by environmental substances such as moisture, oxygen, and airborne acids, it corrodes.
• Rusting of Iron: Iron reacts with moist air to form hydrated iron(III) oxide ($Fe_2O_3 \cdot xH_2O$), a reddish-brown flaky crumbly powder.
• Silver Tarnishing: Forms a black coating of silver sulphide ($Ag_2S$) from trace atmospheric sulphur.
• Copper Corrosion: Forms a green coating of basic copper carbonate [$CuCO_3 \cdot Cu(OH)_2$] from moist carbon dioxide.
Corrosion weakens bridges, ship hulls, and railings, costing billions annually.

2. Rancidity of Fats and Oils

When fats and oils in food materials are left exposed to air, they get aerial-oxidised into volatile, foul-smelling aldehydes and carboxylic acids, spoiling their taste and odour.
Prevention Methods:
1. Adding Antioxidants (e.g., BHA, BHT).
2. Storing food in airtight containers (slows oxygen diffusion).
3. Flushing with Nitrogen Gas ($N_2$): Potato chips bags are flushed with unreactive nitrogen gas to displace oxygen and keep chips crispy and fresh!

Core Reaction Classifications

• Combination: $A + B \longrightarrow AB$ (e.g., $CaO + H_2O \to Ca(OH)_2$).
• Decomposition: $AB \longrightarrow A + B$ (Thermal, Electrolytic, Photolytic).
• Displacement: $A + BC \longrightarrow AC + B$ (driven by Reactivity Series).
• Double Displacement: $AB + CD \longrightarrow AD + CB$ (ion exchange, often precipitation).
• Redox: Simultaneous oxidation (gain of O / loss of H) and reduction (loss of O / gain of H).

Energy Categorization

• Exothermic: Heat is released (Burning of fuel, Respiration, Slaking of lime, Composting).
• Endothermic: Heat/Energy is absorbed (Thermal decomposition of $CaCO_3$, Electrolysis of water, $AgCl$ photolysis).
