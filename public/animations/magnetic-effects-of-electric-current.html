Compass Needle & Pole Properties

A compass needle is a miniature pivot-mounted bar magnet:

  • The end pointing approximately towards the geographic north is called the north-seeking or north pole.
  • The opposite end is called the south-seeking or south pole.
  • Fundamental Law of Magnetism: Like poles repel each other (North repels North, South repels South), while unlike poles attract (North attracts South).
  • Intext Q1: A compass needle deflects near a bar magnet because both possess magnetic fields; the magnet exerts magnetic forces of attraction and repulsion on the needle's poles.

Activity 12.2: Iron Filings Alignment Pattern

  • Observation (Fig 12.2): The iron filings rearrange themselves into a striking pattern of smooth concentric curves converging at the poles!
  • Definition of Magnetic Field: The region surrounding a magnet in which the force of the magnet can be detected is said to have a magnetic field.
  • The lines along which the iron filings align themselves represent magnetic field lines.

Activity 12.3: Compass Plotting Procedure

Step-by-step field line mapping (Fig 12.3):

  1. Place a compass near the north pole of a bar magnet. The south pole of the needle points toward the magnet's north pole; the north pole points away.
  2. Mark the position of both ends with fine pencil dots.
  3. Move the compass so its south pole occupies the dot previously occupied by its north pole.
  4. Repeat until reaching the south pole of the magnet.
  5. Join the dots with a smooth curve. Repeating this from various starting points produces the complete field map of Fig 12.4!

Direction & Closed Loop Characteristic

  • Conventional Direction: The direction of the magnetic field is taken to be the direction in which a hypothetical free north pole would move.
  • Outside the Magnet: Field lines emerge from the North pole and merge at the South pole.
  • Inside the Magnet: Field lines continue from the South pole to the North pole.
  • Continuous Closed Loops: Unlike electrostatic field lines (which start on positive charges and terminate on negative charges), magnetic field lines have no free monopole ends—they form continuous, unbroken closed loops!

Properties of Field Lines & The Non-Intersection Law

Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator
Teacher's Interactive Audio Commentary

"No two magnetic field lines can ever intersect—for at an intersection, a compass would face the physical impossibility of pointing in two directions at once."

Magnetic field is a vector quantity; line density indicates magnitude while tangent arrows denote field direction.
Interactive Lab 12.4: Non-Intersection Proof & Field Crowding
Crowded lines at poles demonstrate high field strength. Field lines never intersect! Click button to explore the two-direction paradox.

Vector Nature & Degree of Closeness

  • Vector Quantity: Magnetic field has both magnitude (strength) and direction.
  • Tangent Direction: The tangent to a field line at any point gives the direction of the magnetic field vector $\vec{B}$ at that point.
  • Degree of Closeness: The relative strength of the magnetic field is shown by how crowded the field lines are.
    • Near the Poles: Field lines are densely packed $\implies$ Field is strongest.
    • Far from the Magnet: Field lines spread wide apart $\implies$ Field is weak.
  • Uniform Field: Represented by equidistant, parallel straight lines.

The Non-Intersection Proof (Board Favorite!)

Why do two magnetic field lines never cross each other?

  • Assume for contradiction that two field lines could intersect at point $P$.
  • The compass needle always points along the net resultant magnetic field at any point.
  • At an intersection point $P$, there would be two tangents, meaning two distinct directions of magnetic field at the same single point!
  • Consequently, a compass needle placed at $P$ would have to point in two different directions simultaneously.
  • Since this is physically impossible, no two magnetic field lines can ever intersect!

Rule Statement & Visualization (Fig 12.7)

Right-Hand Thumb Rule Statement:

"Imagine holding a current-carrying straight conductor in your right hand such that your thumb points in the direction of current. Then your curled fingers wrap around the conductor in the direction of the magnetic field lines."

  • Upward Current: Curled fingers wrap anticlockwise (when viewed from above).
  • Downward Current: Curled fingers wrap clockwise (when viewed from above).
  • Maxwell's Corkscrew Rule: Driving a corkscrew forward along current rotates the handle in the magnetic field direction.

Worked Example 12.1 (Horizontal Power Line)

Example 12.1: Current through a horizontal overhead power line flows from East to West. What is the direction of the magnetic field at a point directly below it and directly above it?

  • Point your right thumb towards the West (direction of current).
  • Your fingers curl over the top from South to North and loop underneath from North to South.
  • At a point directly below: Field is directed towards the North (turns clockwise when viewed from the East end).
  • At a point directly above: Field is directed towards the South (anti-clockwise when viewed from the West end).

Interactive Lab 12.8: Circular Current Loop & Center Straight Line Field Figure 12.8

Field Geometry of a Circular Loop (Fig 12.8)

  • Near the wire, field lines form tight concentric circles around each wire segment.
  • Moving away from the wire into the interior of the loop, the concentric circles expand into wide arcs.
  • At the Centre of the Loop: The arcs flatten out completely and appear as uniform parallel straight lines perpendicular to the plane of the loop!
  • Additive Contribution: Applying the right-hand thumb rule to any segment reveals that every segment pushes field lines in the same direction through the inside of the loop!

Clock Face Rule for Loop Magnetic Polarity

A current-carrying loop behaves like a thin magnetic disc with North and South faces:

  • South Pole (Clockwise): If looking at a face of the loop, the current flows in a clockwise direction, that face behaves as a South pole (S). Field lines enter the face!
  • North Pole (Anticlockwise): If current flows in an anticlockwise direction, that face behaves as a North pole (N). Field lines emerge from the face!
  • Intext Q1: A loop lying on a table carries clockwise current $\implies$ Field lines point downward into the table inside the loop, and point upward outside the loop!

Because current in each turn flows in the exact same direction, their individual magnetic fields add together algebraically: B = n · B₁!

The N-Fold Multiplier Law: B ∝ n · I

Why does an $N$-turn coil produce an $N$-times stronger field?

  • Each turn carries the exact same current $I$ in the same rotational sense.
  • Because magnetic fields obey the principle of superposition, the field vector at the center is the algebraic sum of the fields of each individual turn:

$$B_{\text{total}} = B_1 + B_2 + \dots + B_n = n \times B_{\text{single turn}}$$

  • A coil of 100 turns generates a magnetic field 100 times stronger than a single loop carrying the same current!
  • This linear multiplication is the foundation of powerful electromagnets, electric motors, and transformers.

Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator

Solenoid Definition & Field Pattern (Fig 12.10)

Definition: A coil of many circular turns of insulated copper wire wrapped closely in the shape of a cylinder is called a solenoid.

  • Bar Magnet Analogy: The magnetic field pattern outside a solenoid is identical to that of a bar magnet (Fig 12.10)! One end behaves as a North pole, the other as a South pole.
  • Uniform Internal Field: The field lines inside the solenoid are parallel straight lines. This indicates that the magnetic field is the same at all points inside the solenoid (completely uniform!).
  • Intext Q3: The magnetic field inside a long straight current-carrying solenoid is the same at all points!

Electromagnet Construction (Fig 12.11)

  • How an Electromagnet is Made: A strong magnetic field produced inside a solenoid is used to magnetise a piece of magnetic material, like a soft iron core, placed inside the coil.
  • Why Soft Iron? Soft iron has high magnetic permeability and low retentivity: it magnetises strongly when current is ON, and demagnetises almost completely when current is switched OFF.
  • Advantages of Electromagnets:
    • Magnetism can be switched ON and OFF instantly.
    • Magnetic strength can be varied by adjusting current ($I$) or number of turns ($n$).
    • Polarity can be reversed by reversing current direction.

Interactive Lab 12.11: The Kicking Aluminium Rod (Activity 12.7)
Current flows B to A; magnetic field is vertically upward. By Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, force pushes rod towards the LEFT!

Activity 12.7: The Kicking Aluminium Rod (Fig 12.12)

Experimental setup with suspended aluminium rod $AB$:

  • A $5\text{ cm}$ aluminium rod $AB$ is suspended horizontally between the poles of a strong horseshoe magnet (North pole below, South pole above $\implies$ magnetic field directed vertically upward).
  • When current flows from $B$ to $A$, the rod is displaced towards the left!
  • Reversing Current ($A$ to $B$): The displacement flips towards the right!
  • Reversing Magnetic Field (Poles swapped): The displacement also reverses!
  • Conclusion: A mechanical force acts on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field, perpendicular to both current and magnetic field!

Factors Governing the Magnetic Force (F = B I l sin θ)

  • The displacement of the rod (magnitude of force) increases when:
    1. Current ($I$) is increased in the rod.
    2. A stronger magnet (higher $B$) is used.
    3. Length ($l$) of the conductor inside the field is increased.
  • Angular Condition: The force is maximum when current is at right angles ($90^\circ$) to the magnetic field lines.
  • If the conductor is parallel to the magnetic field ($0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$), the force is zero!

Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (Fig 12.13)

Stretch thumb, forefinger, and middle finger of the left hand mutually perpendicular:

  • First finger (Forefinger): Points in direction of Magnetic Field ($B$).
  • Second finger (Middle finger): Points in direction of Current ($I$).
  • Thumb: Points in direction of Motion / Force ($F$) acting on conductor!
  • Mnemonic: F-B-I (Thumb = Force, Forefinger = B-Field, Middle = I-Current) or Father-Mother-Child (Force, Magnetic field, Current).
  • Practical Applications: Electric motors, loudspeakers, microphones, and galvanometers.

Charged Particles: Examples 12.2 & Intext Q3

  • Example 12.2 (Electron Entering Magnetic Field Fig 12.14):
    Electron moves downward; hence conventional current points upward! Magnetic field is to the right. Applying Fleming's Left-Hand Rule gives force directed into the page (Option d)!
  • Intext Q1 (Proton in Magnetic Field): A proton's velocity and momentum change because magnetic force curves its trajectory, altering direction even though speed remains constant!
  • Intext Q3 (Alpha Particle Projected West Deflected North):
    Positive particle moves West $\implies$ Current is West. Force is North. By Fleming's left hand rule, magnetic field must be directed upward (Option d)!

Bio-Magnetic Origin: Ionic Nerve Currents

  • An electric current always produces a magnetic field—even in biological tissues!
  • When we touch an object, nerves carry an electrical impulse ($Na^+ / K^+$ ion flow) along axons to muscles and the central nervous system.
  • These ionic currents produce transient, microscopic magnetic fields.
  • Magnitude: Bio-magnetic fields are exceptionally weak—approximately one-billionth ($10^{-9}$) of the Earth's magnetic field!

The Heart, The Brain & Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI)

  • Two Main Organs: The two human organs where bio-magnetic fields are most significant are the heart and the brain.
  • MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging):
    • Uses powerful magnetic fields and radiofrequency pulses to interact with hydrogen nuclei (protons) in body water and fats.
    • Generates high-resolution cross-sectional slices of soft tissues without harmful ionizing X-ray radiation.
    • Revolutionized detection of tumors, brain disorders, spinal cord trauma, and cardiovascular disease.

Color Codes & Domestic Supply Parameters

Standard domestic supply in India (220 V AC, 50 Hz):

  • Live Wire (Positive, L): Red insulation. Carries high potential (220 V relative to ground).
  • Neutral Wire (Negative, N): Black insulation. Maintained at zero potential (0 V).
  • Potential Difference: $V = 220\text{ V} - 0\text{ V} = \mathbf{220\text{ V}}$ AC at $50\text{ Hz}$.
  • Two Domestic Circuits:
    • 15 A Rating: For heavy appliances with high power consumption (geysers, air coolers, room heaters).
    • 5 A Rating: For lower-power appliances (bulbs, fans, televisions, chargers).

The Safety Earth Wire (Green Insulation)

  • Earth Wire (E): Has green insulation and is connected to a thick copper plate buried deep in moist earth near the building.
  • Connection: Firmly bonded to the metallic outer body of appliances (electric press, toaster, refrigerator).
  • How Earthing Prevents Fatal Shock: If a damaged live wire touches the metal casing, current immediately rushes through the low-resistance earth wire into the ground rather than through a user's body!
  • This heavy fault current instantly blows the circuit fuse, isolating the faulty appliance safely!

Short-Circuiting vs Overloading Defined

  • Short-Circuiting: Occurs when the live wire and neutral wire come into direct physical contact (due to damaged insulation or internal appliance fault).
    • Resistance drops to near zero $\implies$ Current surges violently to hundreds of amperes!
    • Can produce sparks, explosions, and catastrophic building fires.
  • Overloading: Occurs when the total current drawn exceeds the safe carrying capacity of the circuit.
    • Causes: (1) Connecting too many high-power appliances to a single socket, (2) Accidental spike in supply mains voltage.

The Safety Fuse & Domestic 2 kW Oven Problem

  • Working Principle of Fuse: The fuse wire has a low melting point. An excessive current produces intense Joule heating ($H = I^2Rt$), melting the wire and cleanly breaking the circuit.
  • Intext Q2: 2 kW Electric Oven on a 5 A Line (220 V):
    Power $P = 2\text{ kW} = 2000\text{ W}, V = 220\text{ V}$.
    Current drawn: $I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{2000}{220} = \mathbf{9.09\text{ A}}$.
    Result: Since $9.09\text{ A}$ greatly exceeds the $5\text{ A}$ circuit rating, the fuse wire will immediately heat up, melt, and break the circuit!
  • Precautions: Never plug multiple heavy appliances into one multi-plug adapter; use appropriately rated fuses and MCBs.

Exercises Questions 6 to 9 Solutions

Q6: Electron Beam Deflection in Chamber:
Electron moves back $\to$ front $\implies$ Current flows front $\to$ back. Deflection (Force) is to the right. Applying Fleming's Left-Hand Rule, the magnetic field is directed vertically DOWNWARD.

Q7: State Rules to Determine Directions:
(i) Field around straight wire: Right-hand thumb rule.
(ii) Force on conductor: Fleming's left-hand rule.
(iii) Induced current in rotating coil: Fleming's right-hand rule.

Q8: When Does Short Circuit Occur?
When the live wire and neutral wire come in direct contact due to worn insulation or appliance fault, causing circuit resistance to drop to zero and current to surge dangerously.

Q9: Function of Earth Wire:
Provides a low-resistance path to ground for any leakage current from metal-bodied appliances, keeping the casing at zero potential and preventing lethal electric shocks.
