Visibility & Rectilinear Propagation

During the day, sunlight illuminates the environment. An opaque object reflects light that falls upon it. When this reflected light enters our eyes, photoreceptors stimulate our optic nerves, enabling vision.

By observing everyday optical phenomena, we conclude that light travels in straight lines in a homogeneous transparent medium. The sharp shadow cast by an opaque object from a small light source directly confirms this rectilinear propagation, conventionally represented as a ray of light.

More to Know: Wave-Particle Duality & Diffraction

If an obstacle on the path of light is exceptionally minute, light deviates around the edges rather than following a strict geometric line — a phenomenon known as diffraction. Geometric optics using simple rays fails at this nanoscale.

While diffraction requires light to behave as a wave, 20th-century photo-electric discoveries revealed light behaving as discrete particles (photons). Modern Quantum Electrodynamics resolves this by uniting wave and particle characteristics into quantum duality.

The Universal Laws of Reflection

Regardless of whether a reflecting boundary is flat or curved, two inviolable laws govern reflection:

  • First Law: The angle of incidence ($\angle i$) is strictly equal to the angle of reflection ($\angle r$), measured relative to the normal line ($i = r$).
  • Second Law: The incident ray, the normal to the mirror at the point of incidence, and the reflected ray all lie in the same geometric plane.

Characteristics of Plane Mirror Images

Images produced by standard flat plane mirrors exhibit five definite properties:

  • Virtual & Erect: Formed behind the mirror where rays appear to diverge; cannot be captured on a physical screen.
  • Same Size: Magnification $m = +1$; image height equals object height ($h' = h$).
  • Equidistant: Image distance behind mirror equals object distance in front ($v = -u$).
  • Laterally Inverted: Right-hand side of object appears as left-hand side of image.

Interactive Lab 9.3: Spherical Mirror Anatomy & Curvature

Concave vs Convex Mirror Anatomy

In Activity 9.1, observing a polished steel spoon reveals two curved mirrors: the inward depression acts as a concave mirror, while the bulging back acts as a convex mirror.

A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface curves inward (facing the centre of the sphere) is concave. A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface curves outward is convex. The non-reflecting rear surface is silvered and coated with protective opaque paint.

Core Geometric Terminology

  • Pole ($P$): The geometric centre of the spherical reflecting surface. Lies directly on the mirror.
  • Centre of Curvature ($C$): Centre of the sphere from which the mirror was cut. Lies in front of concave mirrors, but behind convex mirrors. Not part of the mirror surface!
  • Radius of Curvature ($R$): Radius of the sphere ($PC = R$).
  • Principal Axis: Straight line passing through $P$ and $C$, normal to the mirror at $P$.
  • Aperture ($MN$): Effective diameter of the circular boundary of the mirror.

Activity 9.2: Solar Concentration & Paper Ignition

In Activity 9.2, a concave mirror directed toward the sun focuses incident solar rays onto a sheet of paper. As the paper is adjusted, a pinpoint, intensely bright spot appears. Within moments, the paper chars, emits smoke, and bursts into flames!

This bright spot is the real image of the sun formed at the principal focus ($F$). The concentration of radiant energy generates high temperature. The measured distance from mirror to paper gives the approximate focal length ($f$).

Mathematical Relationship: $R = 2f$

For concave mirrors, parallel rays converge to a single real focus $F$ in front of the mirror (converging mirror). For convex mirrors, reflected rays diverge and appear to originate from focus $F$ behind the mirror (diverging mirror).

For all spherical mirrors of small aperture, the principal focus lies precisely halfway between the pole and centre of curvature:
$$R = 2f \quad \iff \quad f = \frac{R}{2}$$

Interactive Lab 9.5: The 4 Fundamental Ray Tracing Rules
Rule 1: A ray incident parallel to the principal axis passes through the principal focus F after reflection (Fig 9.3a).

Rule 1 & Rule 2: Parallel vs Focal Rays

  • Rule 1 (Parallel Ray): A ray incident parallel to the principal axis, after reflection, passes through the principal focus $F$ (concave mirror) or appears to diverge from $F$ (convex mirror) [Fig 9.3].
  • Rule 2 (Focal Ray): A ray passing through the principal focus $F$ (concave) or directed toward $F$ (convex), after reflection, emerges strictly parallel to the principal axis [Fig 9.4].

Rule 3 & Rule 4: Centre of Curvature & Oblique Pole Rays

  • Rule 3 (Normal Ray through $C$): A ray passing through or directed toward $C$ retraces its exact path backwards after reflection [Fig 9.5]. This occurs because radial lines from $C$ are perpendicular to the sphere surface, meaning $\angle i = 0^\circ \implies \angle r = 0^\circ$.
  • Rule 4 (Oblique Ray at Pole $P$): A ray incident obliquely at the pole $P$ reflects symmetrically on the other side such that the principal axis acts as normal ($\angle i = \angle r$) [Fig 9.6].

New Cartesian Sign Convention for Reflection

Chapter 9 • Pages 142–143

To solve numerical mirror problems accurately, we map mirror geometry onto a Cartesian coordinate system with the pole as origin.

Key Takeaway
Rigorous algebraic signs prevent optical calculations from descending into chaos.
Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Pay close attention to sign conventions! Think of the mirror's pole P as the origin (0,0). Anything to the left is negative; anything to the right is positive. Upward heights are positive; downward heights are negative. Therefore, object distance u is always negative, concave focal length is negative, and convex focal length is positive!"

Interactive Lab 9.9: New Cartesian Coordinate Sign Plane
Object distance $u$ is universally negative. Concave focal length $f < 0$; Convex focal length $f > 0$. Upward heights $+h$; downward heights $-h'$.

The 5 New Cartesian Rules (Figure 9.9)

  1. The object is always placed to the left of the mirror. Light strikes the mirror traveling left-to-right.
  2. All distances parallel to the principal axis are measured from the Pole ($P$) as coordinate origin $(0, 0)$.
  3. Distances measured in the direction of incident light (along $+x$-axis, to the right of $P$) are taken as positive ($+$).
  4. Distances measured against incident light (along $-x$-axis, to the left of $P$) are taken as negative ($-$).
  5. Heights measured perpendicular and above the principal axis are positive ($+y$); heights below are negative ($-y$).

Critical Sign Takeaways for Mirrors

  • Object Distance ($u$): Always negative ($-$) for all mirrors because the object is in front.
  • Focal Length of Concave Mirror: Focus is in front $\implies$ negative ($-f$).
  • Focal Length of Convex Mirror: Focus is behind $\implies$ positive ($+f$).
  • Real Image: Formed in front $\implies v < 0$; inverted $\implies h' < 0$.
  • Virtual Image: Formed behind $\implies v > 0$; erect $\implies h' > 0$.

Mirror Formula & Linear Magnification

For all spherical mirrors across all object positions:

$$\frac{1}{v} + \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

Linear Magnification ($m$): Ratio of image height $h'$ to object height $h$:

$$m = \frac{h'}{h} = -\frac{v}{u}$$

Note the minus sign! Negative $m$ indicates a real inverted image; positive $m$ indicates a virtual erect image.

Walkthrough: Examples 9.1 & 9.2

  • Example 9.1 (Convex Mirror): $R = +3.00\text{ m} \implies f = +1.50\text{ m}$, $u = -5.00\text{ m}$.
    $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{f} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{1.50} - \frac{1}{-5.00} = \frac{6.50}{7.50} \implies v = +1.15\text{ m}$ (behind mirror).
    $m = -\frac{v}{u} = -\frac{+1.15}{-5.00} = +0.23$ (virtual, erect, diminished by factor 0.23).
  • Example 9.2 (Concave Mirror): $h = +4.0\text{ cm}, u = -25.0\text{ cm}, f = -15.0\text{ cm}$.
    $\frac{1}{v} = \frac{1}{-15} - \frac{1}{-25} = \frac{-2}{75} \implies v = -37.5\text{ cm}$ (screen placed 37.5 cm in front).
    $h' = -\frac{v}{u} h = -\frac{-37.5}{-25.0} \times 4.0 = -6.0\text{ cm}$ (real, inverted, magnified).

Refraction through a Rectangular Glass Slab

Key Takeaway

Dr. Sophia • Physics Educator
Teacher's Live Audio Commentary Web Speech Narration

"Trace the ray in Activity 9.10! Entering the glass slab, light bends toward the normal. Exiting into air, it bends away by the exact same angle! This equal and opposite bending means the emerging beam travels strictly parallel to the incident beam, shifted sideways by a lateral displacement."

Interactive Lab 9.12: Glass Slab Refraction & Lateral Shift
45°
Equal and opposite bending at parallel faces ensures the emergent ray is strictly parallel to the incident ray, offset by lateral displacement $d$.

Activity 9.10 Protocol & Ray Path

Four pins ($E, F, G, H$) are aligned across a glass block $ABCD$ [Figure 9.10]:

  • Interface 1 ($AB$ Air $\to$ Glass): Ray travels from an optically rarer medium (air) to an optically denser medium (glass). The ray slows down and bends towards the normal ($i_1 > r_1$).
  • Interface 2 ($CD$ Glass $\to$ Air): Ray travels from an optically denser medium (glass) to an optically rarer medium (air). The ray speeds up and bends away from the normal ($i_2 < r_2$).

Lateral Shift & Normal Incidence

Because opposite faces $AB$ and $CD$ are strictly parallel, the extent of bending at both boundaries is equal and opposite. Consequently, the emergent ray is strictly parallel to the original incident ray path.

However, the beam is shifted sideways by a perpendicular distance called lateral displacement (lateral shift).
Special Case: If a ray strikes the slab normally ($i = 0^\circ$), it passes straight through without any deviation!

Laws of Refraction & Snell's Law

Chapter 9 • Page 148

Just as reflection follows precise geometric laws, refraction is governed by two fundamental physical principles formulated by Willebrord Snell.

The First Law of Refraction

The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface separating two transparent media at the point of incidence, all lie in the same geometric plane.

This coplanar law ensures that refraction never deviates into a third spatial dimension.

The Second Law of Refraction (Snell's Law)

For light of a given colour (wavelength) and for a given pair of media, the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant (for $0^\circ < i < 90^\circ$):

$$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant} = n_{21}$$

This constant value $n_{21}$ is called the refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium.

Speed of Light & Absolute Refractive Index

Let speed of light in air/vacuum be $c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ and in a given medium be $v$. The absolute refractive index ($n_m$) is:

$$n_m = \frac{c}{v}$$

Relative index: $n_{21} = \frac{v_1}{v_2}$ and $n_{12} = \frac{v_2}{v_1} \implies n_{21} = \frac{1}{n_{12}}$.
For glass with $n = 1.50$: $v = \frac{c}{n} = \frac{3 \times 10^8}{1.50} = 2.0 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$.

Convex vs Concave Lens Anatomy

  • Convex Lens (Double Convex): Thicker at the middle than at the edges. Bends incident parallel rays inward to intersect at a real focus $\implies$ Converging Lens [Figure 9.12a].
  • Concave Lens (Double Concave): Thicker at the edges than at the middle. Bends incident parallel rays outward, appearing to diverge from a virtual focus $\implies$ Diverging Lens [Figure 9.12b].

Core Landmarks: Optical Centre & Dual Foci

  • Optical Centre ($O$): The central point of the lens. A ray of light passing through $O$ emerges completely undeviated!
  • Centres of Curvature ($C_1, C_2$ or $2F_1, 2F_2$): Centres of the two bounding spheres.
  • Dual Principal Foci ($F_1, F_2$): Because light can enter from either face, every lens possesses two symmetrical principal foci equidistant from $O$.
  • Activity 9.11: Concentrating solar rays through a convex lens burns paper at focus $F$, providing a simple measure of focal length $f$.

Lens Formula & Linear Magnification

For all spherical lenses with Cartesian signs applied:

$$\frac{1}{v} - \frac{1}{u} = \frac{1}{f}$$

The SI unit of optical power is the dioptre (D). Short focal length bends light through sharper angles, yielding greater dioptric power!

Power Formula & The Dioptre Unit

A convex lens of short focal length bends light through large angles, focusing it close to $O$. The power ($P$) of a lens of focal length $f$ (in metres) is:

$$P = \frac{1}{f\text{ (in metres)}}$$

The SI unit of optical power is the dioptre (D). $1\text{ D} = 1\text{ m}^{-1}$.
Convex lens ($f > 0$) $\implies$ Positive Power ($+$).
Concave lens ($f < 0$) $\implies$ Negative Power ($-$).

Combinations of Lenses in Contact

When multiple lenses with powers $P_1, P_2, P_3, \dots$ are placed in direct contact, the net total power $P$ is their direct algebraic sum:

$$P = P_1 + P_2 + P_3 + \dots$$

Opticians use this additive property in testing spectacle frames. For example, combining $+2.0\text{ D}$ and $+0.25\text{ D}$ yields $+2.25\text{ D}$. Multi-lens systems eliminate chromatic and spherical aberrations in cameras, microscopes, and telescopes.
