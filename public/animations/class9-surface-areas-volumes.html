Now master the universal blueprint inside Section Thirteen Point One Point One: THE STANDARD FORM OF A LINEAR EQUATION IN TWO VARIABLES! Every linear equation in x and y can be written cleanly as a x plus b y plus c equals zero, where a, b, and c are real numbers, AND a and b are NOT both zero simultaneously! Why must we forbid a and b both being zero? Because if a equals zero and b equals zero, both variables vanish, leaving a false or trivial statement like c equals zero! Watch two essential conversions! First, suppose you have fractional coefficients like x over two minus y over three equals five! Multiply the entire equation by the least common multiple six to clear fractions, then shift everything to the left: three x minus two y minus thirty equals zero, giving integers a equals three, b equals minus two, and c equals minus thirty! Second, what about a single-variable equation like x equals four? Write it in two-variable standard form by inserting zero times y: one x plus zero y minus four equals zero! Similarly, y equals minus seven becomes zero x plus one y plus seven equals zero—keeping our algebraic format completely uniform!

Now turn to Section Thirteen Point Two: SOLUTIONS OF A LINEAR EQUATION IN TWO VARIABLES! Unlike a single-variable equation with just one answer, a two-variable equation like two x plus y equals eight has an INFINITE family of solutions! What does a solution look like? It is an ORDERED PAIR (x, y) whose x-value and y-value make the left-hand side equal the right-hand side simultaneously! Notice the word ORDERED! Swapping the two numbers matters! Test (2, 4) in two x plus y equals eight: two times two plus four equals eight—TRUE! Now swap them to (4, 2): two times four plus two equals ten, NOT eight—FALSE! Pick ANY real number you like for x, substitute it into the equation, and solve the remaining one-variable equation for y! Take x equals zero: y equals eight, giving (0, 8)! Take x equals one: y equals six, giving (1, 6)! Take x equals five: y equals minus two, giving (5, −2)! Since x can be chosen from infinitely many real numbers—integers, fractions, or decimals—EVERY linear equation in two variables possesses infinitely many ordered-pair solutions!

Rise Over Run

Section 13.3.1 Understanding Slope: Rise/Run, Positive, Negative, Zero & Undefined

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Welcome to Section Thirteen Point Three Point One: UNDERSTANDING SLOPE! Why do some lines climb steeply like a mountain trail while others glide gently or plunge downward? We measure steepness with a single number called SLOPE, denoted by the letter m! Pick ANY two distinct points (x one, y one) and (x two, y two) on a straight line! Slope m equals RISE divided by RUN: the vertical change (y two minus y one) divided by the horizontal change (x two minus x one)! Because similar right triangles along a straight line share the same ratio, slope m stays completely CONSTANT everywhere along that line! Classify all four slope types! First, POSITIVE SLOPE (m greater than zero): the line rises upward from left to right, and larger m means steeper climb! Second, NEGATIVE SLOPE (m less than zero): the line falls downward from left to right! Third, ZERO SLOPE (m equals zero): y two minus y one is zero, giving a perfectly HORIZONTAL line like y equals five! Fourth, UNDEFINED SLOPE: x two minus x one is zero—meaning division by zero—giving a perfectly VERTICAL line like x equals three, pointing straight up and down vertically!

Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now connect standard form directly to slope inside Section Thirteen Point Three Point Two: THE SLOPE-INTERCEPT FORM y equals m x plus d! Start with any non-vertical line in standard form: a x plus b y plus c equals zero, where b is not zero! Solve algebraically for y! Subtract a x and c from both sides: b y equals minus a x minus c! Now divide every term by b: y equals (minus a over b) times x plus (minus c over b)! Compare this directly with y equals m x plus d! Immediately we prove TWO golden formulas! First, the coefficient of x is the SLOPE m equals minus a over b! Second, the constant term is the Y-INTERCEPT d equals minus c over b—because setting x equals zero leaves y equals d! Test this on three x plus two y minus eight equals zero! Here a equals three, b equals two, c equals minus eight! Instantly, slope m equals minus three over two (falling downward), and y-intercept d equals minus of minus eight over two, which is positive four! Whenever two lines share the exact same slope m with different intercepts d, they are strictly PARALLEL!

Now solve simultaneous systems algebraically inside Section Thirteen Point Five Point One! Method One is THE SUBSTITUTION METHOD—ideal whenever one variable already has coefficient one or minus one! Follow our crisp four-step recipe on a real system: Equation One is x plus y equals fourteen, and Equation Two is three x minus two y equals twelve! Step One: Isolate one variable from the simpler equation! From Equation One, subtract y to express x in terms of y: x equals fourteen minus y! Step Two: SUBSTITUTE that expression (fourteen minus y) in place of x inside the OTHER equation! Equation Two transforms into three times (fourteen minus y) minus two y equals twelve! Notice x has vanished, leaving a single-variable equation in y! Step Three: Expand and solve for y! Forty-two minus three y minus two y equals twelve gives forty-two minus five y equals twelve, so five y equals thirty, yielding y equals six! Step Four: Back-substitute y equals six into x equals fourteen minus y to get x equals eight! Always verify (8, 6) in BOTH original equations: eight plus six is fourteen, and twenty-four minus twelve is twelve—confirming our exact unique solution pair!

On Slide Nine, we master Method Two inside Section Thirteen Point Five Point One: THE ELIMINATION METHOD! When neither variable has coefficient one, substitution creates messy fractions! Elimination avoids fractions completely by matching the coefficient of one variable and adding or subtracting the equations! Test this on two equations: Equation One is three x plus two y equals nineteen, and Equation Two is five x minus three y equals nineteen! Suppose we want to eliminate y! Step Three: Substitute x equals five into three x plus two y equals nineteen: fifteen plus two y equals nineteen, so y equals two! Solution: (5, 2)!

Algebraic Solution Criteria

Section 13.5.1 Algebraic Solvability Rules: Coefficient Ratio Tests

Slide 10 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Before spending five minutes solving a system, wouldn't it be wonderful to know INSTANTLY whether it has one solution, infinitely many solutions, or NO solution at all? Inside Section Thirteen Point Five Point One, COEFFICIENT RATIO CRITERIA give you X-ray vision into any system a one x plus b one y plus c one equals zero and a two x plus b two y plus c two equals zero! Case One—UNIQUE SOLUTION: If a one over a two is NOT EQUAL to b one over b two, the two lines have different slopes, so they MUST cross at exactly ONE point! Example: two x plus three y equals seven and four x minus y equals five, since two-fourths does not equal three over minus one! Case Two—INFINITELY MANY SOLUTIONS: If ALL THREE ratios match—a one over a two equals b one over b two equals c one over c two—the second equation is simply a scaled copy of the first! Both equations describe the exact same line, sharing infinitely many solutions! Case Three—NO SOLUTION: If a one over a two equals b one over b two, BUT does NOT equal c one over c two, the lines share the same slope yet have different intercepts—meaning parallel lines that NEVER meet!

When we plot BOTH equations a one x plus b one y plus c one equals zero AND a two x plus b two y plus c two equals zero on the SAME Cartesian coordinate plane, geometry reveals the algebra before our eyes! Every point on Line One satisfies Equation One, and every point on Line Two satisfies Equation Two! Third, COINCIDENT LINES: one line lies completely on top of the other, so EVERY point on the line is shared, giving INFINITELY MANY SOLUTIONS and a dependent consistent system!
