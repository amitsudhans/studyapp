Chemical Word Equations

Reactants, Products, Reaction Arrows, and Skeletal Formulas

Slide 4 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Describing chemical reactions using full descriptive prose can be cumbersome. Chemists developed chemical equations as concise, standardized shorthand representations. Consider our magnesium combustion: writing out full sentences is condensed into a word equation: Magnesium plus Oxygen yields Magnesium oxide. In any chemical equation, the starting chemical substances that undergo change are called reactants. Reactants are placed on the left-hand side with plus signs separating multiple substances. The new substances produced during the chemical change are called products, written on the right-hand side. An arrow points from reactants to products, representing the chemical direction of the reaction. We advance further by substituting chemical names with precise chemical formulas: Mg plus O2 yields MgO. This formula representation is known as a skeletal chemical equation. Notice something crucial here: on the left side, we have one magnesium atom and two oxygen atoms, while the right side shows only one magnesium atom and one oxygen atom. Because the number of oxygen atoms on the reactant side does not match the product side, this skeletal equation is unbalanced. In our next study session, we will explore why nature requires every single skeletal equation to be strictly balanced before any laboratory calculation or stoichiometry.

Conservation of Mass

Lavoisier's Principle, Constant Atom Numbers, and Stoichiometry

Slide 5 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

The foundation of chemical equation balancing rests upon the universal Law of Conservation of Mass, formulated by French chemist Antoine Lavoisier. This fundamental physical law states that matter can neither be created nor destroyed in any chemical reaction. Consequently, the total mass of all elements present in the reaction products must exactly equal the total mass of all elements present in the starting reactants. Because mass is an intrinsic property of individual atoms, this law dictates that the total number of atoms of each element must remain constant before and after a chemical reaction. When bonds break and re-form, atoms merely trade partners like dancers in a ballroom; not a single atomic nucleus vanishes, and no new atoms materialize out of empty space. If an unbalanced skeletal equation shows two oxygen atoms entering a reaction but only one emerging, it violates mass conservation. Balancing an equation means finding whole-number stoichiometric coefficients placed in front of chemical formulas so that atom counts for every element on the reactant side match the product side identically. Mastering this fundamental principle ensures that quantitative calculations, yield forecasts, and industrial chemical manufacturing adhere strictly to physical reality and atomic mass conservation rules.

Combination Reactions Overview

Single Product Synthesis, Coal Burning, and Water Formation

Slide 7 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

Now let us classify chemical reactions systematically, beginning with combination reactions. By definition, a combination reaction is a process in which two or more reactants combine chemically to form a single product. The general format is simple: reactant A plus reactant B yields compound AB. Consider the combustion of coal in a furnace: solid carbon reacts with gaseous oxygen from the air to synthesize one single product, carbon dioxide gas: C solid plus O2 gas yields CO2 gas. Similarly, when hydrogen gas burns in the presence of oxygen, water is synthesized: two molecules of hydrogen gas combine with one molecule of oxygen gas to yield two molecules of liquid water. In both cases, multiple distinct elemental or compound reactants unite into a single unified product species. Combination reactions are often energetically favorable because new bond formations release substantial bonding energy. These reactions form the bedrock of geological mineral formation, industrial chemical production like synthetic ammonia, and everyday atmospheric combustion. Whenever you observe several starting substances merging into one single chemical formula on the right side of the reaction arrow, you can confidently identify that chemical transformation as an authentic, single-product combination reaction in fundamental laboratory chemistry.

Exothermic Chemical Reactions

Heat Liberation, Methane Combustion, Respiration, and Composting

Slide 9 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

We now explore chemical energetics, specifically exothermic chemical reactions. By definition, reactions in which heat energy is liberated along with the formation of products are classified as exothermic reactions. A familiar domestic example is the combustion of natural gas, primarily methane: CH4 gas plus two molecules of oxygen gas combust to yield carbon dioxide gas, water vapor, and substantial thermal energy. Have you ever wondered why eating food keeps our body warm? Cellular respiration is a vital exothermic biochemical process. During digestion, carbohydrates break down into simple glucose. In our body cells, this dissolved glucose combines with inhaled oxygen through enzymatic oxidation: C6H12O6 aqueous plus 6O2 yields 6CO2 plus 6H2O, liberating the metabolic energy that powers all biological cellular activity. Another natural exothermic transformation occurs during the decomposition of vegetable peelings, garden clippings, and animal dung into agricultural compost inside bio-digesters. Thermophilic microbes break molecular bonds, generating noticeable heat within the compost pile. In every exothermic reaction, the chemical potential energy stored in the reactant bonds exceeds the energy of product bonds, with the surplus energy discharged outward into the surrounding laboratory environment as heat, radiant warmth, or light, elevating the ambient system temperature naturally.

Not all decomposition reactions rely on heat; electrical energy can also cleave chemical bonds. This process is termed electrolytic decomposition or electrolysis. In Activity 1.7, two holes are drilled in the base of a plastic mug, fitted with rubber stoppers and graphite carbon electrodes. The mug is filled with water, and a few drops of dilute sulphuric acid are added to increase electrical conductivity. Two inverted graduated test tubes filled with water are positioned over the electrodes, and a 6-volt direct current battery is connected. Immediately, gas bubbles begin forming at both electrodes, displacing water downward by downward displacement. Carefully observe the collected gas volumes: the volume of gas gathered at the negative electrode, called the cathode, is exactly double the volume collected at the positive electrode, called the anode. Electrochemical cleavage splits two molecules of liquid water into two molecules of diatomic hydrogen gas at the cathode and one molecule of diatomic oxygen gas at the anode. Testing with a glowing splinter confirms that cathode gas burns with a pop, while anode gas rekindles the flame vigorously.

When chemical decomposition is initiated by radiant light energy, the reaction is termed photolysis or photolytic decomposition. In Activity 1.8, placing two grams of pure white silver chloride in a clean china dish on a sunny windowsill reveals a fascinating light-activated change. We now explore single displacement reactions. In a displacement reaction, a more chemically reactive element displaces or expels a less reactive element from its aqueous compound. Activity 1.9 illustrates this principle cleanly. Clean iron nails, scrubbed with sandpaper to remove surface grease, are immersed into a test tube containing deep blue copper sulphate solution for twenty minutes. When retrieved, the iron nails have acquired a distinctive reddish-brown coating, while the brilliant blue solution has faded to a pale light-green tint. What occurred on the atomic scale? Iron occupies a higher position than copper on the chemical reactivity series, meaning iron has a stronger thermodynamic tendency to lose electrons. These experiments confirm that more reactive electropositive metals reliably displace less active noble metals from their dissolved aqueous salts in predictable single displacement fashion inside chemistry laboratories.

Double displacement reactions involve a mutual exchange of ions between two aqueous reactant compounds to produce two entirely new substances. The general pattern is: compound AB plus compound CD yields AD plus CB. In Activity 1.10, three millilitres of aqueous sodium sulphate solution are mixed with three millilitres of aqueous barium chloride solution. Instantly, an opaque, brilliant white insoluble solid forms throughout the mixture. Any chemical reaction that generates an insoluble solid precipitate is classified as a precipitation reaction. What is the identity of this white precipitate? Barium cations, carrying a plus two charge, unite with sulphate anions, carrying a minus two charge, to precipitate insoluble barium sulphate: Na2SO4 aqueous plus BaCl2 aqueous yields BaSO4 solid precipitate plus two NaCl aqueous. The sodium and chloride ions remain dissolved as soluble table salt in the water. Double displacement reactions are pivotal in analytical chemistry, wastewater heavy-metal removal, and industrial chemical pigment synthesis used throughout modern textile, paper, and paint manufacturing facilities.

Oxidation and Reduction

Activity 1.11: Copper Oxidation, Hydrogen Reduction, and Redox Transfers

Slide 15 of 20
👨‍🏫
Classroom Teacher Spoken Lecture Pure Indian Male Teacher Sound

We now examine oxidation and reduction reactions, commonly unified as redox reactions. Classically, oxidation is defined as the gain of oxygen or the loss of hydrogen by a substance. Conversely, reduction is defined as the loss of oxygen or the gain of hydrogen. Activity 1.11 demonstrates both processes sequentially. When brown copper powder is heated in a china dish over a burner, its surface turns jet black. Copper metal combines with atmospheric oxygen to form black copper(II) oxide: two Cu plus O2 yields two CuO. Here, copper gains oxygen and is oxidised. Now, if hot hydrogen gas is passed over this blackened copper oxide, the black coating turns back into lustrous reddish-brown copper metal: CuO plus H2 yields Cu plus H2O. In this reverse step, copper oxide loses oxygen and is reduced to copper metal, while hydrogen gas gains oxygen and is oxidised to water. Because oxidation and reduction always occur concurrently, such coupled transformations are called redox reactions. Other classic redox examples include zinc oxide reacting with carbon to produce zinc metal and carbon monoxide, and manganese dioxide reacting with hydrochloric acid to yield manganese chloride, water, and chlorine gas, demonstrating fundamental electron and oxygen atom transfers across chemical systems.

Metal Degradation, Oxide Patinas, Food Oxidation, and Nitrogen Packaging

To conclude our chemical foundation, let us observe the widespread effects of oxidation in daily life: corrosion and rancidity. Corrosion is the gradual degradation of metals when attacked by substances in their surrounding environment, such as moisture, oxygen, and acids. The most infamous example is the rusting of iron: in damp monsoon air, iron oxidizes into hydrated ferric oxide, a flaky reddish-brown powder that detaches, exposing fresh metal to continuous destruction. Silver ornaments develop a tarnished black coating of silver sulphide due to atmospheric hydrogen sulphide, while copper vessels develop a green patina of basic copper carbonate. Corrosion causes billions of rupees in structural damage annually to bridges, railway lines, car bodies, and ships. Meanwhile, rancidity represents the oxidative degradation of fats and edible oils in stored food. Over time, atmospheric oxygen oxidizes lipid molecules, creating volatile fatty acids and aldehydes with foul tastes and unpleasant odours. Food processors prevent rancidity by adding synthetic antioxidants like BHT, storing snacks in airtight containers, and flushing bags of potato chips with unreactive nitrogen gas to displace oxygen completely, ensuring prolonged crispness, nutritional safety, and fresh taste across commercial food supply chains operating worldwide in modern food logistics.
