Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Algebra allows us to generalize mathematical relationships using symbols. Let us dissect the fundamental components of algebraic language:

1. Constants & Variables

Constant: A symbol having a fixed numerical value (e.g. $5, -3, 3/4, \pi$).
Variable: A symbol (usually letters like $x, y, z, t$) that can take various real numerical values.

2. Terms & Coefficients

In the expression $5x^2 - 3x + 7$:

  • The terms are $5x^2$, $-3x$, and $+7$.
  • Coefficient of $x^2$ is $5$.
  • Coefficient of $x$ is $-3$ (note the negative sign!).
  • The constant term is $7$.

Like Terms vs Unlike Terms:

Terms having the exact same variables raised to the same exponents are called like terms (e.g. $7x$ and $-2x$; $4x^2y$ and $9x^2y$). Only like terms can be combined through addition or subtraction: $7x - 2x = 5x$. Unlike terms cannot be combined: $3x + 4y$ cannot be simplified further.

"What distinguishes a polynomial from a general algebraic expression? A polynomial in one variable x is an algebraic expression where the exponent of the variable x in every single term is strictly a Whole Number—meaning zero or a positive integer. If an expression contains negative powers, fractions in exponents, or variables in denominators, it is NOT a polynomial."

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Formal Mathematical Definition:

An algebraic expression of the form:

$P(x) = a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + \dots + a_1 x + a_0$

is called a Polynomial in one variable $x$, provided:

  • The coefficients $a_n, a_{n-1}, \dots, a_0$ are Real Numbers ($\in \mathbb{R}$).
  • $a_n \neq 0$ (leading coefficient).
  • The exponents $n, n-1, \dots$ are strictly Whole Numbers ($n \in \mathbb{W} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$).
✓ Valid Polynomials:
  • $2x^3 - 5x + 7$ (Powers: $3, 1, 0 \in \mathbb{W}$)
  • $\sqrt{3} x^2 + 4$ (Coefficient can be irrational; power is $2$)
  • $y^4 - \frac{2}{3}y$ (Real coefficients, whole powers)
  • $8$ (Constant polynomial: $8x^0$)
✗ NOT Polynomials (Disqualified):
  • $\frac{1}{x} = x^{-1}$ (Negative exponent $-1 \notin \mathbb{W}$)
  • $\sqrt{x} = x^{1/2}$ (Fractional exponent $1/2 \notin \mathbb{W}$)
  • $x + \frac{2}{x^2} = x + 2x^{-2}$ (Variable in denominator)
  • $3x^{2/3} + 5$ (Fractional index)

Introduction to Linear Polynomials

Standard Form P(x) = ax + b (a ≠ 0), Zeroes & Root Geometry

Unit II • Algebra: Polynomials (66 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Algebra & Modeling)

"A linear polynomial is a polynomial of degree one. Its standard algebraic form is P of x equals ax plus b, where a and b are real numbers and a cannot be zero. The value of x for which P of x equals zero is called the zero or root of the polynomial. For any linear polynomial, there is always exactly one unique real zero: x equals minus b over a."

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Standard Form of Linear Polynomial:

$P(x) = ax + b, \\quad \\text{where } a, b \\in \\mathbb{R} \\text{ and } a \\neq 0$

The term $ax$ is the linear term, $a$ is the leading coefficient (slope), and $b$ is the constant term.

Finding the Zero (Root) of $P(x)$:

A real number $k$ is a zero of $P(x)$ if $P(k) = 0$. To find the zero algebraically, set $P(x) = 0$:

$ax + b = 0 \\implies ax = -b \\implies k = -\\frac{b}{a}$

Fundamental Geometric Theorem: Every linear polynomial has exactly one unique real zero. Graphically, this corresponds to the point where the line crosses the horizontal $x$-axis: $(-b/a, 0)$.

Example 1: $P(x) = 2x - 6$

$2x - 6 = 0 \\implies 2x = 6 \\implies x = 3$.
The unique zero is $x = 3$.

Example 2: $P(x) = 5x + 4$

$5x + 4 = 0 \\implies 5x = -4 \\implies x = -4/5$.
The unique zero is $x = -0.8$.

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Linear relationships are governed by the principle of constant rate of change.

The First Differences Rule:

Consider an input-output table with evenly spaced inputs ($x$ values increasing by $\Delta x = 1$):

Input ($x$) 0 1 2 3 4
Output ($y$) 2 5 8 11 14
First Diff ($\\Delta y$) - +3 +3 +3 +3

Because $\Delta y = 3$ is constant for each $\Delta x = 1$, the slope is $a = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = 3$. At $x=0$, $y=2$, so $b=2$. The linear rule is: $\mathbf{y = 3x + 2}$.

Computational Pattern Test:

If $\Delta y$ is constant $\implies$ Linear ($y = ax + b$).
If the second differences $\Delta^2 y$ are constant $\implies$ Quadratic ($y = ax^2 + bx + c$).
If the ratio $y_{k+1}/y_k$ is constant $\implies$ Exponential ($y = a \cdot b^x$).

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Mathematical Model of Linear Growth:

$y = ax + b, \\quad \\text{with } a > 0$
  • $y$: Total accumulated quantity (dependent variable).
  • $x$: Units elapsed (time, distance, hours, items) (independent variable).
  • $a$: Positive growth rate (slope, change per unit).
  • $b$: Initial value / base offset (value of $y$ when $x = 0$).

A city taxi charges a base fare of $₹50$ plus $₹12$ for each kilometer traveled ($d$ km):

$F(d) = 12d + 50$

For a $10$ km trip: $F(10) = 12(10) + 50 = ₹170$.

Application 2: Plant Height Growth

A bamboo sapling starts at $15$ cm and grows steadily at $3$ cm per day ($t$ days):

$H(t) = 3t + 15$

After $20$ days: $H(20) = 3(20) + 15 = 75$ cm.

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Mathematical Model of Linear Decay:

$y = -|a|x + b \\quad \\text{or} \\quad y = ax + b, \\quad \\text{with } a < 0$

The line slopes downward from left to right. As $x$ increases, $y$ steadily decreases until it hits zero (the horizontal intercept).

Application 1: Water Tank Drainage

A $1000$-liter water reservoir drains at a constant rate of $25$ liters per minute ($t$ min):

$V(t) = -25t + 1000$

When will it be completely empty ($V = 0$)?
$0 = -25t + 1000 \\implies 25t = 1000 \\implies t = 40$ minutes.

Application 2: Smartphone Battery Drain

A phone starts at $100\\%$ battery and discharges at $8\\%$ per hour of screen time ($h$):

$B(h) = -8h + 100$

Battery after $6$ hours: $B(6) = -8(6) + 100 = 52\\%$.

Linear Relationships in Two Variables

Connecting x and y: Implicit Form (Ax + By + C = 0) vs Slope-Intercept Form

Unit II • Algebra: Polynomials (66 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Algebra & Modeling)

"So far, we have studied polynomials in one variable. When we equate a linear polynomial to a second variable y, we obtain a Linear Equation in Two Variables: y equals ax plus b. We can also write it in the standard general form Ax plus By plus C equals zero. Every solution to this equation is an ordered pair of numbers (x, y) that satisfies the equality."

Interactive Lab 2.8: Cartesian Coordinate Plane Grapher
Graph of y = 2x - 1: A straight line crossing the y-axis at (0, -1) and the x-axis at (0.5, 0).

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

An equation involving two variables with highest power 1 for each variable is called a Linear Equation in Two Variables.

1. General (Standard) Form:

$Ax + By + C = 0$

where $A, B, C$ are real numbers, and $A$ and $B$ are not both zero ($A^2 + B^2 \neq 0$). Example: $3x + 2y - 12 = 0$.

2. Slope-Intercept (Explicit) Form:

$y = mx + c \quad \text{or} \quad y = ax + b$

Solving for $y$: $By = -Ax - C \implies y = \left(-\frac{A}{B}\right)x + \left(-\frac{C}{B}\right)$. Here slope $m = -A/B$ and $y$-intercept $c = -C/B$.

Infinitely Many Solutions:

Unlike a linear equation in one variable (which has exactly 1 solution), a linear equation in two variables has infinitely many solutions! For any chosen value of $x$, there exists a corresponding value of $y$ that satisfies the equation. Each solution is written as an ordered pair $(x, y)$.

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

The Geometric Fundamental Theorem of Linear Equations:

The graph of every linear equation in two variables $Ax + By + C = 0$ is a Straight Line on the Cartesian coordinate plane.

  • Every point $(x_1, y_1)$ lying on the line is a solution to the equation.
  • Every solution $(x_1, y_1)$ to the equation corresponds to a point lying on the line.
  • Any point not on the line is NOT a solution.
Horizontal Lines ($y = c$):

When $A = 0$, the equation becomes $By + C = 0 \implies y = c$. The line is parallel to the $x$-axis. Slope $m = 0$. Example: $y = 3$.

Vertical Lines ($x = k$):

When $B = 0$, the equation becomes $Ax + C = 0 \implies x = k$. The line is parallel to the $y$-axis. Slope is undefined. Example: $x = -2$.

The Slope of a Line (m or a)

Geometric Steepness, Rise Over Run, Rate of Change (Δy/Δx) & Angle of Inclination

Unit II • Algebra: Polynomials (66 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Algebra & Modeling)

"The slope of a line, denoted by m or a, measures its steepness and direction. It is defined as the vertical change, known as the rise, divided by the horizontal change, known as the run. Given any two distinct points on the line, the slope equals y2 minus y1 divided by x2 minus x1. A line going uphill has positive slope, while downhill has negative slope."

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Formal Definition of Slope ($m$):

$m = \frac{\text{Rise}}{\text{Run}} = \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}, \quad \text{for } x_2 \neq x_1$

The slope represents the constant rate at which $y$ increases for every $1$ unit increase in $x$.

Positive ($m > 0$) Slopes upward to right (Growth)
Negative ($m < 0$) Slopes downward to right (Decay)
Zero ($m = 0$) Horizontal line ($\Delta y = 0$)
Undefined Vertical line ($\Delta x = 0$)

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

1. The $y$-Intercept ($b$):

The value of $y$ where the line crosses the vertical $y$-axis (where $x = 0$):

$(0, b)$

In real-world models, the $y$-intercept represents the initial state or starting value at time zero.

2. The $x$-Intercept:

The value of $x$ where the line crosses the horizontal $x$-axis (where $y = 0$):

$\left(-\frac{b}{a}, 0\right)$

The $x$-intercept is precisely the zero of the linear polynomial $P(x) = ax + b$.

Example: Find Intercepts of $2x + 3y = 6$
  • $x$-intercept: Set $y = 0 \implies 2x + 3(0) = 6 \implies 2x = 6 \implies x = 3$. Point: $(3, 0)$.
  • $y$-intercept: Set $x = 0 \implies 2(0) + 3y = 6 \implies 3y = 6 \implies y = 2$. Point: $(0, 2)$.

"Euclid’s first postulate tells us that two distinct points determine a unique straight line. Therefore, to graph any linear equation, we only need to compute two points—ideally the x-intercept and the y-intercept. However, in good engineering practice, we always calculate a third test point. If all three points lie on the same straight line, our graph is guaranteed to be error-free."

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

Here is the standard algorithmic procedure to graph any linear equation $Ax + By = C$:

The 3-Point Graphing Algorithm:

  1. Point 1 ($y$-intercept): Set $x = 0$, solve for $y \implies P_1(0, y_1)$.
  2. Point 2 ($x$-intercept): Set $y = 0$, solve for $x \implies P_2(x_2, 0)$.
  3. Point 3 (Verification Check): Pick an intermediate integer $x = x_3$, calculate $y_3 \implies P_3(x_3, y_3)$.
  4. Collinearity Test: Plot $P_1, P_2, P_3$. Lay a straightedge ruler across them. If they are collinear (all 3 fall on one line), draw the line through them and extend with arrows on both ends.
What if the line passes through the origin $(0, 0)$?

If $C = 0$ (e.g. $y = 2x$), both intercepts coincide at $(0, 0)$. In this case, simply choose two other convenient non-zero inputs, such as $x = 1 \implies y = 2$ and $x = -1 \implies y = -2$, to obtain two distinct points.

Direct Variation vs General Linear Models

Proportional Models (y = kx) vs Affine Models (y = ax + b)

Unit II • Algebra: Polynomials (66 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Algebra & Modeling)

"In science and commerce, some relationships start at zero—such as cost proportional to weight of fruit, or distance traveled at constant speed. This is Direct Variation: y equals kx, which always passes through the origin. When a fixed starting cost or offset is added, it becomes a general linear model y equals ax plus b, shifting the line upward or downward by b units."

Interactive Lab 2.13: Algorithmic Equation Extractor
Input Dataset: (x1, y1) = (2, 7), (x2, y2) = (5, 16)
Step 1: Compute Slope a = (16 - 7) / (5 - 2) = 9 / 3 = 3
Step 2: Solve Intercept b = y1 - a(x1) = 7 - 3(2) = 1
Discovered Formula: y = 3x + 1 | Prediction for x = 10: y = 31
Algorithmic Automation: This 4-step logic is the exact algorithm modern data analysis tools use to fit linear regression models to empirical measurements.

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

1. Direct Proportionality ($y = kx$):

When the constant term is zero ($b = 0$):

$y = kx \implies \frac{y}{x} = k = \text{constant}$
  • Always passes through the origin $(0, 0)$.
  • Doubling $x$ exactly doubles $y$.
  • $k$ is the constant of variation (slope).

2. General Linear (Affine) Model ($y = ax + b$):

When an initial fixed offset $b \neq 0$ is present:

$y = ax + b$
  • Does NOT pass through $(0, 0)$; passes through $(0, b)$.
  • Doubling $x$ does NOT double $y$.
  • Example: Temperature $F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$.

Professor Sophia's Insight: Because F/C is not constant (due to +32). At C = 0, F = 32 ≠ 0, so the line does not pass through the origin.

Computational Pattern Recognition & Extrapolation

Algorithmic Discovery of Equations from Datasets & Future Predictions

Unit II • Algebra: Polynomials (66 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Algebra & Modeling)

"In data science and computational thinking, machines are given tables of inputs and outputs and must automatically discover the underlying formula. The algorithm is straightforward: first calculate the rate of change delta y over delta x to find slope a. Second, solve for intercept b using any known data point. Once the equation y equals ax plus b is found, we can extrapolate and predict any future output."

Interactive Lab 2.14: Linear Kinematics Simulator ($d = vt + d_0$)
Speed (v): 20 m/s
Speed Slider (m/s):
Initial Pos $d_0$ (m):
Kinematic Distance Equation:
d(t) = 20t + 50

At t = 5 seconds: d(5) = 20(5) + 50 = 150 meters

Physics Connection: Speed v is the slope (rate of change of distance with respect to time). Initial position d0 is the y-intercept.

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

The 4-Step Computational Discovery Algorithm:

  1. Step 1 (Compute Slope): Take any two distinct points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ from the dataset:

    $a = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$

  2. Step 2 (Compute Intercept): Substitute slope $a$ and point $(x_1, y_1)$ into $y = ax + b$:

    $b = y_1 - a x_1$

  3. Step 3 (Synthesize Model): Assemble the predictive equation $y = ax + b$.
  4. Step 4 (Extrapolate & Predict): To find the output for any target input $x_{target}$, compute $y_{pred} = a x_{target} + b$.

Systems of Linear Relationships: Break-Even Analysis

Simultaneous Lines, Intersection Point (x*, y*) & Economic Break-Even

Unit II • Algebra: Polynomials (66 Periods)
Prof. Sophia • Math Lead
Professor Sophia's Audio Walkthrough (Algebra & Modeling)

"What happens when two different linear relationships operate in the same scenario? In business, a company has a Cost function with a fixed overhead plus manufacturing cost, and a Revenue function from selling products. Where these two lines intersect is the Break-Even Point. Before this point, the business operates at a loss; after this point, it generates profit."

Interactive Lab 2.15: Break-Even Economics Solver
Units produced ($x$): 20 units
Total Cost C(x) ₹1200 20x + 800
Total Revenue R(x) ₹1200 60x
Net Profit / Loss ₹0 (Break-Even) R(x) - C(x)
Break-Even Point: x* = 20 units Cost and Revenue curves intersect at x = 20. Producing fewer than 20 units results in loss; producing more yields net profit!

Curriculum Concepts, Models & Computational Thinking

The Break-Even Formulation:

Total Cost Function: $C(x)$

$C(x) = v x + F$

$F$ = Fixed overhead costs, $v$ = variable production cost per unit.

Total Revenue Function: $R(x)$

$R(x) = p x$

$p$ = Selling price per unit ($p > v$).

Finding the Break-Even Point ($x^*$):

Equate Cost and Revenue: $R(x^*) = C(x^*)$:

$p x^* = v x^* + F \implies (p - v) x^* = F \implies x^* = \frac{F}{p - v}$

Example: $F = ₹1000$, variable cost $v = ₹20$, selling price $p = ₹40$. Break-even quantity: $x^* = 1000 / (40 - 20) = 50$ units.
