Valence Electron Sharing

Tetravalent Dilemma: Energy Barriers to Ionisation and Covalent Resolution

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Why does carbon choose to share electrons rather than form ionic bonds like sodium or magnesium? Carbon has atomic number six, giving it an electronic configuration of two, four, with four electrons residing in its outer valence shell. To attain the stable octet of the nearest noble gas, neon, carbon requires four additional electrons. However, gaining four electrons to form a C4 minus anion is energetically impossible: the tiny carbon nucleus containing only six protons cannot stably hold ten electrons due to overwhelming inter-electronic repulsion. Conversely, losing four valence electrons to form a C4 plus cation is equally prohibitive: removing four successive electrons demands an astronomical quantity of ionisation energy, leaving a naked, unstable nucleus holding just two inner electrons. Because neither anion nor cation formation is thermodynamically viable, carbon overcomes this obstacle through a brilliant chemical alternative: sharing its four valence electrons with other carbon atoms or with atoms of other elements. By mutually sharing electron pairs, each participating atom attains a completely filled, ultra-stable valence shell without creating destabilising electrical charges. This mutual sharing of valence electron pairs constitutes a covalent bond, unlocking the vast, stable architecture of organic chemistry across the natural universe.

Covalent Bond Types

Single, Double, and Triple Electron Sharing in Simple Molecules

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Covalent bonding is not exclusive to carbon; it represents a universal mechanism by which non-metals achieve noble gas electronic stability. The simplest illustration is the diatomic hydrogen molecule, H2. A hydrogen atom possesses atomic number one with a single valence electron in its K-shell. Two hydrogen atoms share one pair of electrons, allowing both to attain the stable duplet configuration of helium, joined by a single covalent bond represented by a single line. In chlorine, Cl2, two atoms with configuration two, eight, seven share one pair of electrons to complete their octets. In a methane molecule, CH4, a central tetravalent carbon atom shares its four valence electrons with four individual hydrogen atoms, creating four robust single covalent bonds. When atoms require two electrons to complete their octet, they share two electron pairs to form a double covalent bond, as seen in diatomic oxygen, O2, where two oxygen atoms share four electrons. When atoms require three electrons, as in diatomic nitrogen, N2, each atom contributes three valence electrons, resulting in a powerful triple covalent bond. In carbon dioxide, CO2, carbon forms two separate double bonds with two flanking oxygen atoms, illustrating multi-centre covalent sharing throughout molecular chemistry.

Carbon Allotrope Structures

Diamond Tetrahedral Network, Graphite Planar Layers, and C60 Fullerene

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Elemental carbon demonstrates that structural geometry dictates physical behaviour through allotropy—the phenomenon where an element exists in multiple distinct physical forms with identical chemical identities but contrasting physical architectures. In diamond, each carbon atom is covalently linked to four adjacent carbon atoms in a rigid, three-dimensional tetrahedral network. Because every single valence electron is locked firmly in localised covalent bonds and the inter-atomic spacing is compact, diamond is the hardest natural substance known to science, with an extraordinary melting point, while behaving as an electrical insulator. Conversely, in graphite, each carbon atom bonds to only three neighbouring carbon atoms within flat, planar hexagonal arrays. This specific geometry leaves one delocalised valence electron per carbon atom free to move across the sheets. The parallel hexagonal layers are held together only by weak van der Waals forces, allowing them to slide smoothly over one another; hence, graphite is soft, slippery, and an exceptional electrical conductor. A third allotrope, discovered in the year 1985, is Buckminsterfullerene, C60, where sixty carbon atoms assemble into twenty hexagons and twelve pentagons, forming a hollow spherical cage resembling the geodesic domes designed by famous American architect Buckminster Fuller in innovative modern architectural engineering.

Allotropes of Carbon: Diamond, Graphite & Fullerene

Diamond: Rigid 3D Tetrahedral Network

Each carbon bonded to 4 others in rigid tetrahedral array. Hardest known natural substance; electrical insulator.

Graphite: Hexagonal Planar Sheets & Delocalised Electrons

Each carbon bonded to 3 others; free delocalised electron conducts electricity! Layers slide smoothly (lubricant).

Buckminsterfullerene (C60): Geodesic Football Cage

60 carbon atoms assembled into 20 hexagons and 12 pentagons resembling Buckminster Fuller's geodesic domes.

Carbon Versatile Nature

Catenation Self-Linking, Tetravalency, and Compact Nuclear Stability

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Chemists have catalogued millions of distinct carbon compounds, vastly outnumbering the chemical compounds formed by all other elements of the periodic table combined together in science. What unique atomic superpowers explain carbon's versatile nature in the physical world? Two foundational properties account for this astonishing molecular diversity: catenation and tetravalency combined with compact atomic size. Catenation is the unparalleled ability of an element to form strong, stable covalent bonds with other atoms of its own kind, giving rise to gigantic molecular architectures. Carbon atoms can link together continuously to forge unbranched straight chains, intricately branched dendrites, and closed cyclic rings of virtually unlimited size. Furthermore, carbon-carbon linkages can incorporate single, double, or triple covalent bonds freely. While silicon also exhibits catenation with hydrogen, forming silanes up to seven or eight atoms, silicon-silicon bonds are weak and chemically reactive; in contrast, carbon-carbon bonds are extraordinarily strong, robust, and thermodynamically resilient. The second vital factor is carbon's tetravalency combined with its small atomic radius. Because carbon possesses only two compact electron shells, its nucleus is situated very close to the shared valence electron pairs, holding them with immense electrostatic tenacity and conferring exceptional thermodynamic stability on carbon compounds throughout nature.

Vital Force Demise

Overthrow of Vitalism and Friedrich Wöhler's 1828 Urea Synthesis

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

For centuries, early scientists believed that carbon compounds synthesized by living organisms were fundamentally distinct from inanimate mineral compounds found in the earth. It was long standing dogma that organic compounds could only be fabricated inside biological tissues under the influence of a mysterious, supernatural life energy termed the vital force. Consequently, chemists believed it was inherently impossible to manufacture an organic compound artificially from non-living inorganic mineral reagents in a laboratory test tube. This mystical dogma collapsed completely in 1828 when German chemist Friedrich Wöhler performed a landmark experiment in his laboratory. Wöhler heated ammonium cyanate—a purely inorganic, crystalline mineral salt—and discovered that its atoms rearranged to produce urea, a well-known organic compound excreted in animal urine: ammonium cyanate yields urea upon thermal treatment. This monumental discovery proved conclusively that organic substances obey the exact same physical and chemical laws of valence and thermodynamics as inorganic compounds, requiring no mystical vital force whatsoever. Although the vital force theory was permanently discredited, all carbon compounds—excluding simple oxides like carbon monoxide and dioxide, carbonates, and hydrogencarbonates—continue to be studied systematically under the specialized branch known as organic chemistry because of their vast numbers and unique catenated bonding.

Hydrocarbon Saturation States

Single-Bonded Alkanes versus Double-Bonded Alkenes and Triple-Bonded Alkynes

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Hydrocarbons are fundamental organic compounds composed exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms, classified into two broad primary categories based on their internal bonding: saturated and unsaturated hydrocarbons. Saturated hydrocarbons are compounds in which all adjacent carbon atoms are linked exclusively by single covalent bonds. These compounds are known systematically as alkanes, following the general formula C n H 2n plus 2. Everyday representative examples include methane CH4, ethane C2H6, and propane C3H8. Because every carbon valence is completely satisfied by single sigma bonds, saturated hydrocarbons are relatively stable and chemically unreactive under normal laboratory conditions. Conversely, unsaturated hydrocarbons contain at least one multiple bond between adjacent carbon atoms. Unsaturated compounds containing one or more double bonds are termed alkenes, possessing the general formula C n H 2n; the simplest alkene is ethene, C2H4, where two carbon atoms share four electrons. Unsaturated compounds containing one or more carbon-carbon triple bonds are termed alkynes, following the general formula C n H 2n minus 2; the simplest alkyne is ethyne, C2H2, commonly known as industrial acetylene gas. Unsaturated hydrocarbons are substantially more reactive than saturated alkanes because multiple electron-rich pi bonds readily open up to undergo rapid addition reactions.

Chains Branches Rings

Structural Isomerism in Butane and Cyclic Saturated and Aromatic Ring Systems

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Carbon's catenation capability allows carbon skeletons to assemble into diverse topological arrangements: straight continuous chains, branched frameworks, and closed cyclic rings. As explored in Table 4.2, straight-chain alkanes extend from methane through hexane, C6H14. When a hydrocarbon contains four or more carbon atoms, an intriguing chemical phenomenon arises: structural isomerism. Structural isomers are chemical compounds that possess the exact same molecular formula but exhibit completely different structural arrangements of atoms in space. For instance, with butane, formula C4H10, carbon atoms can link into a continuous four-carbon unbranched chain called normal-butane, or three carbon atoms can form a main chain with the fourth carbon branching off the central atom to form isobutane, systematically named 2-methylpropane. Both isomers share the formula C4H10 but exhibit distinctly different boiling points and densities. Carbon chains can also bend and join end-to-end to form closed cyclic rings. In cyclohexane, formula C6H12, six carbon atoms form a saturated hexagonal ring linked by single bonds, with each carbon carrying two hydrogens. In contrast, benzene, formula C6H6, is an unsaturated aromatic hexagonal ring containing three alternating carbon-carbon double bonds and three single bonds, forming an exceptionally stable conjugated electronic ring system in modern organic chemistry.

Heteroatom Functional Groups

Heteroatom Substitutions, Table 4.3 Families, and Chemical Reactivity Centres

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

In hydrocarbon chains, carbon atoms establish an extraordinarily accommodating and flexible chemical scaffold. Hydrogen atoms bonded to carbon can be readily substituted by atoms of other elements without altering the underlying four-fold carbon valency. Elements that replace hydrogen in carbon frameworks—most commonly halogens like chlorine and bromine, oxygen, nitrogen, and sulphur—are systematically termed heteroatoms. When heteroatoms confer specific chemical reactivity to the organic molecule regardless of the chain length or nature of the carbon framework, they and the specific atomic groups they form are designated as functional groups. Table 4.3 outlines the five cardinal functional group families every chemistry student must master. First, haloalkanes replace hydrogen with chlorine or bromine, designated as chloro- or bromo- derivatives. Second, the alcohol functional group introduces an oxygen-hydrogen moiety, written as minus OH. Third, the aldehyde group features a terminal carbonyl carbon bonded to hydrogen, represented as minus CHO. Fourth, ketones incorporate an internal carbonyl carbon bonded between two flanking carbon atoms, represented as greater-than C double-bond O. Finally, carboxylic acids incorporate a terminal carbonyl and hydroxyl group together, written as minus COOH. Functional groups are the true chemical engines that dictate molecular reactivity across organic synthesis in nature.

Homologous Chemical Series

Gradation of Physical Constants, Identical Reactivity, and the 14u Increment

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

When we arrange organic molecules sharing the same functional group in order of increasing chain length, an elegant mathematical pattern emerges: a homologous series. A homologous series is defined as a family of organic compounds containing identical functional groups where successive members differ by a specific repeating structural unit: a minus CH2 minus group, commonly called a methylene bridge. Consider the homologous family of alkanes: methane CH4, ethane C2H6, propane C3H8, and butane C4H10. Calculating the difference between any two sequential members reveals exactly one carbon atom and two hydrogen atoms. Because the atomic mass of carbon is twelve units and hydrogen is one unit, successive members invariably differ by fourteen atomic mass units. In Activity 4.2, examining the alcohol series—methanol CH3OH, ethanol C2H5OH, propanol C3H7OH, and butanol C4H9OH—confirms the exact same fourteen unit increment throughout all adjacent members. Because all members possess the identical functional group, their chemical properties are virtually indistinguishable. However, as molecular mass increases systematically along the series, inter-molecular van der Waals attractions strengthen, producing a smooth, predictable gradation in physical properties such as rising melting points, boiling points, densities, and changing solubilities in water across the entire family of hydrocarbons.

IUPAC Carbon Nomenclature

Systematic IUPAC Framework: Root Words, Prefixes, and Suffix Modifiers

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

To bring global uniformity to the millions of synthesized organic molecules, the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry established a systematic nomenclature method outlined in Table 4.4. Naming a carbon compound follows three logical sequential steps. First, count the number of carbon atoms in the longest continuous parent carbon chain to determine the foundational root word: one carbon is meth, two is eth, three is prop, four is but, five is pent, and six is hex. Second, identify whether the carbon skeleton is saturated or unsaturated: single bonds use the suffix ane, a double bond uses ene, and a triple bond uses yne. Third, identify any functional group attached to the chain. Haloalkanes are designated by a prefix: chloropropane or bromoethane. For oxygen-containing functional groups, the terminal letter 'e' of the alkane name is deleted before appending the appropriate functional suffix. For alcohols, add the suffix 'ol' to yield propanol; for aldehydes, add 'al' to yield propanal; for ketones, add 'one' to yield propanone; and for carboxylic acids, add 'oic acid' to yield propanoic acid. This rigorous systematic framework allows any chemist worldwide to deduce the precise molecular architecture directly from its IUPAC chemical name.

Hydrocarbon Combustion Chemistry

Exothermic Oxidation, Sooty Flames, Flame Thermodynamics, and Fossil Origins

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

All carbon compounds burn in atmospheric oxygen to undergo exothermic combustion, liberating substantial thermal and radiant light energy along with carbon dioxide and water vapour. For example, burning methane releases heat and light: CH4 plus two O2 yields CO2 plus two H2O plus heat. In Activities 4.3 and 4.4, observing different substances reveals critical flame characteristics. Saturated hydrocarbons generally burn with a clean, non-sooty blue flame because their high hydrogen-to-carbon ratio allows complete combustion in adequate air. However, if air vents are partially blocked—as seen in household gas stoves—incomplete combustion occurs, generating a yellow, sooty flame that deposits black unburnt carbon soot on cooking pots and utensils. In contrast, unsaturated hydrocarbons like alkenes and alkynes burn inherently with a smoky yellow flame because their higher carbon percentage cannot undergo complete oxidation in normal air. But why do some substances produce a flame while others merely glow? As explained in 'More to Know!', flames are produced exclusively when gaseous substances burn. Wood or candles contain volatile liquids or solids that vaporise upon heating into combustible gases; once volatile components escape, red-hot charcoal in an angithi glows without a flame, emitting pure radiant infrared heat steadily into the surroundings.

Alcohol Oxidation Reactions

Controlled Chemical Oxidation: Potassium Permanganate and Dichromate Reagents

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

While combustion represents uncontrolled, destructive oxidation converting carbon skeletons entirely into carbon dioxide and water, organic chemistry also harnesses controlled chemical oxidation to synthesize valuable functional derivatives. Primary alcohols can be oxidized cleanly into carboxylic acids without destroying the carbon skeleton by using potent chemical oxidising agents. An oxidising agent is defined as a substance that is capable of adding oxygen to, or removing hydrogen from, another chemical compound. The two textbook industrial reagents used universally for this transformation are alkaline potassium permanganate, KMnO4, or acidified potassium dichromate, K2Cr2O7. When ethanol, CH3CH2OH, is warmed gently in a laboratory water bath with alkaline potassium permanganate, the distinctive deep purple colour of permanganate gradually discharges as it donates nascent oxygen atoms to the alcohol molecule. Upon continued heating and subsequent acidification, ethanol is cleanly oxidized into ethanoic acid: CH3CH2OH plus two nascent oxygens yields CH3COOH plus H2O. This controlled transformation is fundamental to synthetic organic chemistry: an alcohol containing a single oxygen atom is elevated to a carboxylic acid containing two oxygen atoms. Crucially, the carbon chain length remains completely conserved throughout the entire chemical oxidation reaction, with two-carbon ethanol yielding two-carbon ethanoic acid cleanly and quantitatively.

Addition Substitution Reactions

Catalytic Hydrogenation of Vegetable Oils and Photochemical Chlorination

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Carbon compounds undergo two hallmark reaction mechanisms that distinguish saturated hydrocarbons from unsaturated hydrocarbons: addition reactions and substitution reactions. Addition reactions are the exclusive domain of unsaturated hydrocarbons. Because alkenes and alkynes possess reactive multiple bonds, they can add reagents across double or triple bonds to yield saturated products without eliminating any atoms. A monumental industrial application is catalytic hydrogenation: unsaturated vegetable oils containing long, healthy polyunsaturated liquid hydrocarbon chains are reacted with hydrogen gas at elevated temperature in the presence of a finely divided metallic nickel or palladium catalyst. The double bonds open up completely, adding hydrogen atoms across adjacent carbons to yield solid, saturated fats like vanaspati ghee or commercial margarine spread. Conversely, saturated hydrocarbons, having only single bonds, cannot undergo addition reactions under any normal conditions. Instead, alkanes undergo substitution reactions in the presence of ultraviolet sunlight. In diffuse sunlight, highly reactive chlorine radicals replace hydrogen atoms on methane one by one: CH4 plus Cl2 yields chloromethane, CH3Cl, plus HCl. As chlorination proceeds, successive substitutions yield dichloromethane, chloroform, and finally carbon tetrachloride. Saturated alkanes are normally unreactive, but photochemical substitution enables their chemical functionalisation and derivatisation cleanly in modern industrial chemical synthesis.

Ethanol Chemical Properties

Solvent Versatility, Physiological Hazards, Sodium Reaction, and Acid Dehydration

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Ethanol, formula C2H5OH, is the second member of the homologous alcohol series and a compound of immense industrial and physiological importance. At room temperature, pure ethanol is a volatile, colourless liquid with a distinct pleasant odour and a boiling point of 351 Kelvin. Ethanol is completely miscible with water in all proportions and is a superb industrial solvent, utilised extensively in medicines such as tincture of iodine, cough syrups, and invigorating health tonics. However, ethanol ingestion causes grave physiological harm: consuming dilute alcohol depresses the central nervous system, impairing motor coordination, judgment, and emotional restraint. Consuming pure absolute alcohol can be fatal. Furthermore, ingesting toxic methanol causes blindness and rapid death because methanol is metabolised in the liver into methanal, which reacts rapidly with cellular protoplasm, coagulating it like cooked egg albumen. To prevent the misuse of industrial alcohol, it is denatured by adding poisonous methanol and blue dyes. Chemically, ethanol undergoes two classic reactions. First, reacting ethanol with metallic sodium liberates bubbles of flammable hydrogen gas: two Na plus two CH3CH2OH yields sodium ethoxide plus H2. Second, heating ethanol at 443 Kelvin with excess concentrated sulphuric acid dehydrates ethanol into ethene and water cleanly and reliably.

Ethanoic Acid Chemistry

Glacial Acetic Acid, Fruity Esterification, Saponification, and Carbonate Fizz

NCERT Theory & Laboratory
👨‍🏫
Classroom Lecture Audio Pure Indian Male Teacher Voice • 0.90x Rate

Ethanoic acid, commonly called acetic acid, formula CH3COOH, belongs to the carboxylic acid family and is a foundational commercial chemical. A five to eight percent aqueous solution of ethanoic acid is universally known as vinegar, used widely as a culinary preservative in pickles. Pure anhydrous ethanoic acid possesses a melting point of 290 Kelvin; consequently, during cold winter climates in northern India, it readily freezes into an ice-like crystalline solid, earning the historic name glacial acetic acid. Ethanoic acid is a weak organic acid that ionises only partially in water, distinguishing it from strongly dissociated mineral acids like hydrochloric acid. In Activities 4.7 and 4.8, warming ethanol and glacial ethanoic acid together in a water bath with a few drops of concentrated sulphuric acid produces a delightfully sweet, fruity fragrance. This reaction is esterification: ethanoic acid reacts with ethanol to yield ethyl ethanoate ester and water. When esters are heated with sodium hydroxide base, they hydrolyse back into alcohol and sodium carboxylate soap, a reaction termed saponification. Furthermore, reacting ethanoic acid with sodium carbonate or hydrogencarbonate produces vigorous effervescence of carbon dioxide gas, providing a definitive laboratory test to distinguish carboxylic acids from neutral alcohols in analytical chemistry.

Reactions of Ethanoic Acid: Esterification & Carbonate Fizz

Ethanoic Acid + Ethanol ⎯conc. H2SO4⎯→ Ethyl Ethanoate (Fruity Ester) + H2O
Acetic Acid + Ethanol = Sweet Fruity Aroma 🍎

Esters are sweet-smelling substances used in perfumes and artificial fruit flavourings.

2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + H2O + CO2 ↑ (Brisk Effervescence)

Carbon dioxide gas is liberated with brisk bubbling, turning lime water milky. (Alcohols do not react!)
